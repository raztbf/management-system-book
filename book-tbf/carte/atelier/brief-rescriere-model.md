## 3. Style contract — this is a Malcolm Gladwell pastiche

The model is *The Tipping Point*. Seven rules:

1. **Cold open on a scene.** Named people, a date, a place, a physical detail. Gladwell's first line is *"For Hush Puppies — the classic American brushed-suede shoes with the lightweight crepe sole — the Tipping Point came somewhere between late 1994 and early 1995."* No throat-clearing.
2. **The scene contains a puzzle.** Something does not add up. The reader must want the answer before the theory arrives.
3. **Theory flows out of the story, never before it.** Never state a concept and then illustrate it. Always show the reality, then name it.
4. **Numbered sections** (`### 1`, `### 2`, `### 3`) — each is one step of the argument.
5. **Return to earlier material later**, with new meaning.
6. **The turn is short.** After 600 words of narrative, the sentence that changes everything is eight words long.
7. **First person is rare and heavy.** The author's "I" should appear only where it costs him something. Overused, it deflates.

**Voice notes:** direct, unhedged, occasionally blunt. Uses **bold pull-quotes** on their own line for the load-bearing sentences. **Avoid:** listicles, headers inside sections, "Firstly/Secondly", rhetorical questions in clusters, em-dash overuse, the words "journey", "unlock", "leverage" as a verb, "game-changer".

### 1

On 25 May 1961, John F. Kennedy stood in front of a joint session of Congress and said that the United States should commit itself, before the decade was out, to landing a man on the moon and returning him safely to the earth.

It is probably the most famous vision statement ever delivered, and it has everything the textbooks say a vision should have. A specific outcome. A hard deadline. Enormous emotional force. A scale that made grown engineers cry.

It also worked. Eight years and two months later, Neil Armstrong stepped off a ladder onto the surface of another world, and roughly a fifth of everyone alive watched him do it.

Now look at what it took.

At its peak, Apollo employed something like four hundred and ten thousand people across twenty thousand companies and universities. In 1966 — three years before anyone landed — NASA was consuming 4.4 percent of the entire federal budget of the United States. The total bill came to around $25.8 billion in the money of the day, somewhere north of $300 billion now.

Those are the numbers people quote. They are not the interesting part.

The interesting part is what it did to the people inside it. Apollo ran on six-day weeks that became seven-day weeks, on engineers who slept at their desks through the final year, on marriages that did not survive the decade, on a workforce that understood it was racing a deadline set by a dead president and a rival superpower. It ran on the belief — accurate, as it happens — that the schedule mattered more than anybody's life outside it.

And on 27 January 1967, during a routine ground test, a fire tore through the sealed cabin of Apollo 1 and killed Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee in under half a minute.

The programme did not stop. It absorbed the deaths, rebuilt the capsule, and made the deadline.

I want to be precise about what I am and am not saying, because this is a story I love and I do not want to be cheap with it. Apollo is one of the most extraordinary things our species has ever done. The people who did it were magnificent, and the ones still alive have earned every bit of the pride they carry. Nothing here is an argument that it should not have been attempted.

But it is worth being honest about *what kind of thing* it was.

Apollo was organized around a single result, on a fixed date, and everything else — cost, sustainability, the health of the people, the future of the organization — was subordinated to that result. It succeeded completely. Twelve human beings walked on the moon between July 1969 and December 1972.

And then nobody did, for over half a century.

### 2

The usual way to tell that second half is as a story about budgets and politics. Congress lost interest. Vietnam. The public got bored.

All true, and all beside the point, because it lets you believe that the ending was imposed from outside. It was not. The ending was built into the design from the first day.

Apollo 18, 19 and 20 were cancelled. The Saturn V production line was shut down and the tooling was scrapped. The four hundred thousand people dispersed into other industries and eventually into retirement, and the knowledge went with them — because the knowledge had never been stored in an organization. It had been stored in a *programme*, and programmes end.

There is a detail I find almost unbearable.

In 2013, engineers at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center wanted to understand the F-1 — the enormous first-stage engine, five of which lifted every Saturn V, each producing more thrust than anything built since. So they took a gas generator out of an F-1 that had been sitting in storage, and another out of an F-1 in the Smithsonian, pulled them apart, and ran a structured-light 3D scan over the components to produce drawings.

That is how the most advanced engineering organization on earth got back to its own design. It went to a museum and measured it.

But I do not want you to read that as a story about NASA's filing system, because the real cost was not paid by NASA. It was paid by everyone.

Between 1969 and 1972, six landings brought back 382 kilograms of rock, soil and dust from six sites. Fifty-odd years later, that is still — with a handful of robotic additions — essentially the entire physical ground truth humanity possesses about another world. Everything we believe about the age of the solar system, about how planets form, about what happened to the early Earth, rests on 382 kilograms collected during six visits by twelve men in three years.

And it stopped, not because we had learned what there was to learn, but because the thing we had built was not capable of continuing.

Meanwhile, NASA's own Inspector General puts the cost of the Artemis campaign — the programme to go back — at around $93 billion between 2012 and 2025, with a projected cost of roughly $4.1 billion for each launch of the rocket that will take people there. We are spending tens of billions of dollars to reacquire a capability we already had and threw away.

That is the bill for owning a result instead of a cause. It arrives sixty years later, and it is enormous, and almost nobody connects it to the decision that caused it.

Now run the counterfactual, because it is the whole point of this chapter.

Suppose that in 1961 the sentence had been different. Suppose it had been: *build a machine that can put people on the moon and bring them home, four times a year, indefinitely.*

That is a far worse speech. It has no finish line. It cannot be photographed. It would not have beaten the Soviet Union to anything, which means the political appetite that funded Apollo might never have appeared, and the whole thing might have died in committee. I am not pretending the choice was obvious or free.

But follow it anyway. That goal forces a completely different set of questions from the first week. Not *how do we get there once* but *what does the transport cost per kilogram, and how do we bring it down.* Not *how do we survive three days on the surface* but *how do people live there.* Not *how do we build a rocket* but *how do we build a production line for rockets.* It would have taken longer. The first landing might have been in 1975 instead of 1969. There would have been far less for anybody's ego to feed on, national or personal.

And by 1990 there would have been a permanent presence on the moon. By now there would be a base, decades of continuous data, a generation of human beings who had grown up with people living off Earth as an ordinary fact, direct answers to the questions we are currently spending $93 billion to ask again, and a cost per kilogram to orbit that had been falling for fifty years instead of standing still for forty.

We chose a moment. We got the moment, gloriously. And we have been paying for that choice ever since.

Which brings me to the sentence this entire book is built on, and I want to state it plainly before I have earned it, so you can hold it while I do:

> **The things that change the world do not happen once.**

Karl Benz built a working automobile in 1886. It changed almost nothing for almost anybody. For nearly thirty years the car was a toy for people who already had everything, and the life of an ordinary person was untouched by it.

What changed the world was not the invention. It was the moving assembly line — the machine that made cars ordinary, which took the time to assemble one from around twelve hours to around ninety minutes and drove the price down until a factory worker could buy the thing he was building.

Nobody built one car, confirmed that it worked, and went to sleep satisfied that humanity had been improved. The improvement *was* the repetition. It always is.

### 3

I am opening a business book with a rocket because almost every company I have ever worked with is built on the Apollo model, and almost none of them know it.

The pattern is exact. Set a result. Attach a date. Subordinate everything to it. Find the people who will not stop, and let them not stop. Absorb the damage — the exhaustion, the departures, the shortcuts, the maintenance nobody had time for. Cross the line. Get photographed.

And then discover, six months later, that you cannot do it again, because what you built was a campaign and not a capability.

The record quarter that nobody can explain. The launch that worked because four people did not go home for eleven weeks. The client saved by the founder personally, at midnight, for the ninth time. The best year in the company's history, followed by a year that looks like a collapse and gets blamed on the market.

Every one of those is Apollo at a smaller scale. A magnificent result, produced by heroic effort, whose cause the organization does not own — and therefore cannot repeat without paying the same price again, if it can repeat it at all.

Founders are usually proud of exactly the wrong part. They will tell you about the crossing in enormous detail. They cannot tell you anything about the boat.

And a business built this way carries a second problem, which is the one that eventually kills it: nobody can do that twice. Not the founder, not the team, not the marriages. Apollo had the good sense to end. Your company is expected to do it again next quarter, and the quarter after that, forever.

So what does the alternative actually look like?

SpaceX did not set out to reach orbit. Reaching orbit had been done thousands of times; by the 2000s it was a solved problem. What SpaceX set out to do was make reaching orbit *repeatable and cheap*, which in practice meant not throwing the rocket away.

That goal is, emotionally, almost worthless. Nobody weeps at the idea of a reusable first stage. You cannot make a Kennedy speech out of amortization. And for years it looked ridiculous — boosters tipping over on barges, exploding on landing, missing the deck entirely. The failures were mocked, because the thing being attempted did not look like a victory. It looked like an accounting exercise with fireworks.

In December 2015, a Falcon 9 first stage landed upright. Then it happened again. Then it became routine, then boring, then invisible.

In 2020, SpaceX flew 25 orbital launches. In 2021, 31. In 2022, 61. In 2023, 96. In 2024, 134 — more than half of all orbital launches on the planet, by anybody. In 2025, 165. A single booster has flown more than twenty times.

Now I have to say something I would rather not have to say.

I am not an admirer of Elon Musk. A good deal of how he operates, and a good deal of what he says, sits somewhere I am not willing to stand, and if this book were about character I would not be using him as an example of anything.

But this book is not about character, and I am not going to pretend that a man whose values I do not share failed to build the more useful thing. What SpaceX built is more correct, and more genuinely in the service of humanity, than a single victory constructed to feed an ego — and that is true whether the ego is one man's or a superpower's. Apollo was, among other things, a demonstration to the Soviet Union. It was magnificent and it was also that.

You are allowed to hold both of those judgements at the same time. I would go further: you have to. The alternative is to let your opinion of a person decide what you are permitted to learn, which is a very expensive way to protect your feelings.

Ask yourself which of those two organizations is now more capable of putting a permanent base on the moon. It is not close, and it is not a compliment. It is arithmetic.

The difference is not talent, and it is not effort — Apollo had more of both, by a wide margin. It is not even money. It is that Apollo owned a magnificent *result* and SpaceX owns a boring *cause*.

A result is what happened. A cause is what makes it happen again.

Most companies manage results, and it is almost impossible not to, because results are visible and countable and emotionally available. Revenue is a number you can say out loud. Profit is a number the bank cares about. New customers, new locations, market share, valuation, the size of the contract you just signed — all visible, all measurable, all available to be celebrated on a Friday.

So the company looks at the result and decides that it should be larger. A target is set. A deadline is attached. Pressure is applied. A campaign is launched, a discount is introduced, more calls are demanded, people are hired quickly, hours get longer, decisions move toward the founder, and whatever is available is thrown at the number.

And sometimes the number moves. That is the whole problem. If it never moved, nobody would do it.

But look at what happened underneath. A discount can raise revenue while destroying margin. Contracts pulled forward from next quarter make this quarter stronger and next quarter weaker. Three weeks of overtime protect the delivery and consume the energy that would have produced next month's. Training that gets postponed protects today's capacity by reducing tomorrow's. A founder who personally rescues the client saves the account and makes the company slightly more dependent on the founder than it was on Monday.

Which means we need to be much more careful about what the word *better* means, because almost everybody uses it to mean *bigger this quarter*, and that is not what it means.

> **A better business is one that can deliver the result its community actually needs — repeatedly, and at higher quality, or lower cost, or greater speed than it could before.**

Read that against Apollo. In the summer of 1969, NASA had the greatest quarter any organization has ever had. Measured on output, nothing in human history compares. And it was not, on that morning, a better organization than it had been in 1968 — because the capacity to do it again was not being built, and within three years it would be gone. The output was extraordinary. The underlying capability was already being spent.

Which gives us the sentence I need you to carry through the rest of this book:

> **A company can have its best quarter and become a worse business.**

That is not a paradox and it is not a warning against ambition. It is the ordinary condition of most growing companies, and it is invisible from the inside, precisely because the one instrument everybody is watching — the result — moved the right way.

The Apollo model is very good at producing best quarters. On its own it is not capable of producing anything else. And every one of those quarters gets celebrated on the way past.

So the question this book exists to answer is not *how do we get a bigger number*. It is:

> **What would have to exist inside this company for the result we are proud of to become the result we produce normally?**

That question has a structure. It is the same structure whether you are building rockets, selling software, running four restaurants, or trying to build a marriage that does not need rescuing every eighteen months. It has five stations and two branches, it runs continuously, and it is what the rest of this book is about.
