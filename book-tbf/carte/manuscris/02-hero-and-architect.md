# 2. The Hero and the Architect

### Amundsen, Scott, and the Economics of Rescue

---

### 1

In October 1911, two expeditions set out for the same piece of ice.

They were, on paper, evenly matched. Both were led by experienced men with reputations to defend. Both had money, ships, sponsors, and the attention of their nations. Both were heading for a point on the map that no human being had ever stood on. Within fourteen months, one of the leaders would be a national hero, and the other would be dead in a tent eleven miles from a supply depot, alongside the frozen bodies of four of his men.

The difference between them was not courage. Both had a great deal of it, and the one who died had more opportunities to demonstrate it. The difference was that one of them had spent years building a machine, and the other had spent years preparing to be magnificent.

Roald Amundsen had been rehearsing for the South Pole for most of his adult life without knowing that was what he was doing. As a young man he had lived for two winters with the Netsilik Inuit in the Canadian Arctic, and instead of pitying them he studied them. He learned how they drove dogs — not as draught animals to be pushed, but as a system with its own logic. He learned why they wore loose animal skins rather than tight woven wool, which is that wool absorbs sweat, and sweat freezes, and frozen sweat against skin will kill you at a temperature you would otherwise survive. He learned to travel slowly enough that you never sweat in the first place.

He also, at various points, rode a bicycle from Norway to Spain, ate raw dolphin meat to see whether he could, and apprenticed himself on an Arctic sealing ship. None of this was heroic. It looked, to his contemporaries, faintly eccentric. All of it was rehearsal.

By the time he went south, the machine was already built.

Consider the depots. Amundsen's team laid three tons of supplies into caches at 80, 81, and 82 degrees south — three tons, for five men who would eventually make the polar journey. And because a supply depot you cannot find is exactly as useful as a supply depot that does not exist, he did not simply mark each one with a flag. He planted lines of flags running east and west of every cache, at measured intervals, so that a man staggering through a whiteout, uncertain of his latitude, could not miss it. He was building tolerance for the day things went wrong, because he assumed things would go wrong.

Then came the part that looks, to modern eyes, almost boring.

Amundsen and his men marched between fifteen and twenty miles a day. Every day. On days when the weather was beautiful and the dogs were fresh and they could easily have covered forty, they stopped at twenty and made camp. On days when the weather was appalling and every instinct in the body said stay in the tent, they went out and did their twenty.

Robert Falcon Scott did the opposite, and he did it for reasons that sound entirely sensible. When conditions were good, his men pushed hard and covered enormous distances. When conditions were bad, they rested. This is what most people do. It is what most companies do. It feels like intelligence — responding to circumstance, seizing the moment, conserving energy for when it counts.

It also means that your output is a function of the weather.

Amundsen reached the South Pole on 14 December 1911. Five men set out and five men came back. The whole journey took ninety-nine days, ten fewer than planned. He arrived thirty-four days ahead of Scott, planted a flag, left a tent with a letter inside it, and went home.

Scott arrived on 17 January 1912 to find the tent already there. On the return journey his team was destroyed by exactly the kind of ordinary bad weather that Amundsen had budgeted for. They died in March, pinned in a tent by a blizzard, eleven miles from a depot they could not reach.

---

### 2

Now here is the part that should bother you, and that I did not understand for the first twenty years of my working life.

Scott's story is the one that became a legend.

His last diary entry — *For God's sake look after our people* — was carved into the British imagination for a century. Statues went up. Schoolchildren learned his name. A generation of officers was taught that he represented something about how a man should die. Amundsen, who did the same journey faster, safer, and repeatably, was quietly resented in Britain as a man who had won on a technicality, because he used dogs and did not suffer enough.

Read that again. *He did not suffer enough.*

Amundsen went on to become the first person to reach both poles and the first to traverse the Northwest Passage. Scott went once and did not come back. And for most of the twentieth century, if you had asked an educated European to name a great polar explorer, you would have got Scott.

This is not an accident of British nationalism. It is a completely general property of how human beings assign credit, and it operates inside your company right now.

Scott gave the world a tragedy with a hero inside it. There was a crisis, there was suffering, there was a moment of decision, there were last words. It has a shape. You can film it.

Amundsen gave the world a logistics operation that worked. There is no scene. Nothing went badly enough to be memorable. The most dramatic thing that happened to Roald Amundsen in Antarctica is that he arrived somewhere and then arrived back.

> **The fire creates a scene. Prevention creates an absence.**

And there has never in the history of the human race been a statue erected to an absence.

Everything in this chapter follows from that single asymmetry. Because if rescue is visible and prevention is invisible, then any organization that rewards what it can see will systematically reward the wrong thing — and will do it sincerely, enthusiastically, and with the best of intentions, for years.

---

### 3

I need to stop describing this from a distance, because I did not learn it from a distance.

For a large part of my life, I wanted to be the hero.

I liked being the person who arrived when the situation looked impossible. I liked seeing the answer nobody else could see, making the call that changed the outcome, and hearing someone say *I cannot believe you pulled that off.* Over more than twenty years working on management and growth with more than three hundred companies, I got very good at it. It gave me recognition, influence, and a strong sense that I mattered.

It also exhausted me, made my life unpredictable, and taught me to confuse being needed with being valuable.

But that is the comfortable version of the confession, and I have told it that way before. Here is the version that is actually true, and that took me much longer to be able to say out loud.

Heroism does not distinguish between the person who resolved the crisis and the person who caused it. Very often they are the same person.

For years, that person was me. I manufactured my own tragedies so that I could rescue them.

Look at what I did with my own body. I would allow myself to gain weight — steadily, deliberately in the sense that I knew exactly what I was doing and did it anyway — up to a number of kilograms I genuinely hated. And then I would rescue the situation. Twenty kilos down. A visible, dramatic, photographable transformation. And people would tell me they could not believe it, and ask how I did it, and I would feel the specific warmth of having achieved something.

Nobody ever congratulated me for a normal, stable weight. There is no applause available for a Tuesday on which nothing happened. So I kept building the conditions that made applause possible.

I did the same thing in my marriage. Instead of building the machine that would produce a good relationship continuously — the ordinary, repeatable, unphotographable practices that actually make two people feel close — I let things deteriorate. Not to the point of collapse, but close enough to it that the situation required saving. And then I would arrive. A conversation, an effort, a change, a rescue. And there was real feeling in it, and real relief, and it worked, and it worked because it followed a crisis I had allowed to develop.

And I did it in business, systematically, for years. I introduced impossible projects. I set terrifying objectives that nobody had a method for. I changed direction when things had just started to become stable. I built organizations that ran on chaos, and I did it — I want to be precise about this, because the imprecise version lets me off the hook — I did it because in a chaotic system my particular abilities were extremely valuable, and in an orderly one they were not.

If there is no fire, I am not important.

Therefore I need a fire.

Most founders do not consciously decide to damage their companies. I did not. The mechanism is much quieter than that. You interrupt a process that works because you have had a better idea. You change the rules before people have finished learning the old ones. You insert an untested tactic straight into operations because you feel strongly about it. You keep the important knowledge in your own head, not out of malice but because writing it down is slower than acting on it. You set a target above what the current system can produce, and then you personally close the gap.

The business struggles. People look toward you. You rescue the result. You are valuable again.

Nobody is lying. Everybody's intentions are good. And the loop is airtight, because every rescue produces evidence that the rescuer was necessary.

---

### 4

Now, there is a version of this argument that I want to refuse before we go further, because it is lazy and it is wrong.

It is not true that exceptional people are the problem. It is not true that heroism is a character defect. And it is certainly not true that the answer is to become a smaller, more cautious, more procedural person.

The confusion happens because three completely different things share one name. Separating them is the most useful thing in this chapter.

**Heroic capacity** is an ability. Some people can function well in chaos. They can make decisions on incomplete information. They stay calm under pressure — or at least stay functional, which is more common and just as useful. They see solutions other people do not see. They will walk into unmapped territory without needing to be pushed. Occasionally they produce extraordinary results in genuinely exceptional situations.

This is a real and rare capability, and it is enormously valuable. Nothing in this book is an argument against it.

**The heroic role** is something else entirely. It is what happens when a company requires that capability recurrently in order to produce an *ordinary* result.

Notice the word. Ordinary. The question is no longer whether this person can perform in chaos. The question is that without this person, we do not know how to function on a normal Wednesday.

That is not a statement about the person. It is a statement about the company. Which gives us the definition I use:

> **An organizational hero is the person whose exceptional intervention repeatedly compensates for a capability the system has not yet built.**

Every hero in your company is standing in front of a hole. The hero is not the solution to the hole. The hero is the shape of the hole, and as long as they keep standing there, nobody can see how big it is.

**Heroic syndrome** is the third thing, and it is the identity trap. It happens when a person attaches their value, their security, their status, or their sense of significance to the fact that the system needs them. When indispensability stops being a circumstance and becomes a self.

That is the one that got me.

And here is why the distinction matters practically, rather than just philosophically. Exceptional talent does not automatically make someone a hero. Take a genuinely brilliant person — the best salesperson you have ever seen, the operator who can fix anything — and watch what they do with the brilliance.

If they make their method visible. If they teach it. If they build processes out of it. If they create successors. If they distribute the decisions they used to make alone. If they actively work to eliminate the need for their own intervention. If they leave the system stronger than their own presence in it —

That is not a hero. That is an architect with extraordinary heroic capacity, which is the most valuable person a company can contain.

---

### 5

There is also a darker version, and it is worth naming because most founders have met one and did not have a word for it.

The toxic hero is someone who occupies the heroic role without possessing the heroic capacity. They cannot actually do the extraordinary thing. What they can do is build and maintain the conditions that make them appear necessary.

They generate chaos and then manage it. They keep information in their heads. They do not document, and they have a good reason not to document every single time you ask. They design processes that are harder to follow than they need to be. And above all, they will obstruct, by any available means, the development of anyone who could replace them.

Terry Childs was the network engineer responsible for FiberWAN, the fibre-optic network carrying the City of San Francisco's payroll, email, jail records, and law enforcement documentation. He had essentially built it. He worked absurd hours. He answered his phone at three in the morning. Colleagues who disliked him agreed that he cared about that network the way people care about their children.

He also, over several years, arranged things so that he was the only person on earth who could administer it. He configured routers so their settings existed only in volatile memory, where a successor could not find them. He would not document. He would not share credentials.

And when his managers asked for the administrative passwords, his refusal came wrapped in a justification that was almost impossible to argue with: they were not qualified to have them. He was, he explained, following industry best practice on credential protection. He was protecting the city from its own incompetence.

Look carefully at the shape of that argument, because it is the signature of the type. It is not a lie. His managers probably *were* less technically competent than he was. The justification is usually true. It just happens to also be the mechanism by which the hero becomes irremovable.

In July 2008, during a disciplinary meeting, the city demanded the passwords. Childs gave them false ones. He was arrested. And for twelve days, the City of San Francisco — its payroll, its police records, its email — ran on a network that nobody had administrative control over. It kept working, which is the most chilling detail in the whole story. It kept working the way a car with no steering wheel keeps working, right up until it needs to turn.

The standoff ended in a scene that would be funny if it were not so revealing. The mayor of San Francisco went personally to the county jail, sat in a room alone with Childs, and Childs handed him the passwords. Not to the IT department. Not to his manager. To the mayor.

That is the entire psychology in one image. What he wanted was not money and it was not sabotage. What he wanted was for the system to say out loud that it could not function without him — and for twelve days, at the cost of his career and four years in prison, he made it say so.

He was convicted of felony network tampering and sentenced to four years.

And now the uncomfortable reading, because the comfortable one lets every executive off the hook. The comfortable reading is that Terry Childs was a criminal and a narcissist, and your company does not have one.

The uncomfortable reading is that nobody at the City of San Francisco ever woke up and decided to hand one employee unilateral control of the municipal network. It happened one small, reasonable decision at a time. He was the fastest person to fix it, so he fixed it. Documentation was slower than doing, and the work was urgent, so documentation slipped. He was territorial about credentials, and challenging him was exhausting, and things were working, so nobody challenged him.

The city did not fail to notice a monster. The city failed to build an architecture, and a monster grew in the space where the architecture should have been.

> **Indispensability is not proof of value. It is evidence of unfinished architecture.**

---

### 6

Which raises the question of *where* heroes come from, and the answer is more specific than "certain kinds of people."

Heroes become most powerful in systems where cause and effect are hard to see.

If a process is clear, the standard is visible, the data is trusted, and everyone knows who decides what, then the organization can diagnose most deviations without magic. The problem may still be extremely difficult. But it becomes *discussable*. Three people can look at the same evidence and argue about it productively.

In a foggy system, none of that is true. The rules change. The data is incomplete or contested. Roles overlap. Half the relevant history lives in private conversations that were never written down. And in that environment, the person who can *feel* what is happening becomes indispensable — not because of favouritism, but because genuinely nobody else can reconstruct the situation.

> **Chaos creates the demand for intuition. Ambiguity creates the demand for central authority. Private knowledge creates the demand for the person who owns it.**

And the leader in that system receives continuous, sincere, apparently objective proof that nobody else can do what they do — without ever noticing that the system was arranged so that nobody else could.

In a founder-led company this is particularly dangerous, because the same person usually has the authority to change both the rules and the explanation of the rules. Which means the fog is renewable.

Now, the version of this that surprises people is that a team of heroes can produce exactly the same fog, entirely by accident, with nothing but dedication.

The Nut Island sewage treatment plant sat on a five-acre spit of land in Boston Harbor, and for twenty-five years it was operated by one of the most committed teams anyone had ever seen. They fixed equipment nobody would authorize replacing, using parts they scrounged. Some of them worked nights and weekends without being asked. Absenteeism was near zero. Their safety record over a quarter of a century was so good that visitors from industry did not believe it.

They were heroes in the purest sense — keeping a decaying plant running on personal effort and pride, long past the point at which it should have stopped running.

And during those same twenty-five years, they were one of the largest sources of pollution in Boston Harbor.

Paul Levy, who later ran the agency and wrote the story up for the *Harvard Business Review*, traced the sequence, and every step of it will be familiar.

Management was distracted, and the team was competent, so management left them alone. This felt to everyone involved like a compliment. It was the beginning of the disease. The team, isolated, developed enormous internal cohesion — they were a family, they covered for each other, they were proud of coping without help. They began asking for things: equipment, budget, attention. Management, reassured by an absence of alarming data, did not respond. The team learned that asking was pointless.

Then the fourth step, which is the fatal one. The team decided that if nobody was going to help, they would solve everything themselves. They stopped reporting problems. Not out of malice — out of pride, and out of a settled conviction that the outsiders were useless anyway.

And finally, the team's definition of success drifted away from the organization's. At Nut Island, the crew came to believe their job was to keep the plant running and keep the men safe. By that definition, they were magnificent. By the actual mission — remove pollutants before the water enters the harbour — they were failing catastrophically, and had been for years, and were the last people on earth capable of seeing it.

They routinely discharged raw and partially treated sewage into the harbour. They knew. They did not report it, because reporting it would have meant admitting to outsiders that the family could not cope.

At no point was anyone lazy, dishonest, or unmotivated. They were the most heroic team in the agency, and their heroism was the precise mechanism by which the truth stopped reaching anybody who could act on it.

> **A team that never asks for help is not a strong team. It is a team that has stopped telling you the truth — and the heroism is what made the silence feel virtuous.**

### 7

The argument so far has been about what heroism does to organizations. Now I want you to feel what it does to the people who choose the other path.

There is a plastic tube called a central line that goes into a large vein near the heart. In an intensive care unit it is how patients receive drugs, fluids, and nutrition, and it gets infected, and when the infection goes bad the patient can die. For decades this was treated as the background cost of doing business in a place full of very sick people.

In the late 1990s a critical care physician at Johns Hopkins named Peter Pronovost looked at what actually caused the infections. It turned out to be five things, and every doctor in the world already knew all five: wash your hands, clean the skin with chlorhexidine, drape the patient fully, wear mask and gown and gloves, put a sterile dressing on the site.

There was nothing left to discover. Every step was in the textbook.

So he wrote the five steps on a piece of paper and asked nurses to watch the doctors do them. In more than a third of cases, doctors skipped at least one. Not because they were bad doctors — because they were doing a hundred things at once, under pressure, from memory, and memory is not a system.

Then came the part that mattered more than the list. Pronovost got the administration to authorize nurses to stop a doctor who was skipping a step. Anyone who has ever set foot in a hospital understands what a violent act of architecture that is. It moved the authority to halt a procedure out of the hierarchy and into the process.

The ten-day infection rate in that unit went from eleven percent to zero.

In 2003 the state of Michigan ran it across more than a hundred ICUs. Within three months, central line infections across the state had fallen sixty-six percent. Eighteen months later they were still down. The published estimate is more than fifteen hundred lives and a hundred and seventy-five million dollars.

Fifteen hundred people went home to their families because of a piece of paper, and not one of them knows it. There is no survivor's story. Each of them had an ordinary hospital stay and will spend the rest of their life believing that nothing in particular happened to them.

Compare that with the heroic version. A brilliant intensivist notices a patient crashing at three in the morning, calls it early, opens the right lines, pulls them back from the edge. The family weeps in the corridor. Someone writes to the hospital. The doctor's reputation grows.

That doctor saved one person. The piece of paper saved fifteen hundred. And the doctor is the one who gets thanked.

> **The emotional economy of heroism is unfair to architecture, and it will stay unfair unless you deliberately intervene.**

Which produces the single most important management instruction in this chapter: companies become what they celebrate. If you celebrate only spectacular rescue and heroic rule-breaking, your people will learn to produce exceptional scenes. If you recognize prevention, consistency, transfer, and quiet reliability, they will learn to build.

And you do not have to go to a hospital to find this. It is happening in your company this quarter.

Somebody has saved an account that was about to leave. They flew out, they sat with the client, they stayed late, they fixed it. Everybody in the building knows their name by Friday, and they deserve to.

Meanwhile somebody else spent three quiet weeks changing how work is handed over between sales and delivery, because they had noticed that a particular kind of promise was being made in one room and discovered in another. The consequence of that change is that a certain kind of account will not start leaving in the first place. Not this year, and not for the next ten.

The first person is thanked in the all-hands. The second person is not mentioned, and it is not because anybody is being unfair. There is genuinely nothing to mention. Nothing happened. That is the entire point of what they built, and it is also precisely why nobody can see it.

So ask yourself honestly which of those two people is worth more to this company over five years.

Then ask a harder one. Which of them is more likely to still be here in five years — and what is every single person in that room being taught, week after week, about how you become somebody who matters?

### 8

So what would it actually look like if a whole society decided to stop requiring people to be exceptional?

We have the answer, because several countries have run the experiment.

In 1997 the Swedish parliament voted for something that sounded, at the time, like political theatre. It was called Vision Zero, and it said that nobody should be killed or seriously injured on Swedish roads. Not fewer. Zero. Every traffic engineer in the world said this was impossible, and most of them said it out loud.

But the number was never the interesting part. The interesting part was the sentence underneath, which quietly relocated the blame for every road death in Sweden.

The traditional model of road safety, everywhere, is a hero model. It says: the road is dangerous, and it is your job to be excellent. Pay attention. Don't drink. Don't speed. Don't get tired. Don't look at your phone. If you are exceptional every single time you get behind the wheel, you will probably survive. If you die, it is because you were not exceptional enough.

Vision Zero said the opposite. It said that human beings make mistakes, that this is a fixed and non-negotiable property of human beings, and that a system requiring people not to make mistakes is a badly designed system. In the words of one of its architects: the accident is not the major problem. The problem is that the accident kills you.

So Sweden stopped trying to build better drivers and started building roads that absorb ordinary human error.

They installed thousands of kilometres of what are called 2+1 roads — a cable barrier down the middle of an ordinary two-lane highway, with the passing lane alternating. It looks like nothing. It is ugly. It cost a fraction of building a motorway. It also made the head-on collision that was killing people physically impossible. They replaced intersections with roundabouts, because a roundabout removes the ninety-degree impact that kills. They lowered urban limits to thirty kilometres an hour — not because thirty is a nicer number, but because the survival curve for a struck pedestrian falls off a cliff somewhere above it.

Their standard for a piece of road stopped being *did the driver behave correctly* and became *what happens on this road when a driver behaves incorrectly*.

In 1997 Sweden had 541 road deaths, about seven per hundred thousand people. Today it is around two hundred — under three per hundred thousand — with substantially more traffic. It is one of the lowest rates on earth.

There are no heroes in that story. Nobody in Sweden is celebrated for having driven especially well. There is only a very large number of crashes that people walked away from, and a much larger number that never happened because a cable was in the way.

Iceland ran a version of the same experiment on its children.

In 1998, Iceland had the worst teenage drinking problem in Europe. Forty-two percent of fifteen- and sixteen-year-olds had been drunk in the previous thirty days. Twenty-three percent smoked daily. On weekend nights the centre of Reykjavík filled with drunk children.

The standard response to this, everywhere on earth, is heroic. You find a charismatic person — a recovered addict, a police officer, a dying man — and you send them into schools to tell their story. You make an advertisement with a wrecked car. You give the speech that a fifteen-year-old will remember forever.

Iceland did almost none of that. What most distinguishes what they did is that they largely stopped talking to teenagers about drugs at all.

A team of researchers surveyed essentially every teenager in the country, every year, and asked them everything. The data came back with an unromantic answer. The children who did not drink had four things in common, and none of them was a good speech. They spent time with their parents during the week. They participated in organized activity — sport, music, dance, art — at least four times a week. They were not out late at night. And they felt cared about at school.

So Iceland rebuilt the ordinary week of a teenager around those four things. The state funded sports clubs, music schools, and art programmes, and issued every child a Leisure Card — a subsidy the family could spend on whatever activity the child chose, so that participation stopped depending on what the parents could afford. They set curfews, enforced not by police but by parents walking the streets in organized shifts, which sounds absurd until you notice what it actually does: it makes the norm visible. They asked parents to sign agreements with each other — no unsupervised parties, no buying alcohol for our children, we will call you. Not a promise extracted from a child, which children ignore. An agreement among adults, which changes what is normal on a whole street. And they gave every school its own data, every year, so nobody could argue about whether it was working.

By 2016, the proportion of Icelandic fifteen- and sixteen-year-olds who had been drunk in the past month had fallen from forty-two percent to five. Daily smoking from twenty-three percent to three.

No teacher gave the speech that changed a generation. Nothing went viral. A country decided what outcome it wanted, worked out what actually caused it, and rebuilt the week so the outcome happened by default.

Which is exactly why almost nobody has copied it. It is unbearably boring. It requires municipal budgets, survey instruments, sports federations, and parents signing pieces of paper. It has no moment. It cannot be photographed.

And underneath both of those, there is something in the culture of that part of the world that most entrepreneurs would initially read as a defect.

In 1933 a Danish-Norwegian novelist named Aksel Sandemose published a book about a fictional town called Jante, and buried in it were ten rules the townspeople never spoke aloud but enforced absolutely. *You are not to think you are anything special. You are not to think you are smarter than us. You are not to think you are more important than us.* Sandemose meant it as an indictment — he was describing the suffocation of provincial life. Scandinavians read it and recognized themselves so completely that Janteloven stopped being satire and became the standard name for a real cultural operating system.

To an American or a European entrepreneur this sounds like a recipe for mediocrity. No stars. No swagger. A cultural allergy to anyone announcing their own excellence.

And yet these are among the most productive, most innovative, and most consistently happy societies that have ever existed. They produce, per capita, an absurd number of globally significant companies. They also produce almost no business celebrities that anyone outside Scandinavia can name.

Watch what it actually does inside an organization. In a Nordic meeting, the senior person's view is not the end of the discussion — it is an opening position that a twenty-six-year-old is expected to challenge. Hierarchies are flat not as a design fashion but because vertical deference is embarrassing. Decisions take longer, because consensus takes longer. And then they execute faster, because everybody in the room has already argued with them.

Then look at what Norway did with its oil.

Finding enormous quantities of oil is normally the beginning of a well-documented tragedy: a boom, a currency that destroys your other industries, spectacular political corruption, a bust. Norway's response was to build the least heroic institution on earth. The Government Pension Fund Global holds the money. It is managed at arm's length from politicians. It invests almost entirely outside Norway, so the money cannot inflate the domestic economy. And it is governed by a rule so boring that most Norwegians can recite it: the government may spend, in any year, no more than about three percent of the fund's value — roughly its expected long-run real return. The principal is not touched.

The fund now holds over two trillion dollars. And the entire mechanism exists so that no single finance minister, however brilliant, however popular, however certain that *this particular* crisis justifies an exception, can be a hero with it.

There is nobody to thank. There is a rule, applied by unremarkable people, for fifty years.

> **A society that celebrates heroes gets heroes and needs them. A society that finds heroes embarrassing has to build machines instead — and machines outlive the people who build them.**

### 9

There is one more thing to understand before we can leave this chapter, and it concerns the tool that heroes reach for most often.

The system that manufactures heroes has a shape, and once you see it you cannot stop seeing it.

> **Goal → Action → Output → Dissatisfaction → New Goal → New Action → New Output → …**

The goal states what we want to happen — the victory we intend to celebrate. Action is everything we do to close the gap between the goal and reality. Output is reality. And then dissatisfaction arrives, naturally, inevitably, and this is the part that almost nobody plans for.

Dissatisfaction arrives *even when the goal is achieved*. It arrives shortly after we have got used to the result.

The impossible client becomes a client who now expects delivery. The record quarter becomes next quarter's baseline. The successful launch becomes the product everyone assumes should just work. The applause fades, the result becomes expected, and the hero starts looking for the next fire.

We jump from output to output, from wish to wish, as though nobody had ever explained anything to us about the nature of desire. It is not an accident that Buddhist philosophy locates suffering in wanting, and that most businesses on earth run on management systems built out of wanting. Suffering is the distance between desire and reality. A goal is a machine for manufacturing that distance and then insisting on it.

And this is where the objective — the hero's favourite instrument — does its quiet damage.

Objectives whisper something to you constantly, and it is this: *the present is not good enough*. It cannot be enjoyed yet. It does not deserve celebration yet. There will be a moment, later, when things are finally sufficient — after the next car, the next house, the next round, the next partner, the next number.

Which is a strange way to build a life, when you consider the arithmetic. We spend 99.99 percent of our time on the road toward the things we want, and a vanishingly small proportion of it standing on top of them. A philosophy that locates the good part in the summit has therefore declared almost the whole of your life to be a waiting room.

> **The hero's journey is an excellent structure for a film. It is a terrible operating system for a company and an exhausting structure for a life.**

### 10

I have just spent several pages taking away the only management instrument most people have ever been handed, and it would be irresponsible to stop there.

Because the objection is completely fair. If not objectives, then what? You still have a business that is not where you want it to be. Something has to happen.

Objectives feel universal because they are the only tool anybody teaches. But an objective is not a method for producing a result. It is a method for *declaring* one — and then applying pressure until somebody closes the gap with their body.

Here is what this system does instead, and it is not more complicated.

You start with the machine: what the business currently, reliably produces. You audit that against the vision, which is the default output you have decided you want. The distance between them is not a failure and it is not an accusation. It is information.

And at exactly that moment, almost everybody does the same thing. They convert the gap into an objective, attach a date to it, and push. Because the machine does not know how to produce that number, the pressure has to be absorbed somewhere — so it is absorbed by people working longer, by the founder intervening, by shortcuts, by a process being changed before anybody has evidence that the change is an improvement.

The number sometimes moves. The machine gets worse.

What we do instead is treat the gap as a question rather than a demand. If the answer is already known — if something in the company already works and there is simply not enough of it — we multiply. If the answer is not known, and that is the honest state of most gaps, we run experiments until we find something that does work, at a scale where being wrong costs a fortnight instead of a year. And only when something has been shown to work, more than once, does it get built into the machine.

Then the result arrives. And it keeps arriving, because the thing producing it has changed.

I know this works, because it is the only reason I am not still doing what I described earlier in this chapter.

I told you that for years I would let myself gain weight and then rescue it. What I did not tell you is how that cycle finally ended, because the ending is the entire method.

Every time before, I had set an objective. A number and a date. And I hit them — that is the part people find surprising. I was not bad at achieving objectives. I was extremely good at it. I would reach the weight, and there would be photographs, and people would ask me how I had done it.

And then it came back. Every time. Because I had never touched the thing that produced the original weight.

I had produced a result. The machine that made me heavy — how I ate, when I ate, what was in the house, what I did on an ordinary Tuesday, how I slept, what happened after a bad day — was completely untouched. It sat there, unchanged, patiently waiting to produce its output again. Which it did. It always did.

What finally worked was boring, and it looked nothing like any of the previous attempts.

I stopped setting a target weight. I audited the machine instead: what, specifically, in my ordinary week produces the body I have. Then I ran experiments. Not one grand plan — a series of small tests on the things the audit had implicated. Which meals. What time. What actually gets eaten when I am tired, rather than what I intend to eat when I am fresh. Which kind of exercise I will still be doing in eight months, rather than which kind burns the most in eight weeks.

Several of those experiments failed and were abandoned, which cost me almost nothing, because a failed experiment costs a fortnight.

The ones that worked were built in. Not as an effort. As the shape the week now has.

I arrived at the result without the suffering of the objective. I also arrived without the applause — there was no dramatic transformation to photograph, because the weight came off slowly enough that most people never commented on it at all.

But the machine that exists now produces a different man, at a different weight, than the machine that existed before. And it does it whether or not I am motivated this month, which is the only test that has ever mattered.

The same is true of your company, and the distinction is worth keeping somewhere you can see it.

> **An objective asks reality to change. An experiment asks reality a question. Only one of them can give you an answer you did not already have.**

### 11

So what do we do with all of this?

Not what most people expect. The answer is not to eliminate the heroic — and I need to be careful here, because the last ten sections could easily be read that way.

Go back to the three things I separated earlier, because the answer is different for each one.

**Heroic syndrome** — the identity trap, where a person's sense of their own worth is attached to being needed — is the only one of the three that genuinely has to go. Not because it makes people bad. Because it makes them structurally unable to finish anything that would render them unnecessary. Every other tool in this book is available to a person with heroic syndrome except the last step of every single one.

**The heroic role** — where a company recurrently needs exceptional intervention to produce an ordinary result — should be dissolved. Not by removing the person. By building the capability the person has been standing in for.

**Heroic capacity** — the ability to operate without a map — should be protected, paid for, and pointed at something worth pointing it at.

Which means the hero and the architect are not opposites, and I would rather you did not leave this chapter believing they are. They are two roles, and a serious company needs both of them permanently.

The architect's territory is the machine. Validated work, defined standards, results that arrive whether or not anybody is inspired this week.

The hero's territory is discovery. The part of the loop where the answer genuinely is not known, where no process can be written in advance because nobody yet knows what it would say, and where the ability to walk into fog and stay functional is not a personality quirk. It is the job description.

The hero asks: *what is possible here that nobody has done?*

The architect asks: *why does this recurring situation still need rescue?*

Both questions are necessary. A company with only architects will execute beautifully and go quietly obsolete. A company with only heroes will produce astonishing things and be unable to repeat any of them, which is where this book began.

Which leaves the practical question, and almost nobody asks it out loud. Which one are you?

The answer that gets founders into trouble is *both*. Some people genuinely can hold both roles and hold them well, and if you are one of them this will sort itself out. But most of the founders I have worked with are decisively better at one, and nearly all of the damage I have watched comes from somebody standing in the wrong one and not being willing to say so.

If you are most alive in the unknown — if the part of the week you actually look forward to is the problem nobody has solved yet — then you belong in discovery. And the most valuable thing you will ever do for your company is find or build an architect for the machine and then genuinely leave them alone. Not supervise them. Leave them.

If you are most alive making things work — if what satisfies you is the process that finally runs clean, the handover that stops failing, the month that needed nobody — then you are an architect. And your obligation is to fund and defend the discovery function rather than starving it because it is messy and does not produce reportable results on your calendar.

There is no shame in either answer. There is enormous damage in getting it wrong, and the damage always looks the same: a person with real heroic capacity standing in the middle of the machine, changing things, because that is where the company put them.

But there is a second version of this, and I see it more often than any other. It does not happen inside one person. It happens between two.

The clearest cases are couples — not couples in general, but couples who are also co-founders, who go home to the same house and continue the same argument over dinner.

Let me say the next part carefully, because it is an observation and not a law. In most of the co-founder couples I have worked with, the wife is the one drawn to the architect's role and the husband is the one drawn to the hero's. Not always. There is nothing genetic about it, and I have seen it the other way round often enough to know better than to make a rule of it. But across a large number of companies it has been true more often than it has not.

What matters is not who is in which role. What matters is that almost none of them see the two roles *as* two roles.

Each of them believes their own role is the correct one, and that the other person is a problem to be managed.

So the architect works, quietly and continuously, to stop the hero from doing the thing the hero does — because every time he does it, something that was stable stops being stable. She is not wrong about that. It is precisely what happens.

And the hero experiences the architect as a brake. He believes, sincerely, that the company would be three times the size if he were simply left alone to move. He is not wrong either, in the narrow sense. He would move faster. He would also, eventually, move straight off a cliff — but that part never shows up in the argument, because it has not happened yet.

What neither of them can see from inside it is how completely each one depends on the other.

The hero does not see that without the architect there is no money and no time to explore with. The freedom he is fighting for is manufactured, every single month, by the boring thing he is impatient with. Remove the machine and he does not become freer. He becomes a man doing emergency work on a business that cannot survive a bad quarter, and there is no exploration in that at all.

The architect does not see that without the hero, the machine she has built with so much care will be overtaken. Not this year. But markets move, customers change, somebody finds a better way to do the thing — and a machine with nobody feeding it new methods is a machine going obsolete at a perfectly steady rate. The predictability she is protecting has a shelf life. The only thing that extends it is somebody walking into the unknown and coming back with something.

So they are not opponents. They are not even colleagues obliged to tolerate one another.

> **The architect and the hero should be each other's closest ally, because over any long period neither of them can produce anything extraordinary without the other.**

And this is not only true at work. It is true in the house.

In most families there is somebody holding the predictability — the routines, the money, the school run, the thing that makes an ordinary week possible — and somebody pulling toward what has not been tried yet. The trip nobody planned. The move to another country. The idea that arrives at eleven at night.

And each of them, quietly, believes the other one is the obstacle. One thinks: *you never let anything happen.* The other thinks: *you never let anything settle.*

They are both describing a role. And they are both describing it as a defect.

The families that work, like the companies that work, are not the ones where somebody eventually wins that argument. They are the ones where both people finally understand what the other is actually producing, stop standing on each other's heels, and agree on where the line between the two territories runs.

### 12

I know exactly how much damage this does, because fifteen years ago I did it to a company on purpose.

I was brought into a business that was about to become the largest in its industry. It had two founders, and without either of them ever using these words, they were a textbook example of the two roles.

One wanted stability and profitability. He wanted the thing to work, to make money, to be able to survive a bad year without anybody's heart rate changing. The other wanted growth, at whatever it cost.

An architect and a hero, in one company, arguing.

And I took a side.

I want to be honest about why, because the reason does not flatter me. This was a much less mature period of my life, and one run far more by ego than I would have admitted at the time. The hero was like me. He thought like me, he moved like me, he found the same things exciting. So obviously he was the one who was right — because if he was right, then so was I.

So I went to work on the architect. Not aggressively. With arguments, and patience, and a great many promises about what we were going to build. It took a while. Eventually he stopped objecting. He stood aside and let the two geniuses go and conquer the world.

In a single quarter we took the company from three million euros to ten.

I am not going to pretend that was luck. The method was good. I did the hero's job properly — I went into the unknown and came back with something that genuinely worked, a way of taking that market at a speed nobody in it had seen before. Then, with the scaling underway and my part finished, I left and went to work on other companies.

A few years later the business was doing fifty million and was by a wide margin the biggest in its industry. Every year there was more revenue than the year before. Every year there was a celebration.

Then I had a meeting with the hero founder, who by that point had become a close friend.

He had just been forced to sell a large part of the company at less than half of what it was worth, because it had come close to insolvency.

Nothing external had happened. There was no crash, no competitor who outplayed them, no market that turned. The systems had simply never caught up with the growth.

The machine had no architect any more, because I had helped remove him from the argument. So when a process was missing, they hired somebody to do it by hand. When the hiring produced chaos, they hired managers to supervise the chaos. Every gap in the architecture was filled with a person, and every person needed a person above them, and in the end the whole structure was held together by headcount, goodwill, and one founder's ability to be everywhere at once.

And the entire way down that road, the revenue went up.

That is the part I still find difficult to look at. Every year that company moved closer to the edge, and every year the number said it was winning, and everybody celebrated. The celebrations were not lies. They were accurate readings of the wrong instrument.

There is a postscript, and it is the reason I tell this story instead of a cleaner one. The founders did eventually exit, for something close to a hundred million euros.

By every visible measure, that is a triumph. It is the number that goes in the press release. If you met either of those men at a conference you would think you were talking to somebody who had simply won.

I know what that number cost. I also know, roughly, what it would have been if the architect had never been talked out of the room.

Which is the most uncomfortable thing in this chapter. The Apollo model does not always end in a crash. Very often it ends in a genuinely large success that everybody congratulates — and only three or four people ever know how much bigger, and how much safer, it should have been.

If I had understood then what I am writing now, I would not have picked a winner. I would have seen that both of those men were extraordinary, and that neither of them was the problem. What that company needed was not for one of them to defeat the other. It was for each of them to be given the territory their capability actually fits, and a line between the two that both of them had agreed to defend.

Instead I found a hero, recognized myself in him, and helped him win.

---

I want to end where this chapter started, because the man I have been using as the example of an architect was not a cautious person.

Roald Amundsen was among the most audacious human beings of his century. First to sail the Northwest Passage. First to the South Pole. Among the first to reach the North Pole by air. He spent his entire life going to places that had killed everyone who tried them before him.

On 18 June 1928, at fifty-five, he took off from Tromsø in a French flying boat — a prototype — to search for the crew of an airship that had crashed in the Arctic. The airship belonged to Umberto Nobile, a man he had flown with two years earlier and had since fallen out with publicly and bitterly, in the newspapers, over who deserved the credit for what.

He went anyway. The aircraft disappeared into the Barents Sea. Some wreckage was found two months later. No trace of the six men aboard has ever been recovered. Nobile, as it happens, was rescued by somebody else.

That is not the death of a bureaucrat. It is the death of a hero, chosen deliberately, in genuinely unknown territory, in an aircraft nobody had proven yet.

And it is the same man who marched exactly twenty miles a day when he could easily have marched forty. Who planted lines of flags east and west of every depot because he assumed things would go wrong. Who put three tons of food in the ground for five men.

He was not the opposite of a hero. He was a hero who knew where heroism belonged — and who understood that the discipline on the ice was never the alternative to the audacity.

It was what paid for it.

---

Which brings us to the first station of the loop, and to a question that sounds simple and that almost nobody answers honestly:

What, exactly, should this thing be capable of producing on an ordinary Tuesday, when nobody is being heroic?

That question is harder than it looks. Answering it badly has cost more companies more money than every operational failure combined.
