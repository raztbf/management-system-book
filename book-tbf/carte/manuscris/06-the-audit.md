### 1

Reality has reported back. The machine ran for ninety days and produced what it produced, and the numbers do not care how good the plan sounded in January. The question is what a company should actually do with them — and in this system, the answer is a specific, repeating event: once a quarter, the whole business stops and looks at reality on purpose.

Not a review of activity. Not a report on how hard everybody worked. An audit of the machine.

The word that matters most in that sentence is *quarter*, because the power of this event comes from its inevitability. Companies do not drift because their people are lazy. They drift because nothing ever forces the entire system to be examined at once — so examination happens only when something breaks, which means it happens under stress, which means it turns into a search for somebody to blame. Put the audit on the calendar instead and all of that disappears. It arrives whether the quarter was good or bad, whether anybody feels like it or not, and because it is not triggered by a problem, its arrival is not evidence that there is one.

Ninety days is the right interval for reasons that are arithmetic rather than philosophical. Any shorter and you are reading noise — panicking at weather. Any longer and the drift gets expensive, because a machine that has been sliding for six months has usually slid somewhere costly.

And the audit itself is almost embarrassingly simple to describe.

**It is four sheets of paper.**

| Sheet | What it produces |
|---|---|
| **Vision** | the gaps |
| **Audit** | the bottleneck behind each gap, and which road it takes |
| **Experiment** | the plan for each thing we do not yet know how to do |
| **Multiplication** | the plan for each thing we already know how to do |

The first day of the audit belongs to the first two sheets: what the machine produced, and what is limiting it. *What we found out.* The remaining days belong to the other two, one sheet per finding. *What we are going to do about it.*

There is no fifth sheet, and there is no fifth activity. Anything discussed during an audit that does not end up on one of these four pieces of paper either belongs in a different meeting or it is theatre.

Let me show you the sheets one at a time — because somewhere around the second one, a question is going to occur to you, and that question is what the middle of this chapter is for.

### 2

![Vision worksheet](imagini/vision.pdf)

The first sheet you have already met. It is the vision from the third chapter — the Ordinary Great Quarter — with two columns added. Across the top it carries its own definition: *a good quarter that can be repeated, without heroics or last-minute saves.* Underneath, five columns: the indicator, what it did last quarter, what it did this quarter, what the vision says it should do by default, and the distance between the last two.

Here is one, filled in, from a real business:

| KPI | Last Quarter | This Quarter | Vision | GAP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,180,000 | 1,340,000 | 1,500,000 | −160,000 |
| Profit | 142,000 | 188,000 | 225,000 | −37,000 |
| Clients | 412 | 468 | 520 | −52 |
| CAC | 96 | 88 | 75 | +13 |
| AOV | 310 | 328 | 360 | −32 |
| Purchase frequency | 1.6 | 1.8 | 2.1 | −0.3 |
| NPS | 41 | 47 | 55 | −8 |
| Best sellers | 6 | 8 | 10 | −2 |

The sheet is read twice, and the two readings answer different questions. Read it first against last quarter: what improved, what deteriorated, what stabilised, what quietly became normal. That is the machine's **direction**, and a company that skips this reading throws away ninety days of data. Then read it again against the vision column. That is not the same question at all. It is not *are we better than we were* — it is *does this machine produce, by default, what we have decided it should*. That is the machine's **distance**.

Now look at the example again, because it contains the moment that separates this sheet from every quarterly review you have ever sat through.

**Every single indicator improved. And every single one is still short of the vision.**

In a review built around targets, that table is a catastrophe: eight rows, eight misses, eight explanations owed. On this sheet it is simply the truth, in two halves: the machine is moving in the right direction, and it is not yet the machine we described. **Nobody failed — and there is a great deal of work.** Both halves matter, and neither is about a person.

A few things about this sheet cost me years each.

The vision column holds a range, not a point — reality has weather in it, and a default that cannot survive an ordinary bad month is not a description of a machine, it is a wish with decimals. The definition at the top says *without heroics*, and that is the clause everybody deletes first: if a number only arrives when the founder personally drags it across the line, the machine does not produce that number — a person does, and the gap has not been closed, only hidden, and it will be back next quarter, slightly larger, in front of a slightly more tired human being. And the sheet must have many rows, never one, because any single indicator can be bought at the expense of another — revenue with margin, growth with debt, volume with quality. Which is also why an indicator that lands *above* the vision gets investigated like a gap: on a sheet like this, a number that is too good almost always means capacity was quietly borrowed from a row below it. Notice, in passing, what the little **+13** on the CAC row tells you — for that indicator, lower is better, and the sheet knows it. The sheet is measuring reality. It is not scoring people.

One more thing this sheet quietly teaches, and it changes what "results" means in a company. The output of a business is not the two or three financial numbers that arrive at the end and get sent to the bank. It is **everything the machine produced** — every indicator in the vision, and behind them, every indicator of every validated process. Measure only the end of the chain and every possible failure looks identical: a smaller number than you wanted.

The first session of the audit ends when this sheet is read, and its entire output is a list of gaps. Not decisions. Not commitments. A list of distances — each one governed by the rule that makes everything after it possible:

> **A gap is not a bad sign about a person. It is information about the machine.**

This is not politeness, and it is not culture-poster kindness. It is ownership, in the literal sense. The moment you decide a person is the reason for a shortfall, you have said — without noticing — that this person matters more than the machine. You have handed them all the power. **You have made them a hero.** Find instead that a process is the reason, and the power returns to the thing the company actually owns and can actually change.

### 3

![Quarterly Audit worksheet](imagini/audit.pdf)

Every gap from the first sheet becomes one row on the second, and the second sheet asks exactly one question about each of them:

> **What is blocking the machine from producing the output described in the vision?**

Notice what it does not ask. There is no field for a commitment, a stretch, or a plan to try harder. There is the gap, the bottleneck that limits it, the processes involved — and two checkboxes: **MULTIPLICATION** or **DISCOVERY**.

The answer to the question is not allowed to be an opinion. It comes out of the indicators of the validated processes inside the machine, and finding it is the fifth chapter's arithmetic run backwards. Say the gap is in new clients. The sales process is a chain — companies identified, people reached, qualifying conversations, proposals, contracts — and every link carries an indicator with a derived standard: the hundred calls a day was never an ambition, it was calculated backwards from the number of contracts the vision requires. So you walk back along the chain until you find the link running under its standard. Maybe qualification holds and lead generation does not. Maybe both hold and the channel mix has moved underneath you. Whatever it is, it has a **location**, and a location can be worked on.

That middle column — *processes involved* — decides whether the audit is management or theatre. If it can be filled with real, named, validated processes, the conversation is about mechanism, and conversations about mechanism converge. If the honest entry is *sales, generally*, there is no machine there — there is activity — and a table of senior people staring at a gap with no processes behind it will do the only thing left to do, which is argue.

> **A meeting without validated processes is not an audit. It is a debate, and the best talker wins.**

The best talker is almost never the person closest to the data. And an empty Processes column is not a failure of the audit — it is one of the most valuable findings the audit can produce, written down as a finding in its own right: *we cannot answer this, because no validated process exists here.* In a company's first year of doing this, that line is usually worth more than everything else on the sheet.

There is one thing this sheet cannot do, and it has to be solved outside the sheet: it can tell you **where**, and it cannot tell you **why**. The first chapter showed you Taiichi Ohno solving that problem with a piece of chalk — a circle drawn on the factory floor, an engineer stood inside it for hours, because the first hour of watching shows you what you already believe and only somewhere in the third does the model break and the reality appear. The data never contains the operator walking four steps for a part that could sit at his elbow. **The indicators tell you where. The chalk circle tells you why.** Which is why the field work happens in the weeks *before* the audit, not during it: the people who own the implicated processes go and stand in front of the actual work, follow one order or one client or one shift all the way through, and arrive having *seen* something instead of having read a report about a report. The audit is not where the investigation happens. It is where the investigations are put side by side and the company decides. It also settles who should be invited, and settles it better than any argument about hierarchy ever could: whoever can answer the question with evidence, regardless of title.

Then the two checkboxes — and there are two because there are only two moves. The fourth chapter established it and I will not re-argue it: a company that wants more output can make more of the known, or turn some of the unknown into known. Multiplication, or discovery. **There is no third move.** What looks like a third move — leaning harder on the machine you already have — is not growth; it is borrowing, and the first chapter showed you the repayment schedule.

When the second sheet is full, the company has its quarter: a set of multiplications and a set of experiments. A handful of rows, one checkbox each. That is the entire strategic content of ninety days, and it fits on one side of one piece of paper.

### 4

And here is the question that has been forming since somewhere in the middle of the last section, so let me say it out loud for you.

*Wait. Why all of this? Why route every gap through bottlenecks and checkboxes, experiments and hypotheses — when we could simply take each gap, turn it into an objective, give the objective an owner, a deadline and a bonus, and meet again in ninety days to see who delivered? Isn't that simpler?*

It is simpler. I know precisely how much simpler, because that is how I did it for fifteen years.

---

### 5

For the first fifteen years of my career — more than three hundred companies, twenty-three industries — the growth side of everything I built ran on objectives. And I did not merely use the instrument. I sharpened it. When I felt it strain, I built better versions of it: I invented two variations of my own, Dynamic OKRs and Progressive OKRs, each designed to repair a specific failure I kept meeting. I wrote over a hundred articles about the power of setting goals, and I taught the method to thousands of companies.

**I did not stumble into the altar of objectives. I built it, I served at it, and I sold seats.**

And it worked. This matters, and I need you to hold it firmly, because the version of this argument in which objectives never worked is both false and useless. Fifty of the companies running my system became leaders of their markets. I watched businesses travel from one or two million to beyond a hundred million with objectives as the engine. If the tool did not deliver, you would have thrown it away years ago without my help.

The problem is subtler and much more expensive: **it works well enough.** Well enough that nobody ever goes looking for something better. Well enough that the costs stay invisible, because they are paid in a currency nobody tracks — in the growth that did not happen, in the sleepless nights that felt like the price of ambition, in the quiet disappearance of joy from work that people still describe as successful. For years, the question I never asked was the only one worth asking: the hundred million was real — but if the method had been different, would it have been two hundred? Three hundred? And would the people who built it still like Mondays?

I can ask it now, because a few years ago I did something that gave me the answer.

### 6

I audited my own system.

The spirit of it matters: I was not hunting for a flaw, and I had no thesis. Thousands of companies were running something I had built, and I wanted to know which parts of it carried weight and which parts were decoration — the housekeeping you eventually owe anything you have been doing for fifteen years.

I ran the audit across **three thousand and twenty-three companies** — essentially the entire population using the full system, not a sample and not a flattering selection. And the first measurement was the simplest one available: of all the objectives set in all those quarterly meetings, how many were achieved?

**Fifty-six percent. Forty-four percent were not.**

I want to describe my reaction to that number honestly: I had none. Fifty-six percent, across three thousand real companies with real markets, is respectable, and every consultant alive carries ready-made absolution for the other forty-four — stretch goals are supposed to be missed; seventy percent attainment is the healthy range. I had taught those absolutions myself. I noted the figure and moved to the next item.

And then, days later, for reasons I still cannot fully explain, I went back. Not for a better headline number — for a different question entirely: **what did the achieved objectives have in common with each other? And what did the missed ones share?**

I cut the two piles by every variable I could think of — industry, company size, ambition, financial versus operational. Nothing spoke. Which is how you recognise the moment when something finally does.

**Ninety-seven percent of the achieved objectives had one property in common: the company already possessed a validated method for producing that result.** A business with three working locations sets the objective of opening two more — and opens them, because the economics, the layout, the hiring and the path to break-even have all been walked three times. The objective pointed at a capability already sitting in the building, and put a date on it.

**Eighty-eight percent of the missed objectives shared the opposite property: nobody in the company knew the method.** *Grow sales by thirty percent.* How? Nobody knew. That was the whole anatomy of the objective — a number, a deadline, an owner, and a hole where the cause should have been.

Where the method existed, it was applied, and the objective took the credit. Where it did not exist, the objective sat in a document for ninety days, generated anxiety, and then generated an explanation.

> **In three thousand companies, an objective never once produced a method.**

The first chapter of this book drew a line between a result and a cause — a result is what happened, a cause is what makes it happen again — and it took me until that spreadsheet to see which side of the line my life's instrument sat on. An objective is a **result written in advance.** It belongs to the same family as the thing it is supposed to produce, which is exactly why it cannot produce it.

Now carry the two numbers back to the second sheet, because they are the answer to the question you asked in the middle of this chapter. The ninety-seven percent *is* the multiplication checkbox — a validated method, pointed somewhere and given a date. That is territory where objectives genuinely shine, and this system uses them there gladly, under a different name. The eighty-eight percent is discovery territory with a target nailed over it — ground where nobody knows how, carrying a number, a deadline and somebody's name.

One instrument. Two grounds. Two entirely different outcomes — and, as it took me much longer to understand, two entirely different states for the human being underneath:

> **An objective with a validated method behind it creates focus. An objective without one creates a crisis.**

---

### 7

My first instinct was that I had made an error somewhere, and I recommend that instinct — but three thousand and twenty-three companies is not a sample size that produces accidents of that magnitude. My second instinct was to find out whether anybody had seen this before me.

Somebody had. In 1987 the *Journal of Applied Psychology* published a meta-analysis of more than a hundred goal-setting studies, and found that the effect of goals falls to roughly half on complex tasks — business simulations, scientific and engineering work — because, in the authors' own explanation, on complex work everything depends on whether the person can discover an appropriate strategy. The strategy is the method. And the third author on that paper is **Edwin A. Locke** — the man who, with Gary Latham, founded goal-setting theory. Eighteen years later Latham himself put it beyond misreading: a performance goal *should not be set until an employee has the knowledge to attain it.* The founders had published the limit of their own instrument, in plain sight, decades before my spreadsheet. **What travelled was the part that fit on a slide.**

And once you know the mechanism exists, you can watch it run in a laboratory.

A hundred and seventy people played a complex business simulation — a market, competitors, thirteen rounds of decisions, and halfway through, a deregulation shock that invalidated every strategy that had been working. One group was given a hard performance goal: reach twenty-one percent market share. One group was given a learning goal: discover and test strategies for this market. One group was told to do their best. Of the fifty with the learning goal, eight reached twenty-one percent. Of the fifty-nine doing their best, six. **Of the sixty-one people explicitly instructed to reach twenty-one percent — one.** The people aimed at the number were, by a wide margin, the least capable of reaching the number, because under a hard target on unfamiliar ground people do not search. They grab the first thing that resembles progress and push, since being systematic looks like standing still, and standing still is the one thing they have been told not to do.

The weight of the target grows with the person carrying it, and the cleanest demonstration of that comes from football. Geir Jordet, a Norwegian professor of sport psychology, has spent two decades analysing penalty shoot-outs from major tournaments, kick by kick, on video. His most uncomfortable finding: **the superstars — the players carrying individual awards and the status that comes with them — miss more often than their unheralded teammates.** And you can see the mechanism in their bodies before the ball is ever struck: the players under the heaviest expectation rush, stepping up almost immediately after the whistle, and avoid looking at the goalkeeper — the posture of somebody trying to exit a situation rather than solve a problem. Same ball, same eleven metres, same professional feet. The more identity a person has attached to the outcome, the worse the outcome gets.

And you do not need a stadium to produce that state; a calendar will do. In a year-long field experiment inside a large Swedish public organisation, employees randomly assigned to a twelve-week evaluation cycle carried significantly higher stress hormones than colleagues on a six-week cycle — cortisol up by roughly a fifth — while their *self-reported* fatigue showed no difference at all. They could not feel it; their blood could. The researchers' explanation: it is the anticipation that does the damage — the months of quietly rehearsing justifications for a verdict that has not arrived yet, cognitive work that is invisible, exhausting, and directly in the way of the actual job.

Read that result carefully, because it runs opposite to the obvious conclusion. **It is not the evaluation that produces the fear. It is the interval.** The answer is not to look at people less often. It is to look often enough that nothing has time to accumulate — and to make sure that when you look, there is nothing to defend. The first half of that sentence is the quarterly rhythm. The second half is the four sheets.

### 8

The studies told me what happens and how reliably. But the piece I understand best did not come from a journal. It came from six months in which, for the first time in my adult life, I watched myself.

About seven years ago I stepped away from work for half a year and sank into my hobbies — no plan, no programme, just the things I love, done for as long as I felt like doing them. And somewhere in those months I noticed something I had managed not to notice for forty years. Every problem in my work and my life, I had always treated as a **crisis**. But when a problem turned up inside one of my passions — a technique that would not come, a build that would not work — it was never a crisis. It was a **puzzle**.

Those are not two words for one experience. They are two different states of the body.

**A crisis demands anxiety. A puzzle demands curiosity.**

And the two states age completely differently. In curiosity I can stay with a problem for as long as the problem takes — months, years — without burning out. In anxiety I am always looking for an exit. Any exit. Sometimes the exit is the solution. Just as often it is another problem, loud enough to drown this one out.

That difference would hardly matter if hard problems were quick. They are not. It can take three or four years to solve the puzzle of holding a healthy weight. Four or five to solve the puzzle of a happy marriage. Seven to find the business model that turns your vision into something real. Now run the arithmetic on the other state: anxiety can be held for three months. Six, perhaps. A year, if you are masochistic enough and well trained in absorbing pain. And then the mind and the body resign.

Here is the part that took me longest to see, because the resignation does not look like resignation. The mind does not drop the problem. **It simplifies the problem into one it can escape from.** The slow, complex puzzle of a marriage becomes: *I have the wrong partner.* Half a year into a strict diet, the verdict arrives that the problem is genetic — heavy bones, a slow metabolism, a body that was always going to be this shape. We reach these conclusions not through insight but through a need for the pain to stop. And try the other frame on the same marriage — a set of pieces that create love, safety and calm, some already in place, some missing, some held wrong — and every piece you find and fit produces progress you can feel, and the more of the picture appears, the more curious you become about the rest. Same marriage. Same problem. Different state.

Now put an objective on ground where nobody knows the method, and watch what you have actually built. You have taken a problem — usually a genuinely hard one, the kind that needs years — and installed a number, a deadline and an audience over it. From that moment it cannot be a puzzle for anyone in the building, because a verdict is hanging over it. And the timeline of anxiety is months, while the timeline of discovery is years — so somewhere around the second month, the owner's mind will do what my mind did with my marriage and my weight. It will quietly trade the real problem for a simpler one it can survive. An explanation. A reclassification. A best guess, announced publicly and then defended to the deadline, because from the moment it was spoken, a person's competence was welded to it.

**That is what the eighty-eight percent looks like from the inside.** Not laziness. Not weak accountability. Ordinary minds, placed in a scheduled crisis whose honest duration exceeded what a mind can hold, finding the exit — and delivering it, four times a year, in the form of an explanation.

Now you know why the second sheet has two checkboxes and neither of them says *commit harder*. The rest of this chapter is the machinery that replaces the crisis — and I can show it to you quickly now, because you finally know what every field on it is for.

### 9

![Experiment worksheet](imagini/experiment.pdf)

Every row that ticked DISCOVERY becomes one of these.

At the top, the experiment's name and the bottleneck it exists to solve — no experiment lives in this company without a stated reason. Then six blocks: the **process** it touches · the **hypothesis**, written as *if… then…* · the **source** of the idea — data, an insight, a client request, somebody's experience, a book · the **KPIs** we will measure · the **constraints** — budget, time, and the rules we will not break in order to win · and the **method**, how it will actually run.

And then the footer, which surprises everybody: **owner, team, deadline.** Exactly like an objective. A name, a group of people, a date. Nothing about this sheet is an argument for vagueness, and there is nothing gentle about an experiment with your name on it and six weeks on the clock.

**The entire difference between this sheet and an objective lives in two fields.** Because there is a hypothesis and there are agreed measures, what the owner owes at the deadline is not a result. It is **evidence** — and evidence, unlike a result whose method nobody possesses, can be delivered honestly on a date. Those two fields are also what changes the emotional physics of the whole company, and this is a design requirement, not sentiment. People, it turns out, learn very little from their own failures — failure threatens the ego, and the mind tunes out — yet learn perfectly well from everybody else's. So the gain from a failed experiment is real but not automatic: it needs a structure that lands the failure on the idea instead of the person. The hypothesis and the KPIs are that structure. They are the two lines that let somebody stand up at the next audit and say *the hypothesis did not hold* instead of *I did not deliver* — and everything this book has said about fear, defence and the two weeks of assembling explanations turns on the difference between those two sentences.

Let me show you what this sheet makes possible, with the experiment that changed my own company's history.

About twelve years into my career I founded TBF, to hand what I had learned to as many entrepreneurs as possible. I had one problem: with this audience, I had no brand. Plenty of success inside the companies I had worked with directly — nothing in education. For two years we did everything known to work: press, webinars, newsletters. It moved like honey. Then, one autumn, an idea: thirty days, live, every single morning, ninety minutes from ten o'clock — teaching the actual systems. Not a teaser for a course. The whole thing, free.

Almost nobody in the company believed it could work, including me. The realistic forecast was that by day four I would be broadcasting to five people. And here is the point of telling you this: **an idea with those odds does not survive a meeting organised around objectives.** Nobody volunteers to own a sales target attached to a thirty-day free marathon that everybody at the table expects to fail. Under the old system, that idea dies in the planning session, politely, without anybody ever finding out what it was worth. So it was not proposed as an objective. It was proposed as an experiment — a hypothesis, a set of measures, a constraint, a deadline.

The first run: close to two thousand companies registered, and eleven hundred were still attending on the final morning. And then something that made no rational sense at all — more than six hundred of them bought the course that explained the material they had just watched me teach in full, for free, for a month.

What happened next matters more than the result. We did not scale it. We wrote it down for what it was: a Level 1 process — *it worked once.* The following year we ran it again, deliberately, to find out whether the first result had been the method or the weather. Eight thousand companies registered; more than five thousand watched every day. Level 2. Into the machine it went, and it has run five times since. More than twenty thousand companies have passed through it, and it is the single most important growth engine my company has.

Total cost of finding that out: one experiment sheet, and one month of mornings. The old system would have quietly priced it at zero.

How to *design* an experiment that cannot lie to you — falsifiable hypotheses, measures chosen before you know the answer, what the Source field is worth after two years of filling it in — is the next chapter's work. This sheet is simply where a discovery row goes to become one.

### 10

![Multiplication worksheet](imagini/multiplication.pdf)

And every row that ticked MULTIPLICATION becomes one of these.

At the top, the process to be multiplied — and a small field that carries more weight than it looks: **which multiplication this is.** Number one, number two, number five. The fourth chapter defined Level 3 as a process that works through other people; the honest corollary is that the *first* multiplication of anything is half an experiment, because you are not only copying the process — you are discovering how it copies. It will be slower and more expensive than the second one, and that is not failure, that is what first ones are for. Writing the number down sets everybody's expectations before the money moves.

Then the sheet asks four things. **People to recruit** — roles and counts. **People to transfer** from the units that already run the process — and this is the field that saves multiplications, because a working process always contains parts that never made it into the documentation, and those parts travel in people, not in files. The team that gives up two experienced people and recruits replacements is, incidentally, running a free test of itself: if it returns to standard, its processes really were Level 2; if it cannot, you have just learned something enormous at the cheapest possible price. **Send the carriers, not the document** — you saw in the fifth chapter what happened to the company that photocopied a factory and scattered the people who understood it. Then **standards with a ramp** — the indicator targets at three, six and twelve months, because a new unit is not asked to perform at full standard in its first month, and pretending otherwise manufactures exactly the panic this system exists to remove. And **owner, budget, deadline**, with the deadline defined precisely: *team complete and running.*

Notice that this sheet is covered in numbers and dates, and that this is entirely legitimate — because here, **the method is known.** A target set where nobody knows the method is a wish. A target set on a validated, documented method is just a specification. That distinction is the whole chapter, printed on two forms.

One rule governs this box, and it gets broken constantly, always with the best intentions:

> **You may only multiply Level 2 and above.**

If the thing that would close the gap has worked exactly once — one person, one place, one occasion — the job for next quarter is not multiplication. It is taking that process from Level 1 to Level 2, over on the discovery branch. This discipline costs something every time, because the thing worked, the gap fits it, and everybody wants to roll it out on Monday. If you need to know what that instinct costs, ask the industry that validates for a living: in pharmaceutical development, with billions of dollars riding on the ladder and the law enforcing it, roughly **seventy percent of drugs that succeed in Phase 2 do not survive Phase 3.** The Phase 2 results were real. They simply did not generalise. Seven out of ten — in the most disciplined validation system human beings have ever built. Now consider what your one good quarter proves.

### 11

The practical arrangements are few, and none of them is a detail.

The audit takes **three days**, because the first half-day is spent merely arriving — people are still in last week, still answering the question somebody asked them in the corridor on the way out — and real analysis does not begin until everybody has actually landed. It happens **away from the offices**, because context leaks: in your own building every person sits inside their department, their status and tomorrow's meetings, and somewhere else they become slightly different and considerably more honest. And the telephones stay upstairs, in the bedrooms. Not on the table. Not face down. Upstairs.

And the audit has one more output — the one almost every company forgets, and the one the whole loop quietly depends on.

Go back to the drawing from the first chapter and look at where the two branches point. Not at the machine. **At the vision.** Because a finished quarter of experiments and multiplications produces two different things, and they travel to two different places. The first is a method, or a piece of capacity — that goes into the machine, the way everything enters the machine: built beside it, validated, then merged. The second is **knowledge about what this business is now capable of** — and that does not belong in the machine at all, because the vision was written with what you knew at the time, and what you knew at the time is precisely what the quarter just changed. Discover a way to acquire customers at half the cost you believed was structural, and your vision's profit range is now sitting on a constraint that no longer exists — leaving it there is a failure of ambition. So the last act of every audit is a question addressed to the vision itself: *is this still true?*

> **The machine is updated by validated methods. The vision is updated by validated knowledge.**

Which is also how you know when you have arrived somewhere. Not when you hit the number. When the machine produces the number repeatably, without heroics — **when hitting it has stopped being interesting** — at which point every gap on the first sheet reads zero, and the honest thing to do is raise the vision and begin again.

---

### 12

Two quarterly gatherings, then. The same three days, the same house, the same telephones upstairs. From the outside, indistinguishable. And they build two different companies, because they open with two different questions.

*Who missed their number?* The answer to that question is always a person. I asked it for fifteen years, and I can tell you exactly what it produces: two weeks of defence-drafting before anybody arrives, ambition calibrated quietly downward, ideas with long odds dying unproposed, and four verdicts a year on the identity of everybody senior enough to own a number.

*What is blocking the machine?* The answer to that question is always a mechanism. And everything people call culture — whether bad news arrives early, whether anybody proposes the thing that might not work, whether the quiet person with the actual data speaks — follows from which question the quarter is built around. You do not fix culture with values on a wall. You fix it by changing the first question of the first morning.

There is a consequence I did not see coming when I made the change. The fifth chapter described what Kahneman and Klein established about skilled intuition — it grows only in environments regular enough to be predictable, with long practice and honest feedback. A company that reinvents its rules every ninety days is an environment where nobody can ever become good at anything. The audit builds the opposite: the vision holds, the machine holds, the processes hold, and what varies from quarter to quarter is which experiments are running beside the machine, in a bounded space designed for exactly that. **Stability everywhere, variation in one controlled place.** That is not only how the machine stays protected. It is the only arrangement in which the people inside it can become excellent at their work.

Winners and losers have the same goals. That line belongs to James Clear, and I read it years before I understood it. What winners and losers do not share is a method for finding out what is actually true — and that is what the four sheets are: not paperwork, but the method, printed. An audit run on them produces no winners and no losers. It produces two lists and a decision — and the person who owns the worst number on the table is, quite often, the most useful person at it, because their gap is where the machine is about to learn something.

One list says multiply. The company will go and do more of what it already knows, and most of its growth will come from that list, and almost nobody will find it exciting.

The other list says: find out.
