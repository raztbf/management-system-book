# 6. The Audit

### Seventy-Eight Percent a Year, Four Sheets of Paper, and the Meeting That Ran Twice

---

### 1

Three years ago, two close friends asked me for a favour.

They own a restaurant chain, and for five straight years that chain had grown at seventy-eight percent a year — the kind of number that gets you profiled in magazines and imitated by competitors. They ran their company on the quarterly method most of the world runs on, and they ran it properly: every ninety days, the two founders and their five directors left the offices for three days, took over a rented guesthouse, and locked the telephones in the bedrooms. Day one, they evaluated the past quarter's objectives and drew the lessons. Days two and three, they set the objectives for the next ninety days. Each objective got an owner, each owner got a bonus riding on it, and everybody went back to work.

They had learned that structure from me. I built it, refined it and taught it for more than fifteen years, to more than thirty thousand companies, and these two had followed it faithfully for five years and grown spectacularly on it. But now they had heard — from me, and from a few other entrepreneurs — that I had stopped using it. That there was a new system. And the fragments they had heard sounded like news from another planet. *What do you mean, no objectives? Why would anyone push without a bonus to lose? What do you mean, no saving the quarter?* I understood their confusion completely, because before I built the new system, I would have said exactly the same things.

So they asked me to come to their next quarterly meeting and show them the transition, and I agreed, on one condition. I would not open my mouth on day one. They would run the meeting exactly as they always ran it, I would sit and watch, and only then would I show them another way — on their own numbers, on their own quarter, on the spot. I have spent a career watching people learn, and they learn one way above all others: not from theory delivered in advance, but from feedback that arrives while they are doing something that matters to them.

What follows is those three days, more or less as they happened. I am telling you this story instead of explaining the audit to you, because the audit is four sheets of paper, and four sheets of paper are easy to describe and easy to forget. What these seven people went through is not.

---

### 2

Day one belonged to the old system, and the old system opened the way it always opens: with the evaluation. Four objectives had been set ninety days earlier. One at a time, each director stood up in front of the group and accounted for theirs.

**Sales growth of twenty percent per restaurant** belonged to the marketing director. It had not been achieved — she had delivered twelve. For ninety minutes she walked us through sixty slides of market context explaining why. The centrepiece was a competitor who had opened three new restaurants that quarter, all near hers, all riding the hype that new places get; a large share of customers had simply gone to try the new thing. One of the founders interrupted: *did you not know the competitor was opening?* She knew, she said. She had not expected the effect to be that large. She owned the failure, and she announced the fix: next time a competitor opens near one of our locations, she would raise the marketing budgets much more aggressively.

*What I was thinking, and did not say:* the fix she had just committed to was a guess. Nobody had ever tested whether bigger budgets keep customers from trying a shiny new opening — in my experience, they mostly do not, and there are markets where the smarter move is to absorb two months of hype and let a good product bring people back on its own. Ninety days earlier she had guessed that the competitor would not hurt her, and been wrong. Now, to exit the failure with dignity, she was guessing again — with more money. The meeting recorded it as a lesson learned.

**Opening two new locations, within twenty percent of budget,** belonged to the development director, and it had been achieved — technically, with one day to spare. Two nights before this meeting he had stayed up until dawn with the builder fixing a façade problem so the second location could open before the quarter closed. His ninety minutes were a war epic, told with real pride. He knew the equipment supplier had a habit of delivering late, so he had taken the supplier's sales director out to dinner and charmed him into delivering our machines first, ahead of every other client. When the menu screens got stuck in customs with a ten-day delay, he drove to an electronics store and bought televisions off the shelf. He had gone to war with a neighbouring restaurant that was sabotaging the opening. And he let us understand, with just enough modesty, that without him the opening would have slipped by at least thirty days — while also assuring us that his opening process was superbly tuned. If he runs it.

The applause was warm — a little thinner from the two directors who had missed their targets, but warm. And then one of the founders stood up and topped the story: in the company's first three years he had done the same and worse, no weekends, debts at the door, and once, before an opening, he had personally rebuilt a delivery of tables that had arrived three centimetres too tall.

*What I was thinking:* heroes attract heroes, and then they manufacture more. Of course the founders applauded — this man was them, ten years younger. But listen to what was actually said: the process is superb, *and* it works only if he is in it. When heroism is what a group celebrates, its most capable people will make sure — without ever deciding to — that every process they build runs only while their own backside is in the seat. Not because they are bad people. I have honestly never met bad people in this work. I have met people surviving inside a system, using whatever that system rewards.

**Cutting food waste by fifteen percent** belonged to the operations director, who used no slides and spoke for nearly two hours. He opened with his victories. One weekend, running on four hours of sleep, he had personally moved stock between locations — pulling product that was about to expire out of the slow restaurants and rushing it to the busy ones. He had spent four days standing in two kitchens watching cooks weigh portions, and discovered half the plates going out with two hundred grams of meat instead of a hundred and fifty, because at rush volume nobody weighs — they eyeball. He described how he had yelled the kitchens straight and then checked them mercilessly. And after all that: waste was down nine percent, not fifteen. He had lost weeks helping the two openings. He could not replace his weakest workers because HR's hands were full with the recruitment target. And he wanted the group to hear two things: that fifteen percent was a fantasy that could not be reached, and that he deserved his bonus anyway, because what he had done was extraordinary.

*What I was thinking:* I had just watched a hero whose magic did not come off. A hero in that position will always show you the sacrifices — the sleepless weekend, the four days in the kitchen — because the sacrifices prove that the problem is reality, not him. What a hero will never stand up and say is: *I tried seven things, I measured them, five did nothing and two worked well.* That sentence is available only to somebody for whom the quarter was never about themselves. The hero does not fail; reality fails, around him. And a person without the syndrome, holding the same bad number, would have proposed the next ten experiments instead of a verdict on what is possible.

**Recruiting three location managers and fifteen workers** belonged to the HR director, and it was achieved: three managers, fifteen workers, all trained, all working at standard. Then she spent an hour on what it had cost. Two full weekends at the office to compress the training and hit the deadline. One freshly trained manager poached by a better offer, leaving her — her words — in the mud, so she had poached a trained manager from a competitor in return and spent a weekend in his location making sure he understood our systems. The objective was green. The green had been dragged over the line.

Then came the second half of the day: conclusions. The meeting decided that the marketing director should raise budgets wherever competitors open nearby. That the operations director *hugs the NO* — always finds the reason a thing cannot be done — and that this attitude is what blocks results. That directors must declare their hiring needs three months in advance so HR can serve them.

The development director was praised again, by name, in front of everyone. The two who had missed their numbers sat with the particular stillness of people practising their faces. Officially, lessons had been learned and the team was ready to plan the next quarter.

I had watched this exact day, in this exact shape, hundreds of times across fifteen years, and I had designed most of its furniture. Sitting in the corner with no slides and no objective, I finally had the distance to name what it was.

> **An awards ceremony for the heroes, and a quiet execution for the rest.**

The finance director, incidentally, was the only person who had arrived without an objective of his own. It is worth remembering him — the one calm chair in the region — because nothing that happened all day had touched him, and that is its own data point about what the day was actually made of.

---

### 3

This is where I broke my silence, and I did it with one question.

*Before we plan anything — what do the two objectives that were achieved have in common with each other? And what do the two that were missed have in common?*

It took a while, and some steering. The first answers were about effort, about market conditions, about attitude. But eventually the group arrived, on its own, at the property that actually split the four objectives in two.

**The achieved objectives had a validated method behind them.** The development director had opened thirteen locations. He knew every problem that could surface, how much slack to plan for each, what he needed from every other director — and he had harassed them until he got it. The HR director likewise: she had a proven way of finding the right people and inducting them. Yes, she worked weekends; yes, it took several attempts; but at every step she knew exactly what works and what does not.

**The missed objectives had no method at all.** Nobody in the company knew how to make customers ignore a hyped competitor. Nobody knew how to take waste from six percent to three. Those two objectives consisted, in their entirety, of a number, a deadline, an owner and a bonus — with a hole where the method should have been.

Then I told them something I had never taught in any of my courses, because I had only recently learned it myself. A while back I had audited my own life's work: I took one thousand and twenty-four of the roughly three thousand companies running my full system — the system these founders were using at that very table — and measured what actually happened to the objectives set in meetings exactly like this one. **Fifty-six percent were achieved; forty-four were not. Ninety-four percent of the achieved objectives had a validated method behind them. Eighty-eight percent of the missed ones did not.** Whatever the industry, whatever the ambition, the pattern barely moved. Where a method existed, it got applied, and the objective took the credit. Where none existed, the objective sat in a document for ninety days, produced anxiety, and then produced an explanation — I had just listened to two of those explanations, beautifully crafted, that same morning.

> **An objective rarely produces a method. Mostly, it finds one that already exists — and takes the credit.**

Rarely is not never, and I want to be honest about that, because the numbers say ninety-four and eighty-eight — not a hundred. Under some targets, somebody genuinely does invent a way where none existed, and every leader remembers those moments vividly, because they are glorious. But look at what you are betting on when you run a company's growth that way: an engine that fires perhaps one time in ten, remembered as if it fired every time. The exception is real. Building on it is the expensive part.

And it is not only my data. In a laboratory version of exactly this situation, researchers ran a hundred and seventy people through a complex business simulation — competitors, thirteen rounds of decisions, and halfway through, a deregulation shock that broke every strategy that had been working. One group was told to hit a hard number: twenty-one percent market share. One group was told to learn: discover and test strategies for this market. One group was told to do their best. Of the fifty learners, eight reached the number anyway. Of the fifty-nine doing their best, six. Of the sixty-one people explicitly aimed at the number — **one.** On unfamiliar ground, a target does not even deliver the target. Under it, people stop searching; they grab the first thing that resembles progress and push, because being systematic looks like standing still, and standing still is the one thing they have been forbidden to do. I discovered this finding years after my own audit, and I have never needed a study less and treasured one more.

---

### 4

Then I asked my second question, and this one ended the peace.

*Of the objectives achieved this quarter — how many would repeat next quarter if the director responsible were replaced tomorrow by somebody competent from outside?*

The argument that followed was the loudest hour of the three days. The directors who had delivered defended themselves with complete sincerity: without their experience, their relationships, their feel for what must happen and when, none of it would repeat — and they were right. Then they defended the deeper position underneath: that this is simply how it is. That you need exceptional people for this to work at all. That what they carry cannot be written into a process, because a thousand things can go wrong and you cannot write a thousand cases into a document.

The founders went quiet in a different way. You could watch the same thought land on both of them at once: *the business is in the directors' hands. If these people leave, everything goes with them.* They had spent five years celebrating exactly the indispensability that was now frightening them.

And the two directors who had missed their numbers said something that deserves more attention than it ever gets in meetings like this. When you finally achieve an objective, they said, the company simply files it under *normal* — and expects it again next quarter, on top of the new objectives. But repeating last quarter's miracle often takes as much effort as the first performance, sometimes more. So quarter by quarter the plate fills: new targets to hit, old miracles to maintain, all of it by hand.

I stopped the argument there — not because it had gone wrong, but because it had gone perfectly. Every wound this book has described was now open on the table, named by the people who carried it. And I know one thing about teaching that I trust completely: the moment people learn is the moment it hurts. If it does not hurt, the brain files it under *interesting* and keeps walking.

So I gave them, in ninety minutes, everything you have read in this book so far. The difference between a result and a cause, and why their seventy-eight percent a year was a stack of results. The hero and the architect — the capacity, the role and the syndrome — and what it means that every one of them had all three. Vision as a default output rather than a summit. The machine, the four levels of validation, the known and the unknown. I will not repeat those chapters here; the point is what happened as they landed. The directors were not hearing accusations. They were hearing, for the first time, accurate descriptions of their own days — and every few minutes somebody said some version of *that is exactly what happened to me in March.* By the end they had understood the thing the whole morning had been demonstrating: **they were not building a machine. They were managing an output, by hand, four times a year, and calling the hands heroes.**

---

### 5

Then we stopped diagnosing and started building, and the first thing we built was the vision.

Their old vision was a victory: *become the biggest restaurant chain in the country.* We replaced it — with the founders visibly nervous about it — with a description of an ordinary great quarter: what this machine should produce in a normal ninety days, without heroics and without a single saved result. We took the vision worksheet from the third chapter, left the reality columns empty for the moment, and spent ninety minutes filling in only the indicators and the standards. Here is where the sheet ended up:

![Vision worksheet](imagini/vision.pdf)

| # | Indicator | Vision (default / quarter) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | New restaurants opened | 2 |
| 2 | Budget deviation per opening | max. 10% |
| 3 | Days from signed lease to opening | max. 90 |
| 4 | Openings requiring all-nighters or founder intervention | 0 |
| 5 | Minimum profit / restaurant older than 1 year | €100K |
| 6 | New restaurant reaches network standards | max. 6 months |
| 7 | Sales growth per mature restaurant | 3–5% |
| 8 | Food waste, as share of sales | max. 3% |
| 9 | Average location rating (Google) | 4.2–5 (avg. 4.5) |
| 10 | Internal operational audit score | min. 90% per location |
| 11 | New products tested on 1,000 customers | 2–4 |
| 12 | Customers buying 3+ times per month | min. 25% |
| 13 | Location managers trained, with 2 months of practice | 2 |
| 14 | Manager retention at 12 months | min. 90% |
| 15 | Worker turnover | max. 10% / quarter |

Look at row four for a moment, because it caused the longest argument of the ninety minutes. *Openings requiring all-nighters or founder intervention: zero.* That row does not measure a result. It measures how the results are produced — it is the phrase *without heroics* converted into a number — and the development director recognised instantly that it was aimed at the most applauded parts of his morning.

When the sheet was finished, something unexpected happened: the founders were scared. It took a minute to draw out why. A machine that produces all of this, every quarter, predictably, without rescue — magnificent. But their dream, the biggest chain in the country, appeared nowhere on the page. Their victory had been deleted.

So we did the arithmetic together. If the machine on that sheet exists — two clean openings a quarter, new locations reaching standard in six months, mature locations growing on their own — how long until they are, inevitably, the biggest chain in the country? The calculation took ten minutes. **Five years.** Five years with full nights of sleep, satisfied customers, managers who stay sane — and, as we shall see, with a rhythm of experimentation no rival chain can afford, because every rival's people are too busy saving quarters.

> **In this kind of vision, victories do not disappear. They stop being dreams and become arithmetic.**

Two questions followed, and both belong in this book because you are asking them too.

The first, from a founder: *is this vision now fixed?* No — and it must not be. The vision improves with the company. Every quarter of experiments, multiplications and audit feedback makes you smarter, and next quarter you may add two indicators and retire one; at sixty restaurants, opening two more per quarter may stop mattering at all. I explain it with one question: **would you follow the plan of somebody dumber than you?** Obviously not. Well — if you have spent ninety days experimenting, measuring and learning, then you today are smarter than you were when you wrote the plan. Following it blindly means taking orders from a dumber version of yourself.

The second question: *how long should it take us to reach this ordinary great quarter?* And here is the answer I gave, which is not the answer anybody wants: any deadline you attach to it is arbitrary. Dress it in data all you like — it is a guess wearing a spreadsheet. It will take what it takes. Some blockages will fall in one quarter, after two experiments. Some will take two years and twenty. There is a man I think about whenever somebody demands a schedule for discovery: the mathematician Andrew Wiles, who spent seven years in his attic on a single problem — the most famous in mathematics — with no deadline, no audience and no promises, and later described the work as moving through a dark mansion, six months of bumping into furniture before finding each light switch. Nobody schedules light switches. What you can schedule is the search — and whether you enjoy it. Because if the road itself is good — if you are learning, growing, and not enslaved to your own business — then what, exactly, is the hurry?

---

### 6

Then we filled in the reality: last quarter's numbers, this quarter's numbers, and the gap — the distance between what the machine produces today and the sheet we had just written.

And then I did one more thing, with a pen, that changed the temperature of the day. Next to this quarter's output I drew an extra column, wrote **SAVED** at the top of it, and asked one question for each row: *did this number happen by itself, or did somebody rescue it?* We went down the sheet ticking, and the group did the ticking, not me.

**Ninety-three percent of the rows got a tick.**

| 5 | Min. profit / mature restaurant | €70K | €75K | ✅ | €100K | €25K |
| 6 | New restaurant reaches standards | 8 mo. | 7.5 mo. | ✅ | max. 6 mo. | 1.5 mo. |
| 7 | Sales growth / mature restaurant | 3.2% | 3.5% | ✅ | 3–5% | 0 |
| 8 | Food waste | 6.3% | 5.7% | ✅ | max. 3% | 2.7% |
| 9 | Average Google rating | 4.8 | 4.8 | ❌ | 4.2–5 | 0 |

| 15 | Worker turnover | 14% | 17% | ✅ | max. 10% | 7% |

Read that sheet the way they read it, because it rearranged everything the morning had celebrated.

Row one: two restaurants opened, gap zero — and a tick, because the number exists thanks to a façade rebuilt at dawn and a supplier charmed at dinner. Row three: ninety days, exactly at standard — and a tick. Row thirteen: two managers on the bench — a tick, bought with HR's weekends and a poaching raid. **A gap of zero with a tick in the saved column is not a process at Level 2. It is a rescue wearing a process's clothes** — and next quarter it must all be rescued again, by the same people, at the same personal price. The machine, in truth, produces less than this sheet shows; much of what the sheet shows was produced by seven exhausted humans, by hand.

And one row had no tick at all. The Google rating: 4.8, quarter after quarter, gap zero, saved by nobody. Their food and their service genuinely ran at standard without anyone bleeding for it — the one corner of the company that was already a machine. It is worth pausing on how that feels from inside: nobody had ever been applauded for the rating. There was nothing to applaud. It simply happened, every day, which is exactly the point of this entire book, and exactly why meetings built on applause never notice it.

Two more things about this sheet, and they are load-bearing.

First: this vision is not an objective with better manners, and the difference is not philosophical. Nobody will stand up next quarter and be judged against these numbers. Nobody's bonus hangs on row eight. The sheet describes the *default output* the machine should produce — and so the gaps are not somebody's failures-in-waiting. The output of a quarter does not tell you how good your people were. **It tells you what your machine can currently produce.**

> **A gap is not a bad sign about a person. It is information about the machine. The moment you decide a person is the reason for a shortfall, you have said — without noticing — that this person matters more than the machine. You have made them a hero.**

Find instead that a process is the reason, and the power returns to the thing the company actually owns, and can actually change.

Second: I asked them to look at their business the way I had learned — slowly, expensively — to look at mine: **as a puzzle, not as a crisis.** Some pieces are in place: the rating, HR's induction method, the development director's thirteen openings of experience. Some pieces exist and need multiplying. Many are still missing and need to be discovered. Their old vision — the biggest chain in the country, wanted hard, on a deadline, with no validated method to get there — was precisely the kind of objective the morning had proven doesn't work, scaled up to the size of the whole company: a permanent, five-year-old crisis. And a crisis demands anxiety, while a puzzle demands curiosity. People can live inside curiosity for years, and they are good years. Inside anxiety, people do not solve the problem — they look for the nearest exit from the pain, and the nearest exit is usually a toxic simplification: blame the colleague, blame the competitor, blame the state, blame the market. I told them how many years I had run my own life exactly that way, turning every hard thing I touched into a crisis with a deadline, and what it had cost me before I noticed. The nodding this time was not polite.

---

### 7

One sheet remained for day one: the machine itself.

We took the machine worksheet from the fourth chapter and inventoried every process this company needs in order to run — all of it, from finding and evaluating locations, negotiating leases, fitting out and equipping sites, through purchasing, goods receiving, stock and transfers, kitchen production and portioning, quality control, service, hygiene, maintenance and the daily open-and-close of each restaurant, to recruiting and inducting workers and managers, scheduling, evaluation and retention, local marketing, the loyalty programme, new-product development, budgeting, per-location reporting and supplier contracts. **Thirty-four processes.**

Then we asked, for each one, the only question that matters: at what level is it validated? Worked once through a hero — or works repeatedly, documented, measured, without rescue?

**Twenty-seven percent were at Level 2. Eight percent were at Level 3. Everything else — sixty-five percent of the machine — was running on rescue.** Sixty-five percent of this company's results, every quarter, depended on somebody saving them. The founders stared at the two sheets side by side for a long moment, and one of them said the sentence I had been waiting three hours for: *we have not been building a company. We have been building a place that turns people into heroes, and trains them.*

There was a second realisation hiding in the same numbers, and I gave it to them because they would need it the next morning. For five years, their quarterly meetings had run on opinions — eloquent, passionate, senior opinions — because opinions were all there was. Where processes are not validated and measured, nobody can point at a mechanism, so everybody points at impressions, and the meeting belongs to whoever argues best.

> **A meeting without validated processes is not an audit. It is a debate, and the best talker wins.**

Here is the part I did not predict, though I should have. Looking at a page that said sixty-five percent of your company needs rescuing every ninety days, this team was not crushed. They were, for the first time in years, visibly relieved. They had a map. They could see the pieces that were placed, the pieces that needed copying, the pieces that were missing — and they could see that the exhaustion of the last five years had not been a character flaw or a market condition but an architecture, and architecture can be changed. One of the calmest illuminations I have ever watched arrive in a group of people, and it arrived from two filled-in worksheets.

---

### 8

I closed day one with an assignment that made them groan.

*Tomorrow morning, plan the next quarter your way. The old way. Set your growth objectives exactly as you have for five years. Then, after lunch, we will plan the same quarter again — with the audit.*

They pushed back, and their reasons were flattering: they were convinced, they said; the new system obviously made more sense; why burn half a day on theatre? I held the line, and I want to give you the reason, because it is the same reason this chapter is a story instead of a manual. If I had walked in on day one and presented the new system to polite applause, they would have agreed with everything and understood nothing. It was watching their own meeting — and then having it named — that made the understanding stick. Learning science has caught up with this: researchers call it *productive failure*, and a meta-analysis of fifty-three studies found that people who wrestle with a problem first and receive the theory second understand it more deeply, and transfer it further, than people taught the theory first — even though, during the wrestling, they fail. The struggle is not the price of the lesson. It is the lesson. Tomorrow morning was not wasted time; it was the second half of their education, and I told them to run the same play with their own teams when they got home.

That evening we grilled, told stories, and did not talk about business. And the next morning at eight o'clock sharp, every single person was in the living room of the guesthouse — fed, finished. One of the founders leaned over to me: *I have run this meeting for five years. There has never been a morning when everybody was ready at eight.*

---

### 9

The old-style planning took two hours, and it was a masterclass — not in planning, but in everything this book has tried to show you about what objectives do to excellent people. I had hidden the two completed worksheets overnight, and I interrupted only to insist they not use them: *if you have never planned by looking at the machine, do not start this morning.*

The development director committed to opening **three** locations next quarter. The HR director committed to recruiting **twenty-five workers and three managers**. The marketing director committed — again — to **twenty percent** sales growth. The operations director committed to cutting waste by **one percentage point**. And the finance director, objective-less no longer, committed to raising **four million euros** of bank financing for the next eight restaurants.

Then, before we replanned, I asked each of them one question.

To the development director: *why three?* Because he could, he said. If he pulls hard, maybe even more. I reminded him what two had cost — the sleepless nights, the weekends, an opening that landed on the last day of the quarter. He smiled and said that this is the price of performance, and he is willing to pay it. I did not argue. But I will tell you what I was thinking, because it is the whole second chapter of this book sitting in one chair: a man whose worth is only visible to himself when he is doing the impossible, sacrificing his evenings and probably his family to prove to everyone that he matters. There is nothing to criticise in him. There is everything to criticise in what he was shaped by — an entire culture's agreement that this is what a valuable person looks like.

To the HR director: *how sure are you of the twenty-five and three?* Ninety-nine percent, she said. *Have you ever recruited that many in one quarter?* No — but she knows what she can do, and if necessary she will sleep at the office. I asked the question underneath: why is that sacrifice needed at all? What would happen if she simply said *not yet* — committed to what she can certainly deliver, and built a validated method for bigger numbers first? She said she could not disappoint her colleagues; they were counting on her; anything less would be letting the team down. So I turned to the founders: *would you be disappointed?* And the founders said no — that they would honestly prefer slower growth with less stress and guaranteed quality. There was a silence with a specific texture after that. A management professor named Jerry Harvey once described a family in Texas driving fifty-three miles through hundred-degree heat to eat a terrible cafeteria dinner in Abilene, because each of them privately believed the others wanted to go; only back home did they discover that nobody had wanted to go, including the father-in-law who proposed it. Harvey called it the Abilene paradox: a group marching in agreement toward a destination that no individual member actually wants. This company had been driving to Abilene, at seventy-eight percent a year, for five years — every director sacrificing to meet an expectation the founders did not hold, and the founders applauding sacrifices they did not want, because that is what everyone assumed everyone wanted.

To the marketing director: *why twenty percent again, when it was just missed?* The first answers were rationalisations, and I kept asking, and what finally came out hit the founders harder than anything else in the three days: **shame.** Committing to the big number again would show them how much she cared. Delivering it would wash away last quarter. And here I told a story on myself, because she had earned the company of it. Every time in my life I gained twenty kilograms, I did the same thing she had just done: I set a drastic, public objective to lose it. I never audited the machine that had made me heavy — the habits, the emotions, the ordinary Tuesdays — because that inquiry was slow and painful. The objective was neither. For a few weeks, a public objective works like absolution: it washes the shame, spectacularly, in advance. I call it **therapy by objectives**, and I want to be careful here — there is no cause for embarrassment in it. It is a survival mechanism, and in the world as currently arranged, a rational one. But it means the number was never chosen because it was achievable or wise. It was chosen for its emotional dosage. And science has now photographed the price tag: a sports psychologist named Geir Jordet spent two decades analysing penalty shoot-outs, kick by kick, and found that the superstars — the players carrying awards and status — miss more often than their unheralded teammates, rushing their run-ups and avoiding the goalkeeper's eyes, the body language of somebody trying to exit a situation rather than solve a problem. The heavier the identity attached to an outcome, the worse the outcome gets. She had just attached her whole standing in the company to a number nobody knows the method for.

And to the operations director: *why one percentage point?* Because, he said plainly, he does not believe more is possible — he had seen what one point cost him. He would accept the objective, in his own words, *"I will try, but I do not believe."* There it is, the other exit. After a failed objective, people take one of two roads: double the bet, and use the new objective as therapy for the old one — or shrink the ambition to something that cannot hurt them again, because they have stopped believing. Notice that neither road has anything to do with what is actually possible; both are managed doses of feeling. This is precisely why the system you are about to watch runs on experiments instead. An experiment does not care what anybody believes, it cannot kill anybody's self-esteem, and it never asks a person to bet their dignity on a guess. It just asks reality a question — and reality, unlike the meeting, answers.

### 10

After lunch, I handed back the two worksheets, and we planned the same quarter a second time.

The instrument for this is the third sheet of paper — the audit worksheet — and it works like this. Every gap from the vision sheet becomes a row. For each row, the team identifies the **bottleneck**: the thing in the machine that is currently blocking that output. Then it names the **processes** where that bottleneck lives, so everybody knows where to look. Then it marks one of two solutions. That is the entire sheet.

By late afternoon the sheet was full. Here it is, compressed — and read the bottleneck column as the answer to a single question: *where does a hero currently stand?*

| # | Gap | Bottleneck (in the machine) | Solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Openings — saved | Equipment delivery guaranteed by personal charm, not contract; no backup supplier | **Discovery** |
| 2 | Budget +8% | Site changes agreed verbally, no budget checkpoint; overruns surface at the final invoice | **Discovery** |
| 3 | 90 days — saved | Works accepted only once, at the end; defects surface the night before opening | **Discovery** |
| 4 | 7 rescues vs 0 | Stock transfers decided and executed personally by the ops director — only he sees all locations' stock | **Discovery** |
| 5 | Profit −€25K | No comparable per-location P&L; nobody can say where weak locations lose their margin | **Discovery** |
| 6 | Standards in 7.5 vs 6 mo. | New teams start with zero carriers of the standard — nobody transfers from mature locations | **Multiplication** |
| 7 | Growth — saved | Growth bought by ad-hoc budget pushes; no standing local marketing plan per location | **Discovery** |
| 8 | Waste 5.7% vs 3% | Portioning unverified at volume — eyeballing, undetected except by heroic surveillance | **Discovery** |
| 11 | 1 product vs 2–4 | No pilot location, no pre-set test criteria — every new product is a full-network gamble | **Discovery** |
| 12 | 20% vs 25% regulars | Loyalty database never used — no message, no offer has ever gone to existing customers | **Discovery** |
| 13 | Bench — saved | Manager training starts only when a need erupts; no standing internal pipeline | **Multiplication** |
| 14 | Retention 85% vs 90% | Departure risk discovered at resignation — no retention conversations exist | **Discovery** |
| 15 | Turnover 17% vs 10% | Onboarding gets compressed or skipped under opening pressure — exactly when people decide to stay or leave | **Multiplication** |

But the power is in three rules we enforced while filling it, and the first is the one I had to repeat all afternoon. **The bottleneck must be found in the process, not in the person.** Again and again the discussion tried to slide toward who was to blame — who had not done their job well enough, who lacked follow-through. It is the easiest road, and it changes nothing. The guiding question is not *why did Mark or Catherine fail to deliver* — it is: **what is actually blocking the machine from producing the output in the vision?** The first question always finds a culprit. Only the second one finds a mechanism.

The second rule: **rows with a tick in the saved column count as gaps, even when the gap is zero.** Two restaurants opened is the vision's number — but the bottleneck there is not the result, it is the way the result was produced: the exact places where a founder or director had to intervene with extraordinary action, unpaid nights, or experience nobody else in the company holds. A result that needed rescue is a sign of unfinished architecture, and the audit's job is to find the unfinished part.

And the third rule emerged from the best question of the afternoon. Somewhere in the middle of the sheet, the marketing director looked at the accumulating rows and asked, with visible dread: *so we have to solve all of these gaps this quarter?*

**Fuck, no.**

The road to the vision might be four quarters or forty. This quarter, we solve *the* bottleneck of each gap — the one currently in front — and only that. Next quarter, the audit runs again, reality reports back, and whatever blocks the gap *then* gets solved then. Like a puzzle: you find one piece that fits, you place it in the machine, and then you look for the next one. Nobody solves a puzzle by grabbing all thousand pieces at once — that was, we now understood, the old system's exact method, and we had spent yesterday morning listening to what it does to people.

Let me show you what one row of this actually sounded like, because the sheet hides the work. The waste row: gap 2.7 points. The first proposed bottleneck was, of course, a person — *the cooks don't care, they need discipline*, and the operations director had four days of yelling to prove it. So we asked the question properly: what is blocking the *machine* from producing three percent waste? Follow the process: there is a recipe book with exact gramajes — so the standard exists. Do the cooks know it? Yes. So where does it break? At volume. At rush hour, nobody weighs; they eyeball, and the eyeball rounds up, fifty grams at a time, thousands of plates a week. And who verifies portions, on an ordinary Tuesday, when no director is standing in the kitchen? Silence. Nobody. There was the bottleneck, and notice what it is not: it is not lazy cooks. **The portioning process has no verification step** — the fifth chapter's oldest law, *what is not verified does not happen*, wearing an apron. The only time it had ever been verified, waste dropped — that was the director's heroic four days — which proves the mechanism and proves that a human sacrifice is currently standing where a process step should be.

Which brings us back to the sheet's final column — the audit's verdict — where there are only two possible entries, because the fourth chapter left the machine only two moves. For every bottleneck, one question: **does a validated solution for this already exist somewhere in the company?** If yes — multiply it. Do not reinvent a wheel you own. If no — discover: form hypotheses, run experiments, and find out. That's it. That is the entire strategic decision of a quarter, and it fits in one column.

Look at the three multiplication rows, because they teach the rule better than any definition. Row six: the standard the new restaurants take seven and a half months to reach *already exists* — the mature locations produce it daily. The solution is not to invent anything; it is to transfer two or three experienced people into every new opening, carriers of the standard, the way the fifth chapter showed Toyota seeding every new plant from an old one. Row thirteen: HR's method for finding and training managers is genuinely validated — she proved it thirteen times. What is missing is cadence: the method runs only when a crisis summons it. Multiplication here means running the *same validated method* continuously, two managers always in training, crisis or no crisis. Multiplication, notice, is not only about geography. It is any case of *more of what already works*. Row fifteen is its twin: the induction that produces workers who perform at standard exists — it gets skipped under pressure; the multiplication is dedicated training capacity so the validated process runs every time instead of when there is a free weekend.

And then the objection that completes the lesson — it came, of course, from the development director. *Then let's multiply my opening process. It is validated thirteen times.* It is the perfect question. Thirteen successes — and every one of them through the same man, his relationships, his dinners, his dawns. That is not a Level 2 process; it is a Level 1 result achieved thirteen times, which is precisely what his own morning presentation had claimed as a badge of honour: *superbly tuned, if I run it.* **You may only multiply what works without you.** So his openings went where they truly belonged — into discovery, one experiment at a time, until the process could survive his absence — and the man who the day before had argued that his talent could never be written down spent the rest of the afternoon, to everyone's quiet astonishment, arguing about which piece of it to extract first.

Somewhere in there, one of the founders raised the question that every founder raises at exactly this point: *and the bonuses? No objectives, no bonuses?* The answer is that bonuses survive intact — they are paid for running the experiment or the multiplication as designed, which, unlike hitting a number nobody has a method for, is entirely within a person's power. *But then I'm paying just for that?* No. You are paying for validated information about what works — the most valuable thing a company can buy. When you pay a doctor to find out why you cannot sleep, you first pay for the analyses — that is the audit, checking everything already known about how the body should run. And when the analyses come back clean and the answer is still missing, the doctor does not set you an objective to sleep better by Friday. He runs experiments: melatonin; magnesium; no coffee after noon. Each one, whatever its result, is validated information that closes in on the cause — and when the cause is solved, the solution goes into the machine of your evenings. Nobody calls the doctor a failure when the melatonin doesn't work. The melatonin failed. The search advanced.

### 11

We were nearly done when I added one more thing to the plan — a kind of experiment that would never appear on the audit sheet, because it does not come from a gap.

Everything to this point had been driven by the machine's blockages, and that is right and disciplined. But some of the best results of my career have come from experiments with no bottleneck behind them at all — experiments that begin with the words *I wonder what would happen if…* Pure curiosity, given a worksheet.

The best example I own is one this team already knew, because it is how the founders and I met. Years ago, after a long time in the trenches of other people's companies, I started an education company. It was never my main business. It was the thing I wanted to leave behind: a way to hand a real system to the entrepreneurs who were fighting their way up without one, the way I had watched hundreds fight. We marketed it the way experience says you market education — press, webinars, newsletters — and it worked exactly the way experience promised it would. Nothing was broken. There was no gap, no bottleneck, no problem demanding a solution. There was only a question that would not leave me alone: *I wonder what would happen if we gave everything away — taught the entire methodology, live, free, ninety minutes every morning for thirty days — and then asked people to buy the same information, packaged as a course?* It made no commercial sense, which is exactly why I could not stop thinking about it. We called it **30 Days MBA**. Everyone including me expected five viewers by day four — and as an objective, carrying a sales target, the idea would have died in any planning meeting on earth, because nobody volunteers to own a number attached to a thirty-day free marathon the whole table expects to flop. As an experiment, it needed nobody's faith: a hypothesis, a handful of metrics, a start date. The first run, close to two thousand companies registered and eleven hundred were still watching on the last morning — and then six hundred of them bought the course teaching the material they had just watched me give away, which made no rational sense and was therefore the most valuable information that company has ever produced. We did not scale it; it had worked *once*, and Level 1 is not a machine — so we ran it again the next year, deliberately, to see whether the result was the method or the weather. Eight thousand registered. It went into the machine, it has run every year since, more than twenty thousand companies have passed through it, and it remains that company's single biggest growth engine. The founders sitting in front of me had watched it three times. They were, themselves, the result of a question that no gap analysis would ever have asked.

So we went around the table one last time, each person finishing the sentence *I wonder what would happen if…* — and two hypotheses made the quarter's list. From the operations side: *what would happen if we cut half the menu and kept only the top-selling fifty percent?* — a question the waste and complexity numbers had been whispering for years, which nobody had ever dared to phrase, because under the old system a shrinking menu sounds like shrinking ambition. And from marketing, suddenly playing offence instead of defence: *what would happen if we opened co-branded locations with influencers — their name on the restaurant beside ours, two or three of their favourite dishes served only there — in the cities where their fan base is enormous?* Ninety days earlier, in the old meeting, this same woman had been assembling sixty slides of self-defence. Now she was proposing the most inventive growth idea the company had generated in years, and enjoying it. Nothing about her talent had changed overnight. The question she was answering had.

That was the end of day two. The whole of day three belonged to turning every decision into a project — one worksheet per experiment, one per multiplication, each with its hypothesis or its standard, its budget, its owner, its team and its deadline, and every one of them designed around a single non-negotiable property: **everything the owner commits to sits one hundred percent within the owner's power.** Nobody left that guesthouse owing the company a result nobody knows how to produce. They left owing it a well-run search, or a well-run copy. How those projects are designed, how they are managed through the quarter, and how their results get folded back into the machine — that is the business of the next two chapters.

### 12

I want to tell you how the three days ended, because I have run and witnessed something like a thousand quarterly meetings, and I had never seen this before.

They did not want to leave. The directors stood around the two worksheets taped to the wall, arguing — cheerfully — about their experiments: what the menu test might show, whether the influencer locations would work in the second city or only the first. The people with multiplications to run were the calmest people in the house: for once, the quarter ahead of them consisted of doing something they already knew how to do, properly, instead of promising something they didn't. When I asked for feedback at the end, the answer that came back, in several versions, was the one I have since heard from many companies: *this was the most pleasant quarterly meeting we have ever had — and we cannot wait for the next one, to find out what the experiments say.* Read that twice. They were not eager to report. They were eager to *find out*. Somewhere in two days, the meeting had stopped being a courtroom and become the place where the company learns what is true — and the difference between dreading a verdict and awaiting an answer is the entire subject of this book.

One director — the operations man, of all people, the one who *takes the NO in his arms* — said something worth keeping. *If the menu experiment works,* he said, *implementation will be easy. My people never resist what has been validated here, in our company, on our numbers.* And I stopped him, because he had just handed me my own biggest mistake, gift-wrapped. For years I implemented changes by force — by authority, by charisma, by insisting people trust me despite my rich personal history of being wrong. I read every resistance as stubbornness. It took me embarrassingly long to see that my people were never against me, or against my ideas. They were against *unvalidated change* that could make their lives worse, imposed by a man asking for faith instead of showing evidence. Run the experiment in public, show the result, and resistance mostly evaporates — not because people became agreeable, but because you finally gave them what they were always entitled to: proof. The best method of learning is experimentation, for a company as for a person — and a person who has watched the evidence assemble does not need to be converted. They were in the laboratory when it happened.

The audit, then — the whole of it, distilled. Once a quarter: read the vision sheet against reality and mark the gaps, *and the rescues*. For each, find the bottleneck in the machine, never in the person. Route each bottleneck one of exactly two ways — multiply what is validated, discover what is missing — and add whatever pure curiosity proposes. Give every decision an owner, a team and a deadline. Then go and run the quarter, and let reality report back. It is four sheets of paper, and it produces no winners and no losers — only, each quarter, a company that knows more than it did ninety days ago and a machine with one more piece in place.

Five years of celebrated heroism had built that company a treadmill. Two days of audit handed them a puzzle. The machine did not improve overnight — sixty-five percent of it still needed rescuing on the morning they drove home. What changed overnight was the question the company asks itself every ninety days. The old question — *who won, who lost?* — had been answered four times a year, loudly, with applause and shame, and had produced not one validated method in five years. The new question — *what is blocking the machine, and how do we find out?* — cannot be answered with applause at all. Only with pieces.

I cannot resist telling you how the quarter itself turned out, because the team could not resist telling me. The menu experiment ran in one stable location: cutting half the menu cost eight percent of sales — and raised that location's profitability by fifteen. The influencer experiment was run on an existing restaurant that had sat below the chain's profitability standard for more than two years. It was rebranded in a month — the influencer's name on the front beside theirs, his favourite dishes served only there, thirty percent of the restaurant his once the costs were recovered — and in its second month after launch, it stood in the top three restaurants of the entire chain by sales. Whether it holds, nobody yet knows. That is why the experiment does not close until another quarter has passed.

And precisely because those results are spectacular, I owe you a warning before this chapter ends. **Results like these are not typical.** There are quarters in which one hundred percent of your experiments invalidate their hypotheses — and that is a good quarter too, because every invalidated hypothesis is time, money and attention you will never again spend maintaining a dead idea, and a push toward the solutions you have not yet tried. I say this because people fall in love with their own experiments, and a person in love starts wanting to *make* the experiment work. Watch what has happened in that moment: the experiment has quietly turned back into an objective, and its owner is once again forcing reality, through heroism, to look the way he wants it to look. An experiment exists to deliver real information — nothing else. There is no fail in an experiment, and there is no win. There is only information, and it goes to one of two places: into the machine, or into the next experiment.
