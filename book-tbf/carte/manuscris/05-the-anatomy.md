# 5. The Anatomy

### The Tennis Court, the Red Beads, and What Is Not Verified

---

### 1

In 1948, in San Bernardino, California, two brothers closed a restaurant that was making money, in order to fix it.

Richard and Maurice McDonald ran a drive-in on Route 66. It worked. It had carhops, a menu of twenty-five items, china plates and metal cutlery. It also had every problem every restaurant has ever had: orders came back wrong, food took time, teenagers loitered in the car park and drove away paying customers, plates broke, cutlery walked out the door, and the labour cost of doing all of it properly was crushing.

The ordinary way to improve a restaurant is to find better staff. The McDonalds looked at their own receipts instead, and noticed that eighty percent of their revenue came from hamburgers.

So they shut down. They cut the menu from twenty-five items to nine. They replaced china with paper, eliminated the carhops, eliminated cutlery.

And then they did the thing this chapter is about.

They went to a tennis court behind Richard's house, and with a piece of chalk they drew the kitchen on the ground. Full size. Every station — the grill, the fry area, the drinks, the dressing table, the service window.

Then they brought their staff out onto the court and made them cook an imaginary lunch rush. No food. No equipment. Just the motions, in real time: pick up the imaginary patty, turn, place it here, two steps left, dress it there, hand it through.

And they watched where people collided.

Every time two employees crossed paths, that was a defect in the layout. Every time somebody had to turn around, that was wasted seconds, multiplied by the number of burgers in a day. Every time somebody had to wait, there was a bottleneck. So they rubbed out the chalk and redrew the kitchen, and ran it again. Richard McDonald later described a rain shower washing the whole thing away and forcing them to start over.

Now look at what they were actually doing out there, in the language of the last chapter.

The kitchen layout was, at that moment, a **Level 0 hypothesis**. They had an idea about how a kitchen should be arranged, drawn from experience and from staring at their own numbers. They had no evidence.

What they built on that tennis court was the cheapest possible experiment. Chalk, on a public court, with imaginary hamburgers. Every collision they found there is a collision they would otherwise have paid for in stainless steel, in floor space, and in wages — for the next twenty years.

They stayed on the court until it worked. And what they carried back was no longer a hypothesis. It was a specified path, ready to be built and then run thousands of times.

That is the moment where the unknown becomes known, and it is the whole subject of this chapter. Not the idea. The conversion.

Because the Speedee Service System, when it finally opened, produced fifteen-cent hamburgers in seconds, at consistent quality, made by teenagers with no cooking skill whatsoever.

The skill had been taken out of the people and put into the layout.

### 2

Start with the smallest unit, because everything else is built from it.

**A process is not a list of tasks.** It is a repeatable path from an input to an output.

That distinction sounds academic until you have read a few hundred documents that companies call processes and that are, in fact, lists of tasks — things somebody does, written in the order somebody remembered them, with no trigger, no owner, no measurement and no definition of finished.

A process description is useful when it answers eight questions:

> What triggers the process?
> What input is required?
> What sequence is followed?
> Who owns each step?
> What output must be produced?
> Which indicators reveal whether it is working?
> What exceptions are allowed?
> What happens when the standard is missed?

Most companies answer two of those and wonder why writing it down changed nothing.

And now watch what happens when somebody answers all eight for a piece of work that everybody agreed was already being done well.

Two doctors at Great Ormond Street Hospital for Children in London sat down after a long day of paediatric cardiac surgery, exhausted, and turned on the television.

Their problem was one of the most dangerous twenty minutes in the hospital. When a child comes out of open-heart surgery, they are moved to intensive care — an unconscious, unstable child, with a ventilator, drips, drains, monitoring lines and an entire clinical history, transferred from one team to another, through a corridor, with equipment being unplugged and replugged in a specific order.

It was, in their own description, chaotic. Noisy. Nobody obviously in charge. Everybody talking at once. Equipment disconnected in the wrong sequence. Information dropped.

And here is the part that matters. Every single person involved was excellent, and every single person was trying. This was not a hospital with a staffing problem. Alan Goldman and Martin Elliott knew that a disproportionate share of their errors happened inside that window, and they had no idea what to do about it, because the obvious levers — better people, more care, more attention — were already at maximum.

On the television was a Formula One race, and what they were watching was a pit stop.

A car arrives at high speed and stops on a mark. Around twenty people converge on it. Every one of them has one job and touches nothing else. Nobody speaks. It takes seconds. Then the car leaves.

They looked at that and recognized their own problem wearing different clothes: a machine arriving in an unstable state, a large team, a very short window, a rigid sequence, and catastrophic consequences for error.

So they wrote to Ferrari.

Ferrari said yes. The doctors filmed their handovers and sent the tapes to Maranello, where the technical director and the race crew watched footage of children being wheeled out of operating theatres. Then the hospital team went to Italy and stood in the pit lane.

What Ferrari gave them was not medicine. It was the eight questions, answered.

*Who owns each step* — one person is explicitly in charge, and it is not the most senior person in the room. In the pit lane it is the man with the lollipop. At Great Ormond Street it became the anaesthetist, whose only job during the handover is to **run** it rather than to do a task.

*What sequence is followed* — fixed, and rehearsed, and the same every time.

*What exceptions are allowed* — a phase in which nobody speaks except the person leading, because in the pit lane chatter is how you lose a wheel.

*What output must be produced, and when is it finished* — a checklist read aloud, and a defined moment at which responsibility formally transfers from one team to the other.

And one structural decision that came out of the analysis and that nobody in the hospital had thought of: the handover was split into a quiet **technical** phase, moving lines and machines, followed by a separate **information** phase. Doing both at once was where the losses were coming from.

Published results showed technical and information errors each falling from around thirty percent of handovers to about ten.

Put that on the ladder, because it is the whole lesson.

Before Ferrari, the handover was at **Level 1**. It worked — often. It worked when the right people were on shift, when nobody was tired, when the corridor was quiet. Nobody had ever specified it, so its success was a property of the people rather than of the process, and its failures looked like bad luck.

After Ferrari, it was **Level 2**. Same hospital. Same doctors. Same children.

> **Ninety percent of what you need has already been solved perfectly by an industry that is not yours. The innovation is noticing that their problem is structurally your problem.**

### 3

Now the part that almost nobody builds, and without which none of the above survives a month.

A process alone is inert. It is a description of how work should go. For it to become part of a machine, four more things have to be attached to it, and they have to be attached in a specific order — because each one is derived from the one before it.

> **Process → Indicator → Standard → Role → Responsibility**

Let me walk it with something concrete, because in the abstract it sounds like paperwork and in practice it is the difference between a company that works and a company that argues.

**The process.** A business sells a service to other businesses. The path runs: identify a company that fits the profile · reach the right person · have a qualifying conversation · send a proposal · follow up · close. Six steps, a defined input at the top and a signed contract at the bottom.

**The indicators.** Now you measure everything the process does. Companies contacted. People reached. Conversations held. Conversations that qualified. Proposals sent. Proposals answered. Contracts signed. Average contract value. Time from first contact to signature.

Note the word *everything*. Not the outcome. Companies routinely measure only the last number in the chain — revenue closed — and then wonder why a bad month is impossible to diagnose. If you measure only the end of the process, a failure anywhere along it looks identical, and every conversation about it becomes a conversation about attitude.

**The standards.** Some of those indicators need a line. Not all of them. A standard says: *this is what has to be true for the process to produce what the vision says it should produce.*

And here the arithmetic runs backwards, which is the part people find surprising. You do not start from how many people you have. You start from the output you need.

If the Ordinary Great Quarter requires forty signed contracts, and the process converts roughly one proposal in four, you need a hundred and sixty proposals. If one qualifying conversation in three produces a proposal, you need around five hundred conversations. If one call in ten produces a conversation, you need five thousand calls in the quarter, which is roughly a hundred a day.

A hundred calls a day is now a standard. It was not chosen because it sounds ambitious. It was derived.

**The roles.** And now the number of people is also derived, rather than negotiated. If one person can sustainably make twenty-five calls a day alongside the rest of the work, you need four people. Not three, because three will not reach the standard and everybody will spend the quarter being disappointed in them. Not six, because six is a cost you have not earned.

> **A role is a requirement of the system. The system tells you how many you need, and what each one has to produce — before anybody has been hired, promoted, or given a title.**

**The responsibilities.** And finally, the thing that turns a role into something a human being can be held to.

A responsibility is an indicator, with a standard, **that is within that person's power.**

That last clause is the whole thing, and it is where almost every performance conversation in the world goes wrong.

Twenty-five calls a day is within the salesperson's power. They can decide to make the calls. The close rate on leads that marketing generated is not — or is only partly, and the part that is not will poison every review they ever have.

> **Responsibilities are the results you are actually paying that salary for. If a person cannot control it, you are not paying for it. You are hoping for it, and holding them accountable for the hope.**

### 4

To see what happens when you get that last part wrong, watch an eighty-year-old man run a factory in front of a room full of executives who have paid a great deal of money to be there.

W. Edwards Deming would bring out a plastic box containing four thousand small beads. Eight hundred of them red, three thousand two hundred white. And a paddle with fifty holes in it.

Then he would hire from the audience. Six Willing Workers, two Inspectors, a Chief Inspector, a Recorder. He would explain, with complete seriousness, that this was a demanding job with strict standards, that there were people waiting for these positions, and that the customer accepts only white beads.

The job was simple. Stir the beads. Dip the paddle. Lift it out. You now have fifty beads. The red ones are defects. The standard is no more than three.

Then he ran the factory exactly the way real factories are run.

Worker one dips: sixteen red. *Sixteen. That is unacceptable.* Worker two: nine. *Excellent — everyone, watch how she does it.* Worker three: fourteen. *What is going on? We are all using the same paddle.*

Day two, the good worker gets worse and the bad worker gets better. Deming puts up a chart. He introduces merit pay. He puts the best performer on the wall and warns the worst. He gives a speech about commitment. Management announces a stretch goal of two red beads.

By day four he is laying off the three worst performers and telling the remaining three to work double shifts, because in his experience the best workers can carry the load.

The numbers never change. They cannot change. There are eight hundred red beads in the box and fifty holes in the paddle, and no amount of effort, incentive, fear or praise alters the ratio of red beads in a plastic container.

The workers did not choose the beads. They did not design the paddle. They did not set the standard. They are, in Deming's phrase, *willing* — and their willingness is irrelevant.

His summary was blunt: the overwhelming majority of variation in an organization's outcomes comes from the system, not from the people in it. His own figure was ninety-four percent.

Now notice the specific mistake, because it is more precise than "management is unfair."

Every one of Deming's interventions is a reasonable management action. Praising the top performer is reasonable. Coaching the bottom one is reasonable. Setting a stretch target is reasonable. Merit pay is reasonable.

The error is upstream of all of it. **Somebody made red beads a responsibility of a person who had no control over red beads.** Everything downstream — the praise, the warning, the target, the dismissal — was noise applied with total sincerity to a system nobody had looked at.

And the human cost is not zero. The worker who pulled sixteen was publicly told they were failing. They will believe it. They will try harder. They will feel worse. And next month they will pull eleven and be congratulated for an improvement they did not cause.

Which is why the sequence in the last section runs in that order and not in any other. **Process first, then role, then person.**

Design the company around the strengths and weaknesses of the people you happen to employ and your architecture becomes a record of your hiring history. That is tolerable in the first year. If it becomes permanent, you have a company shaped like an accident.

And it produces the confusion that damages people most: loyalty to a person mistaken for loyalty to the machine. A good person can be in the wrong role. Somebody outstanding in one context can struggle in another, and that is not a moral event. Changing a role is not a judgment about human worth. It is an attempt to align a capability with what the system needs.

### 5

So how does a process actually climb from a hypothesis to something you would let into the machine?

The sequence is short enough to memorize, and almost nobody runs it:

> **Hypothesis → Experiment → Level 1 → Repeat the success → Standardize → Level 2**

At the start, everything is a hypothesis. You draw the processes from market research, from what you have seen work elsewhere, from advice, from the experience of the people you have hired. All of it is Level 0. None of it is knowledge, however confident anybody sounds.

Then each hypothesis goes through an experiment designed to answer one question: can this work here, at all? A tennis court with chalk on it. A test with two hundred customers instead of twenty thousand. Something cheap enough that being wrong costs a fortnight.

What survives is Level 1. It worked. Once.

And now comes the transition the last chapter was about, and the discipline is entirely in the word *repeat*. You run it again, deliberately, under conditions that vary — a different customer, a different month, a different person. Not to celebrate it. To find out whether the first result was the method or the weather.

Only then do you **standardize** — which means writing down what is supposed to happen, in enough detail that the repetition belongs to the process rather than to the people who happened to be there.

And only then does it enter the machine.

For the hypotheses that never make it from Level 0 to Level 1 — and there will be many, because nine out of ten ideas do not work — you do not push harder. You run a different experiment. That is not failure. That is the discovery loop doing exactly what it exists to do, at a cost of a fortnight rather than a year.

### 6

Level 3 is the last rung, and it has a specific definition that is easy to state and hard to earn.

**A Level 3 process is one you have multiplied at least once, and for which you now have a validated multiplication process.** Meaning: the next time you want to copy it, you know exactly what to do.

Multiplication, done properly, is a sequence. You have the blueprint. You recruit the right people against it. You induct them and you train them. And within a defined period, the new team delivers at the standard of the team it was copied from.

Which raises the practical question, and this is where most companies get it wrong in an interesting way. Do you build the new team from scratch?

No. And the reason is worth understanding, because it is not obvious.

**You take a few people out of the existing team and move them into the new one.**

Those people already live the standard. They do not have to be taught what good looks like, because they have been producing it. They carry the parts of the process that never made it into the document — and there are always parts that never made it into the document, no matter how carefully it was written. That is the fastest and most reliable transfer mechanism there is.

And now the part that makes this elegant rather than merely practical.

The team you took them from does not collapse. It recruits and replaces them — **which is itself a test.** If that team can lose two people and return to standard, its processes really are at Level 2, and you have just proved it at no extra cost. If it cannot, you have learned something enormously important before you found out the expensive way.

Meanwhile the new team does not start from zero with a room full of people who have never seen the standard.

This has a name in manufacturing. Toyota runs what it calls a **mother plant** system: each new factory outside Japan is assigned an established plant that supports it, sends people to it, and takes its people in for training. It is not a document handover. It is a transplant with a living graft in it.

Which brings back the difference between Toyota and General Motors from the last chapter, and makes it sharper.

GM copied the andon cord to Van Nuys and dispersed the handful of people who had actually learned the system at Fremont. It sent the artifact and scattered the carriers.

Toyota sends the carriers.

One last thing, and I want to be clear about it because otherwise this reads as a demand that everything be replicable.

**Most of your processes do not need to be Level 3, and trying to get them there is a waste of the only thing you cannot buy more of.** Transferability costs real work — extraction, documentation, training design, testing, a first multiplication that will be slower and worse than the original. Spend it where you intend to multiply. Everything else is perfectly good at Level 2, and a company where every process has been polished for a transfer that will never happen has confused thoroughness with judgement.

---

### 7

So now you have a kitchen.

The processes exist. The indicators are attached. The standards are set and derived, not guessed. The roles are defined by what the system requires, and the responsibilities inside those roles are things a human being can actually control.

Those are the fixed parts. They are the stations on the tennis court after the chalk stopped moving — the arrangement that stays the same whoever walks into it.

But the McDonald brothers did not open a kitchen. They opened a restaurant, and a restaurant requires people standing in the kitchen, doing the work, on a Tuesday, when nobody is watching.

Which requires three more processes, and they are the ones most companies never build at all. A process for evaluating performance. A process for recruiting. A process for training.

The rest of this chapter is those three, and the first one is where almost all the value is.

### 8

I have a saying I have used more often than a parrot asking for a cracker.

**What is not verified does not happen.**

People hear that and assume it is cynical — a statement about human beings being lazy or dishonest. It is not. Let me show you the cleanest evidence I know, and notice who it is about.

Hand hygiene in hospitals is the single most effective infection control measure ever identified. Every doctor and every nurse knows this. It is not disputed, it is not difficult, it takes fifteen seconds, and failing to do it kills people.

So researchers measured how often it actually happens, using different kinds of observers.

When the observers were **covert** — medical students who were not recognized as auditors — compliance ran at around forty-four percent.

When the observers were **recognized** infection control nurses, compliance ran at around seventy-four percent.

When the observers were unit ambassadors, visibly present and known to everybody, it ran at around ninety-four percent.

Same hospital. Same staff. Same procedure. Same day.

Read those three numbers again, because they are not a story about bad people. They are a story about the best-trained professionals our society produces, doing a fifteen-second task they know saves lives, and doing it twice as often when somebody is watching.

If that is true of them, it is true of everybody, and it is certainly true of your company.

> **Verification is not a way of showing you do not trust people. It is one of the few things that reliably makes performance possible — and it is a form of respect, because the alternative is letting somebody fail quietly for six months and then telling them about it in a review.**

There are two kinds of verification, and a well-built machine uses both.

The first is **external**: somebody other than the person doing the work checks that it happened to standard.

The second, and the better one where it is possible, is **self-verification**: the person can see their own numbers, in real time, without waiting for anybody. A machine built properly makes this the default. The salesperson can see their own calls. The technician can see their own resolution times. Nobody has to be told at the end of the month what they could have seen on the Tuesday.

But some verification cannot be automated, and here we arrive at a problem almost every company has and almost none has named.

**Who does the checking?**

In most organizations, the answer is: the manager. And that answer is the beginning of a very expensive spiral.

Look at what the research on span of control actually says. Where work is **standardized and measurable**, a manager can carry ten to twenty direct reports. Where work is **varied and judgement-heavy**, that number falls to six to ten.

Read those two ranges again, because they are the whole argument in numbers.

A company without a machine has unstandardized work. Which means it needs roughly **twice as many managers** to cover the same number of people — not because its managers are weak, but because verifying unspecified work takes far longer than verifying specified work.

So the company adds a layer. And because each layer has to be verified too, it eventually adds another. And here is the bit that shows up in the bank account: **managers cost more than the people they manage.** They have to, or nobody would take the job.

So the money that could have paid the people doing the work goes into layers of supervision instead. Everybody is paid slightly worse, the organization is slightly slower, and the reason is that nobody wrote the processes down.

I want to be careful here, because this is not an argument against managers. Managers make decisions, resolve conflicts, develop people, and take responsibility. Those are real jobs and they justify real salaries.

The problem is that **verification has been bundled into the manager's job, and verification does not require a manager.**

Two industries figured this out a long time ago.

Manufacturing solved it eighty years ago. As production got complex enough that one person could no longer be responsible for every aspect of it, quality inspection emerged as its own function — and by the Second World War, quality control departments were deliberately built **separate from, and independent of, the manufacturing departments**. The reasoning was explicit and it has never been improved on: you cannot ask the person responsible for the output to also be the person who verifies the output. Not because they are dishonest. Because they are inside it.

Aviation solved it too. Airlines have **check pilots** — pilots specifically authorized to evaluate other pilots. A captain's line check is not conducted by his boss. It is conducted by a peer with a particular authorization, and the authorization is about the process rather than about the hierarchy. In the industry where a missed verification kills the most people, verification was deliberately decoupled from the chain of command.

Knowledge work never did this. It just kept hiring bosses.

So here is the role I think most companies are missing, and it costs a fraction of what they are currently paying for the same function.

**The operational controller.** Somebody whose job is to verify that what is supposed to happen is actually happening, to the standard, in the process — and who has no hierarchical authority over the people they are checking.

Now the objection, because you are already having it. *You are telling me to hire people whose entire job is to check other people. That is bureaucracy.*

No. **You are already paying for it.** The verification is happening right now, badly and expensively, buried inside manager salaries where it appears on no line of your P&L. The only question is whether you keep paying for it at manager prices, bundled with an authority the task does not require, or whether you separate it and price it correctly.

And you have met a controller already in this book.

In the second chapter, Peter Pronovost's central line checklist worked — but the thing that made it work was not the list. It was that he obtained authorization for **a nurse to stop a doctor** who skipped a step. Somebody with no hierarchical authority over the person at all, and complete authority over the process.

That was the first operational controller in these pages. It just did not have a name yet.

### 9

Verification is one of three conditions a person needs in order to become genuinely good at something, and the other two are worth knowing precisely, because they change what protecting the machine actually means.

Daniel Kahneman spent much of his career arguing that expert intuition is largely an illusion. Gary Klein spent much of his career studying firefighters and finding that their intuition was real and often astonishing. In 2009 they wrote a paper together in an attempt to work out why they disagreed, and discovered that they mostly did not.

Their conclusion was that the argument had never really been about experts. It was about **environments**.

Skilled intuition develops when two conditions are met. The environment has to be **sufficiently regular to be predictable** — there have to be genuine, stable relationships between what you observe and what happens next. And the person has to have had **prolonged opportunity to learn those regularities, through practice with rapid and unambiguous feedback**.

Where both conditions hold, expertise is real and often remarkable. Chess players. Firefighters. Anaesthetists. Athletes. These people develop intuitions that outperform analysis, and they earn them.

Where the conditions do not hold, expertise never develops, no matter how much experience accumulates. Their canonical examples are people who pick individual stocks and people who forecast long-term political events. Twenty years in those fields produces confidence and produces nothing else, because there was never a stable pattern there to learn.

Now apply that to a company, and it stops being a psychology paper.

**A company that changes its rules every quarter is a low-validity environment.**

Not a difficult one. Not a demanding one. A low-validity one — a place where the relationship between what a person does and what happens next is unstable, and where therefore nobody can learn anything durable. Your people are not failing to develop mastery because they lack talent or commitment. They are failing to develop it because you keep moving the thing they were learning.

Imagine if football changed its rules every season. This year you may not touch the ball with your hands; next year you may. This year there is an offside rule; in two years there is not. Players would not become worse because they had stopped caring. They would become worse because nobody can build skill on top of a surface that keeps moving.

> **This is what protecting the machine is actually for. Not caution, and not control. Stability is the condition under which your people are able to get good.**

Which gives you the third reason the rule from the first chapter matters — build beside the machine, validate, then merge. It protects output. It protects the customer. And it protects the only environment in which anybody in your company can develop genuine competence.

And it explains something I spent years misunderstanding.

Over twenty-odd years working directly on management and scale with more than three hundred companies across twenty-three industries, I have heard the same complaint from almost every founder: *my people resist every change I try to make.* There is always resistance. It is exhausting, and it looks like a culture problem, and most founders eventually conclude that they have hired the wrong sort of person.

Then something happened that I did not predict.

When I introduced the experimental system into these companies — when changes started entering the machine only after being validated to Level 2, and when we presented the evidence to the people who would have to live with them — the resistance largely disappeared.

Not reduced. Disappeared, in most cases, and quickly.

Which told me that the diagnosis had been wrong the entire time, in every one of those companies including mine. The research on this is unambiguous, and it says what those founders' own employees would have told them if anybody had asked: **people do not resist change. They resist uncertainty, and they resist the motives of somebody who has asked them to reorganize their working life around an untested idea before.**

An employee who has lived through three abandoned software rollouts and two half-finished restructurings has not become cynical. They have become **correct**. They have accumulated evidence, from you, that changes announced with confidence tend to be reversed with silence.

> **Your people are not resisting your idea. They are pricing in your track record.**

Which means the fix is not better communication, and it is certainly not more conviction. It is evidence. Show somebody that a change has been tested, tell them what it was tested against, and the argument you expected does not happen.

### 10

The second process every machine needs is recruitment, and the anatomy from earlier in this chapter changes what recruitment even is.

Most organizations recruit **positions**. They know they need a marketer. So they write an advertisement for a marketer, they interview people who have been marketers, and they hire the one who interviews best.

But marketers come in several hundred varieties with entirely different skills, and "we need a marketer" describes a hole in an org chart rather than a requirement of a system. Which is why the interview then fills with the only things left to talk about: previous employers, general impressions, and whether the room liked the person.

In a machine, you do not recruit a position. **You recruit a set of responsibilities**, and those responsibilities came from standards, and those standards came from a process. Which means you already know, before anybody applies, exactly what this person will have to produce and at what level.

That changes the filters completely. You are no longer asking whether somebody seems capable in general. You are asking whether there is evidence they can produce these specific outputs, at this specific standard, in conditions like these.

And this is one of the rare places in management where the research is both clear and recently sharpened.

For twenty-five years, the reference point in personnel selection was a meta-analysis from 1998 that ranked cognitive ability tests as the strongest predictor of job performance. In 2022, Sackett and colleagues found a systematic statistical error running through decades of that literature — a correction for range restriction that had been applied in a way that inflated the numbers — and redid it.

Most predictors dropped. And the ranking changed at the top.

**The structured interview is now the single best predictor of job performance we know of**, ahead of cognitive ability tests.

Structured means something precise, and it is not a matter of being organized. It means the questions are decided in advance and derived from the actual requirements of the role. It means every candidate gets the same ones. It means the answers are scored against anchored criteria rather than impressions. It means the interviewer cannot wander into whatever they find interesting about the person in front of them.

Which is to say: the evidence for the best hiring method we have is an argument for exactly the thing this chapter has been building. **You cannot run a structured interview without knowing what the role has to produce. And you cannot know what the role has to produce until the process, the indicators and the standards exist.**

Most companies cannot hire well because they have nothing to hire against.

### 11

The third process is training, and I will be brief about it, because most of what is sold as training is theatre.

The best training in existence is timely, correct feedback. People learn more from being told precisely what they did and what it produced, close to the moment they did it, than from any curriculum. That is the second Kahneman condition, and a well-built machine delivers a great deal of it automatically, because the person can see their own indicators without waiting for anybody's opinion.

Where formal training is genuinely useful, it follows a rule that almost nobody applies: **train on what the evaluation revealed, and nothing else.**

A person is below standard on one responsibility. Train them on that. Not on that plus nine other things they already do competently, delivered in a two-day session to a room of eleven people with eleven different gaps.

That is why people hate training. In a typical programme, perhaps a tenth of the content is relevant to any given person in the room, and everybody in there knows it by the first coffee break.

And notice that this is the same discipline as the audit, applied at the scale of one human being. In the machine, we do not attempt to fix every problem — we find the bottleneck that is preventing the next level of output, and we work on that. With a person, it is identical. You do not improve everything about somebody. You find the one responsibility that is constraining their result, and you address that.

### 12

Which brings me to the thing I most want you to take out of this chapter, and it is not a technique.

Go back to the first chapter and look at the loop again.

The **vision** describes the default output. The **machine** produces it. The **output** is what reality actually delivered. The **audit** compares the two, and the gap is not a verdict. It is information about the machine, which sends you to multiply what works or to discover what does not exist yet.

Now look at what a performance evaluation is.

A person's **responsibilities and standards** describe their default output — what should happen in an ordinary week, without anybody being heroic. What they actually produce is the **reality**. And the evaluation is the **audit**: you put the two side by side and you measure the gap.

It is the same loop. It is not a metaphor, and it is not an analogy. It is the identical structure, viewed at a different magnification.

Which tells you exactly what to do when the gap is real, and it is not what most managers do.

You do not blame the person. Not out of kindness — out of accuracy. **The person is never the problem**, in the same sense that a bad quarter is never the problem. Both are outputs, and an output is evidence about a system.

So you diagnose, and there are only a handful of answers.

They may be in the wrong role — capable, but capable of something else. They may need training, on the specific thing the gap revealed. Their way of working may be genuinely incompatible with what this organization needs, in which case the honest thing is for them to be somewhere else, and saying so early is a kindness rather than a cruelty. They may need a larger challenge, because the gap is boredom. Or things may simply be working, and the correct decision is to change nothing at all, which is a decision almost nobody makes explicitly.

That is a diagnosis, and every one of those outcomes is actionable. Compare it with what happens in a company without this structure, where a manager sits down after six months of accumulated frustration and says that somebody has not been proactive enough, or does not have the right attitude — and the rules appear to change during the conversation, because the expectations were never made operational in the first place.

Nobody in that room is lying. The manager genuinely feels what they feel. But the employee is being audited against a standard that has only ever existed inside somebody else's head, and there is no version of that conversation that ends well.

> **Clarity does not remove disagreement. It makes disagreement useful.** Instead of arguing about what was expected, two people can examine whether the process, the standard, the resources, the skill or the decision was wrong — and every one of those is a question with an answer.

So the loop runs at the company, and it runs at the process, and it runs at the person. Three magnifications of one structure.

Which raises the obvious question, and it is the one the rest of this book exists to answer.

We have spent two chapters building a machine and learning how to run it. It produces its default output, reliably, without rescue.

And then reality reports back — and reality does not care what you intended, how hard anybody worked, or how good the story sounded in the meeting.

## Povestea de mai jos sustine mult mai bine partea de evaluari bilunare decat sa fie folosita la partea de audit

And you do not need a stadium to produce that state; a calendar will do. In a year-long field experiment inside a large Swedish public organisation, employees randomly assigned to a twelve-week evaluation cycle carried significantly higher stress hormones than colleagues on a six-week cycle — cortisol up by roughly a fifth — while their *self-reported* fatigue showed no difference at all. They could not feel it; their blood could. The researchers' explanation: it is the anticipation that does the damage — the months of quietly rehearsing justifications for a verdict that has not arrived yet, cognitive work that is invisible, exhausting, and directly in the way of the actual job.
