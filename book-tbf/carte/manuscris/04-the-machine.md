# 4. The Machine

### Danaher, Aravind, and the Management of the Known

---

### 1

In 1984, two brothers bought a failing real estate trust listed on the New York Stock Exchange, mostly for the listing, and renamed it after a river in Montana where they had been fishing.

Their head office was a small house under the Key Bridge in Washington. It contained Steven and Mitchell Rales, two assistants, a chief financial officer, and a finance staff of fewer than ten people. Nothing about it looked like the beginning of anything.

Over the following four decades, that company acquired more than four hundred businesses.

Not four hundred in one industry. Hand tools, then electronic instruments, then dental equipment, then water treatment, then medical diagnostics, then life sciences. Businesses with no customers in common, no technology in common, and no obvious reason to sit under the same roof. The kind of portfolio that a business school would describe as a conglomerate, which is normally the polite word for a company that has stopped knowing what it is.

And a startlingly high proportion of those four hundred acquisitions worked.

Since 1980, Danaher's annualized return to shareholders has been roughly three times that of the S&P Industrials Index. Since 1997, its annualized return has run above twenty percent. It compounded, for forty years, through recessions and bubbles and the collapse of entire industries it happened to be standing in.

Now name the chief executive.

You cannot. Almost nobody can, and that includes people who have worked in industrials their whole careers. There is no Danaher founder-genius, no keynote circuit, no memoir. The Rales brothers gave almost no interviews. The company has had a series of extremely capable chief executives whose names have never left the industry.

So here is the question this chapter exists to answer.

How do you buy four hundred unrelated businesses, most of them mediocre when you bought them, and make almost all of them better — without being a genius, and without anybody being able to name you?

The answer starts in the spring of 1987, at a conference in Hartford, Connecticut.

By then Danaher owned a company in Bloomfield called Jacobs Manufacturing, which made engine brakes for diesel trucks — the device that produces the loud clatter you hear when a lorry slows on a hill. Everyone called it Jake Brake. Its president was a man named George Koenigsaecker, and at that conference he met three Japanese consultants who had just started a firm called Shingijutsu.

The three of them had spent their entire careers at Toyota, working for Taiichi Ohno.

You have already met Ohno. He is the man from the first chapter who drew a circle in chalk on a factory floor, told a young engineer to stand in it and watch, and came back four hours later to ask what he had seen.

His people came to Connecticut and started taking Jake Brake apart.

What happened at that plant over the next four years became, between roughly 1988 and 1991, a codified operating system. Danaher gave it a name so boring it is almost a joke: the Danaher Business System. And then it did the thing that made everything else possible.

It stopped treating the system as something that had worked at Jake Brake, and started treating it as **the product**.

Danaher does not buy businesses. It buys places to install a system. When it acquires a company, the system arrives with it — the tools, the language, the metrics, the training, people who have run it elsewhere. The acquired business keeps its customers and its products and usually most of its people, and it gets a new operating architecture, and within a few years it produces materially more than it did before, with the same market and largely the same staff.

Four hundred times.

> **Danaher's asset is not a portfolio of companies. It is a machine for improving companies — and the reason it could do it four hundred times is that the machine was written down.**

That sentence is the whole chapter, and it has an uncomfortable corollary that we will come back to at the end. Everything Danaher owns, someone else could copy. The Danaher Business System is not secret. It is taught, published, discussed at conferences, and its principles came from Toyota, who published them.

Almost nobody copies it. We will get to why.

---

### 2

Before we go any further into what a machine is, I need to give you the distinction that organizes everything else in this book, and that I did not have for the first half of my career.

Everything a company does is either **known** or **unknown**.

Known work is work where we have the method. Not where we have a theory about the method, or a strong feeling about the method, or a method that worked once in unusual conditions. Where we have the method, have proved it, and can reproduce the result.

Unknown work is everything else. We want an output and we do not yet know how to produce it reliably.

That sounds like an obvious distinction, and it is. What is not obvious — and what took me twenty years and several damaged companies to understand — is that these two kinds of work require completely different management technologies, and that almost every serious management failure I have ever seen comes from applying one to the other.

**The known is managed through processes, indicators, standards, roles, training, and performance evaluation.** You write down how the work is done, you measure it, you set a line for what good looks like, you assign it to somebody, you teach them, and you check.

**The unknown is managed through experiments.** You form a hypothesis, you design the cheapest credible test, you decide in advance what would count as evidence, and you let reality answer.

Now watch what happens when you swap them.

Apply the tools of the known to the unknown and you get the objective trap from the second chapter. You do not know how to produce the result, so you set a target instead, attach a date, and apply pressure. The team takes its best guess, the guess becomes a commitment, the commitment becomes a matter of somebody's competence, and from that moment nobody in the room can afford to discover that the guess was wrong. You have converted uncertainty into premature commitment, and you have done it using the language of rigour.

Apply the tools of the unknown to the known and you get something less dramatic and almost as expensive. You run experiments on a question that was answered three years ago. You reopen a process that works, because somebody had a fresh idea about it, and you destabilize an operation in order to test something the company already knows.

> **Managing the known through experiments is waste. Managing the unknown through targets is damage.**

Which brings us back to the loop.

Look at the diagram from the first chapter again and read it with this distinction in hand, because it says something now that it did not say before.

**The machine is the management of the known.** Everything in it has been proved.

**Discovery is the management of the unknown.** It is where things go that have not been proved, and its output is not revenue. Its output is knowledge.

**Multiplication is the multiplication of the known.** It takes something already proved and produces more of it.

That is the entire architecture, in three lines. A company manages what it knows in order to be predictable. And then, when it wants more output, it has exactly two moves available: turn some of the unknown into known, or make more of the known.

There is no third move. There never has been. What looks like a third move — pushing harder on the existing machine — is not growth. It is borrowing, and the chapters you have already read describe what the repayment looks like.

---

### 3

So: the machine is the predictable part of the business.

Notice again what that sentence excludes, because the exclusion is doing most of the work. The machine is not the company. The company contains everything — every initiative, every hunch, every meeting, every opportunity somebody is enthusiastic about. The machine is the much smaller thing inside it that has been validated well enough to produce a result repeatedly and on purpose.

Which means there is a border, and things have to earn their way across it.

A sales script that succeeded once is not in the machine. A product that delighted a handful of early customers is not in the machine. A tactic copied from a competitor is not in the machine, because you have observed their output and not their conditions. And the founder's conviction is not in the machine, however strong the conviction and however good the founder's record.

I want to be precise about what I am saying, because founders hear this as an insult and it is not one. None of those things are worthless. Several of them are the most valuable inputs the company has. They are simply not *knowledge* yet. They are candidates.

The mistake is not having them. The mistake is letting them behave as though they had already been proved.

Which requires a way of saying how proved a thing actually is.

---

### 4

There are four levels, and the entire discipline of this book depends on being honest about which one you are standing on.

> **Level 0 — Hypothesis.** It comes from experience, research, or advice. It has never been tested here. It might be true.
>
> **Level 1 — Proof.** It worked. Once. Under conditions you can describe. It *can* work.
>
> **Level 2 — Repeatability.** It worked repeatedly, and it has been standardized well enough that the repetition is not an accident. It *keeps* working.
>
> **Level 3 — Transferability.** It works through other people. Another team can take the blueprint and produce the same output without the original team rescuing them.

The machine contains every Level 2 and above process capable of generating your Ordinary Great Quarter within its constraints. That is the entry rule, and the moment you make it negotiable it stops meaning anything at all.

Now let me take you through the three transitions, because each one has its own way of going wrong, and the middle one is where almost everybody is lying to themselves.

---

**Level 0 to Level 1** is the transition everybody romanticizes and almost nobody does.

In 1979 James Dyson was renovating a house and got annoyed with his vacuum cleaner. The annoyance was specific and, if you have ever owned a bagged vacuum, familiar: it lost suction almost immediately. He opened it and worked out why. The bag is not just a container; it is the filter. The moment a thin layer of dust coats the inside of it, air can no longer pass through, and the machine is finished. Not full. Choked.

He had also seen a sawmill using a cyclone tower to separate sawdust from air by spinning it. No filter. Nothing to clog.

The hypothesis was formed in an afternoon: put a cyclone in a vacuum cleaner. Level 0.

The hypothesis turned out to be correct. It took him roughly five thousand one hundred and twenty-six failures to make it true.

The number gets repeated as a story about persistence, which is the least interesting thing about it. What matters is what those five thousand attempts consisted of. Almost all of them were cardboard and tape, and almost all of them differed from the previous one by a **single variable** — the angle of the cone, the diameter of the inlet, the height of the cylinder. Build one. Run it. Measure. Change one thing. Build the next.

This is the least glamorous work available to a human being. It is also the only kind that produces knowledge, because if you change three things at once and the result improves, you have learned nothing except that you are lucky.

---

**Level 1 to Level 2** is where the real damage happens, and it happens quietly, and almost every company I have worked with has done it.

Something works. A new sales approach closes three deals in a month. A different onboarding sequence produces a batch of unusually happy customers. A campaign performs. A pricing change lands.

And the room does something that feels entirely reasonable. It concludes that the thing works — and moves it into the machine. Standardizes it. Trains people on it. Builds targets around it. Hires against it.

But look at what actually happened. You have one observation. And a single observation is compatible with an enormous number of explanations, only one of which is *the method works*.

It might have been a good month. Markets have weather.

It might have been the customers. If you tried the new approach on the three warmest leads in the pipeline, you have measured the leads, not the approach.

It might have been novelty. Some things work precisely because they are unfamiliar, and stop working at exactly the moment they become the standard.

It might have been the conditions. A competitor was distracted. A budget cycle opened. Somebody's contract came up for renewal that week anyway.

And then there is the explanation that this book exists to make visible, which is almost never the one anybody reaches for.

**Somebody did something remarkable, and nobody saw it.**

The new sales approach closed three deals, and it closed them because the person running it stayed late rewriting the proposal, or called in a favour, or noticed something wrong in the second meeting and quietly fixed it before anybody else knew there was a problem. None of that was written down. None of it appeared in the report. What appeared in the report was: *new approach, three closed.*

You are now about to standardize a process whose actual active ingredient is a human being compensating for it — and to hand that process to people who will not compensate, because they do not know they are supposed to.

That is how a company manufactures the heroic role described in the second chapter without a single person intending to. Nobody decided to make the operation depend on rescue. Somebody just mistook a rescue for a result.

If you want to see how hard this transition is even when an entire industry is designed around it, look at how drugs are developed.

Pharmaceutical research has done what almost no other field has bothered to do: it has legally institutionalized the ladder. A compound goes through preclinical work, then Phase 1 with a few dozen people to check that it does not hurt anybody, then Phase 2 with a few hundred to see whether it does anything, then Phase 3 with thousands, across many sites, in the messy variety of real patients. You are not permitted to skip a stage. Each one is designed to answer a different question, and the questions are asked in a fixed order.

That is Level 0 through Level 2, with regulators enforcing it and billions of dollars behind it.

And it still loses most of what it carries.

Roughly seventy percent of drugs that succeed in Phase 2 do not survive Phase 3. Not because anybody cheated, and not because the Phase 2 result was fake — it was real. It just did not generalize. Lack of efficacy accounts for about half of all late-stage failures: the thing that worked in a smaller, cleaner, more controlled setting stopped working when it met the full width of reality.

So sit with that number for a moment. The most disciplined validation system human beings have ever built, with legal enforcement and enormous capital behind it, loses seven out of ten things between *it worked* and *it keeps working*.

Now consider what your one good quarter proves.

> **A single success is not evidence that a method works. It is evidence that a method worked once, which is a different sentence, and the gap between them is where most operations get built.**

Level 2 requires two things, and companies routinely claim it with neither. The result must have repeated, under conditions that varied — not the same customer, the same season, the same person. And it must have been **standardized**, which means somebody wrote down what is supposed to happen, so that the repetition is a property of the process rather than a property of the people who happened to be there.

---

**Level 2 to Level 3** is transferability, and it is the transition that decides whether you have a business or a very good team.

Here is the distinction, and it is sharper than it first appears.

A Level 2 process works, repeatedly, in the hands of the people who run it. A Level 3 process works in the hands of people who did not build it — a different team, in a different room, possibly in a different city, following the blueprint, producing the same output, without the original team having to come and rescue them.

That is not the same thing at all, and the gap between them is invisible until you try to cross it.

Take a sales team that is performing well against a defined process. The numbers are consistent. The process is documented. By any reasonable audit it is Level 2, and it is.

Now try to build a second one.

The first team's performance might be coming from the process. It might also be coming from the fact that those six people have worked together for four years and can finish each other's sentences. It might be coming from one person who quietly handles every difficult case and has never been asked to describe how. It might be coming from a manager who absorbs friction that nobody has ever named, because absorbing friction does not appear on an organizational chart.

All of those produce a good result. **None of them transfer**, and you will not find out which one you had until the new team is three months in and underperforming, and everybody starts wondering what is wrong with the new people.

This is what Danaher actually solved, and it is why the number in the first section is four hundred rather than four.

Installing a business system in an acquired company is not sending a document. It is sending the tools, the vocabulary, the measurement, the training, and — critically — people who have run the system somewhere else and can run it here. Danaher's product is not a manual. It is a method for getting a method into a building where nobody has seen it before, and the reason the company could do that four hundred times is that it treated the transfer itself as a process to be improved, rather than as a thing that either works or does not.

So the test for Level 3 is uncomfortably concrete:

> **Could a newly recruited and inducted team, following the written blueprint, produce the same range of output — without the founder or the original team rescuing them?**

If the answer is no, you do not own a transferable process. You own experience trapped inside particular human beings, which is a real asset and a completely different one, and it will not survive being copied.

One qualification, because otherwise this reads as a demand that everything reach Level 3, and it is not.

**Most things in your business do not need to be transferable.** A process that will only ever run in one team, in one place, for the life of the company, is perfectly fine at Level 2. Transferability costs real work — extraction, documentation, training design, testing — and you should spend it only where you intend to multiply.

The failure is not having Level 2 processes. The failure is *believing you have Level 3* when you have Level 2, and discovering the difference after you have signed the lease on the second location.

### 5

Now the crime.

The most expensive mistake I see, by a very wide margin, is a company taking a Level 0 hypothesis and installing it directly at Level 2 — building operations around it, hiring people into it, constructing systems that are extremely hard to unwind — before anybody has proved it is the right path.

It feels faster. It is the slowest route available.

Webvan was going to be the future of groceries, and the people who believed that were not fools. The founder had already built Borders Books into a national chain. The investors were Sequoia, Benchmark and SoftBank. They hired the chief executive of Andersen Consulting to run it. The 1999 flotation valued the company at around eight billion dollars, and in total it raised something on the order of one and a quarter billion.

The thesis was coherent, and I want to be fair to it, because coherence is exactly the trap. Groceries are the largest retail category in the economy. Margins are thin because of stores — real estate, shelf-stackers, shrinkage. Remove the store, replace it with a highly automated distribution centre serving an entire metropolitan area, and your cost per order falls below what a supermarket can achieve while you deliver to the customer's door.

Every sentence in that paragraph is plausible. Nobody could find the flaw by arguing about it. The flaw could only be found by running it.

Webvan did not run it. Webvan built it.

The Oakland distribution centre cost around thirty million dollars and contained roughly five miles of conveyor belt. It was a genuinely impressive piece of engineering. Then, before it had proved it could serve one city profitably, Webvan signed a contract worth around a billion dollars to build twenty-six more of them across the United States, and bought its closest competitor in a stock deal valued at over a billion.

Twenty-six cities. Before profitability in one.

What running it would have taught them — what it did eventually teach them, at a cost of over eight hundred million dollars — was a set of things nobody could have argued their way to. That a thirty-minute delivery window is enormously more expensive than a two-hour window and customers do not value it proportionally. That the automated warehouses ran at under thirty percent capacity because order density in a given postcode was far below the model. That grocery customers substitute, complain about bruised produce, and are not loyal. That the money dies in the last mile — a truck, a driver, traffic, parking — and no amount of conveyor belt upstream fixes it.

Webvan filed for bankruptcy in July 2001, eighteen months after listing.

> **Building the operation is not the fast route to validating the model. It is the most expensive possible way to run an experiment, and it destroys your ability to change the answer.**

The same crime happens in a second form, and it wears a much better suit.

In November 2011, J.C. Penney hired the most admired retail executive in the world. Ron Johnson had built the Apple Store — not managed it, built it: the layout, the Genius Bar, the removal of the checkout counter, the idea that a computer shop could be the highest-revenue-per-square-foot retail space on earth. His hiring was announced and the stock rose seventeen percent in a day.

His diagnosis was correct. J.C. Penney's coupon model was corrosive; the company was marking merchandise up in order to mark it down, running something like six hundred promotional events a year, and had trained its customers to treat the printed price as a lie.

What he did next is the story. He replaced it — everywhere, at once. Out went sales, coupons and clearance; in came everyday low pricing. Out went the store layout; in came a plan to rebuild every store as a collection of branded boutiques. New logo. New advertising. New leadership team.

When people inside the company suggested testing the pricing change in a subset of stores first — the standard, boring, hundred-year-old practice of the retail industry — his answer became famous.

*We didn't test at Apple.*

That sentence is not stupid. It is true. Apple did not A/B test the Genius Bar. Apple ran on the taste of a small group with an extraordinary record, inside a machine built around them, selling products with no substitute, to customers who were already devoted.

None of those conditions existed at J.C. Penney.

Sales fell twenty-five percent in a year. Net loss of $985 million. Holiday quarter down twenty-eight percent. Roughly four billion dollars of revenue gone. He was fired seventeen months in, and his predecessor came back and immediately restored the coupons with a full-page apology to customers.

Ron Johnson is an excellent executive. That is the point of including him. He arrived at a company with no validated architecture, imported a private machine that had worked somewhere structurally different, and installed it at Level 2 without ever running it at Level 1.

Both of these are the same failure, and it has a shape you can learn to recognize. A coherent explanation of how the business will work is not evidence. **Coherence means the story does not contradict itself, which is a very low bar.** Webvan's story did not contradict itself either.

### 6

Now I want to give you the reason to do all of this, because so far this chapter has mostly described work, and you are entitled to ask what it buys.

It buys something that sounds impossible until you see it done.

Dr Govindappa Venkataswamy retired from the Indian government medical service in 1976, at the age of fifty-eight, with rheumatoid arthritis so severe that his fingers were permanently deformed. He had trained himself to hold surgical instruments anyway and had personally performed something like a hundred thousand eye operations. On retirement, he opened an eleven-bed clinic in his brother's house in Madurai, in Tamil Nadu.

The problem he faced was arithmetic, not medicine.

India had millions of people going blind from cataracts, which is a condition that is completely curable by a twenty-minute operation. The operation was not the constraint. The number of surgeons was. If every ophthalmologist in India worked at the normal rate for the rest of their lives, most of those people would die blind.

So Dr V, as everyone called him, went looking for a model of how to produce something in enormous quantity at consistent quality, and he found it somewhere no doctor would think to look.

He looked at McDonald's.

His reasoning was almost childlike in its directness: *if McDonald's can sell billions of burgers, and Coca-Cola can sell billions of bottles, why can't I sell millions of sight-restoring operations?*

Here is what he built.

In an Aravind operating theatre, the surgeon does not walk between patients. There are two operating tables side by side. The surgeon operates on one while a full team preps, positions, anaesthetizes and drapes the patient on the other. When the surgeon finishes, they rotate the chair. Everything a surgeon does that is not surgery — the paperwork, the counselling, the preparation, the follow-up, the sterilization, the recovery — has been moved out and given to somebody else.

And now the part that matters for this chapter.

That somebody else is, in very many cases, a young woman recruited from a rural village, with no medical background whatsoever, trained in-house for two years.

Aravind's surgeons average around two thousand cataract operations a year against an Indian national average of roughly four hundred. The system sees over ten thousand outpatients and performs well over a thousand surgeries a day. Somewhere between half and seventy percent of patients pay nothing at all, subsidized by the ones who do.

And the clinical outcomes for the patients who pay nothing are the same as for the patients who pay. Aravind's complication rates sit at or better than published rates in the United Kingdom.

I want you to hold those two facts next to each other, because the conclusion they force is one that almost nobody in business is willing to say out loud.

The people doing most of the work were, before Aravind found them, ordinary. Not gifted. Not credentialed. Not the sort of people any Western health system would have let near an operating theatre. And the outcomes are world-class, not despite that, but because the system was built to make the outcome independent of who is standing there.

> **The known can be run by ordinary people, because the system does most of the work. That is not a compromise. That is the entire point of building one.**

And notice what Aravind is, in the language of this chapter. Cataract surgery is not an unsolved problem. Nobody at Aravind is discovering anything. The method was known before Dr V opened his eleven beds, and it was known in every teaching hospital in the world.

What Aravind built was not a better operation. It was the **management of the known**, taken further than anybody had bothered to take it.

In fact Aravind is the only organization in this chapter that has climbed the entire ladder, and you can point at each rung.

**Level 0** was a hypothesis, and an absurd-sounding one: *if McDonald's can sell billions of burgers, why can't I sell millions of operations?*

**Level 1** was an eleven-bed clinic in his brother's house, where it worked.

**Level 2** was Madurai — the two tables, the rotating chair, the specified steps, repeated thousands of times until repetition was a property of the process rather than of the people in the room.

And **Level 3** is where it stops being a story about a hospital.

Aravind built an institute, LAICO, whose entire purpose is to install the system somewhere else. It has now worked with more than three hundred and thirty-five eye hospitals around the world — hospitals Aravind does not own, in countries Dr V never worked in, staffed by people who have never met anybody from Madurai. It has trained over three and a half thousand managers from eighty-three countries, and built thirty-five structured training programmes to do it.

One of those hospitals, in Blantyre, Malawi, increased its surgical volume by a hundred and forty-two percent within a year of starting the process.

That is the actual definition of Level 3, and there is no softer version of it. A blueprint developed in Tamil Nadu, carried to Malawi, run by strangers, producing the output — without anybody from the original team standing behind them.

And the deepest piece of transferability at Aravind is not the surgery at all. It is the sisters.

The mid-level ophthalmic personnel are recruited at seventeen or eighteen, from low-income rural families, with a secondary school education and no work experience. Four months of basic training, then two years of specialization. At the end of it they are running diagnostics, counselling patients, preparing theatres, and doing work that in most countries requires a nursing qualification.

Aravind did not find those women. It **makes** them, repeatedly, on a schedule, from raw material that every other health system in the world had written off as unqualified.

That is the hardest form of transferability there is. Not a documented process — a documented process for producing the person who runs the process.

And it is the whole opportunity, sitting in almost every company I walk into. Not a discovery waiting to be made. A known thing, running at Level 1, held together by people who are compensating for it — in a company that has decided the real problem is that it cannot find good enough staff.

This is the argument I most often have to make to a founder, and it usually arrives as a complaint. *If I could just find better people.* *We need an A-player in that seat.* *The problem is that nobody is as good as I am at this.*

Sometimes that is true. Much more often, it is a description of an unbuilt system that has been mistaken for a hiring problem.

And even when it is true, it is a strategy that does not survive contact with arithmetic. You will not find enough exceptional people. If you find them, you cannot afford them at the volume you need. If you can afford them, you cannot keep them, because exceptional people leave — for a better offer, for a competitor, for a company of their own, or because their spouse got a job in another city. A business whose ordinary output requires exceptional people has built its foundation out of other people's career decisions.

Building a machine is how you stop asking human beings to be exceptional in order for a Tuesday to work.

### 7

If Aravind proves the case from one direction, there is a factory in California that proves it from the other, and it is the more uncomfortable of the two.

The General Motors assembly plant in Fremont was, by common agreement, the worst car factory in the United States. The stories are not exaggerations. Workers drank in the parking lot at lunch and came back to the line. Absenteeism ran above twenty percent — on any given morning, one in five people simply did not come in, and the plant ran anyway, badly, with whoever happened to be standing there. There was sabotage: a Coke bottle sealed inside a door panel so it would rattle for the life of the car. When GM closed the plant in 1982, there were around eight hundred unresolved grievances outstanding.

Everyone knew what the problem was. The problem was the people. Everyone at GM said so. Fremont had the worst workforce in the industry.

In 1984 GM reopened the plant as a joint venture with Toyota, called NUMMI. Toyota ran it. And Toyota, to the horror of GM management, insisted on hiring back the old workforce. The same men. The same union. Including the most notorious troublemakers, some of whom Toyota flew to Japan for training.

Within months, the cars coming off the Fremont line were rated at near-perfect quality — essentially the same as the cars coming out of Toyota City. Absenteeism collapsed. Grievances collapsed. Men who had spent a decade sabotaging their employer told interviewers that for the first time in their working lives they enjoyed going to work.

Not one thing about those human beings had changed. What changed was the machine they were standing in.

The specifics are almost comically unglamorous. GM's system had one rule above all others: never stop the line. If a defect passed you, you let it go, and someone at the end would catch it, or wouldn't. Toyota gave every single worker a cord. Pull it, and the line stops. Pull it, and a team leader comes to *you*.

To a GM worker this was insane. In the first weeks, some pulled the cord just to see whether it was real. It was. The line stopped. Nobody was punished. Someone came to help.

GM's system defined a job as a set of motions to be repeated. Toyota's defined a job as a documented standard that the person doing it was expected to improve. GM's system treated a defect as a person's failure. Toyota's treated a defect as information about the process.

For twenty years, General Motors believed it had a people problem.

It had an architecture problem wearing a people problem's clothes — and that is the single most expensive misdiagnosis available to a founder, because the evidence for the wrong conclusion is overwhelming and sits in front of you every day, in the form of people who are visibly not performing.

But the NUMMI story has a second half that almost nobody tells, and it is the more useful one, because it is about the transition we were just discussing.

General Motors owned half of NUMMI. It had unrestricted access. It sent hundreds of managers through that plant to learn how it worked. And it had a plant of its own, Van Nuys, four hundred miles down the coast, that was struggling in exactly the way Fremont had been.

So GM tried to copy it.

It failed completely. Quality at Van Nuys never improved. The plant closed in 1992.

Read the reasons, because every one of them is a lesson about transferability.

They copied the artifact and not the conditions. The andon cord was installed at Van Nuys, because the andon cord is the visible, photographable, easily-described part of the system. But the cord only works inside a set of arrangements that are almost entirely invisible: a team leader who can actually come when it is pulled, a standard clear enough that a worker knows a defect when they see one, and — above all — an organization that has genuinely decided a stopped line is better than a bad car. Without those, the cord is a piece of rope.

They dispersed the carriers. The handful of people who had learned the system at Fremont were sent back into a company that punished the behaviour they had learned. GM was, in that era, an enormously decentralized organization in which plant managers were sovereign. A method has to survive its environment, and this one did not.

And there was a condition at Fremont that nobody had counted as part of the system, because nobody had noticed it was doing work. The Fremont workers had lost their jobs. The plant had closed and they had been unemployed, and Toyota had given the jobs back. The Van Nuys workers had never lost theirs, and did not believe they would, even when GM said the plant would close. One of the people trying to run the transformation there described the lack of receptiveness as so deep that there were simply too many people convinced they did not need to change.

That is the invisible ingredient, and it is the exact thing that kills multiplication. Nobody at GM wrote *the workforce must have already lost this plant once* into the blueprint, because nobody knew it was in there.

So look at what General Motors actually had. It had a Level 2 process — NUMMI worked, repeatedly, for years, in one building. What it believed it had was Level 3.

And four hundred miles away, Toyota was opening plants in Kentucky and Ontario and reproducing the result, because Toyota had spent decades doing the unglamorous work of making the method independent of the people who invented it.

Same system. Same continent. Same decade. One company could move it and the other could not, and the difference was not intelligence or money or access. It was that one of them had done the extraction and the other had taken photographs.

---

### 8

Two warnings before we go on, because everything above can be read as a bigger promise than I am making.

**The first: predictable does not mean certain.**

No business output is perfectly predictable. Markets move. People leave. Suppliers fail. Competitors react. Regulation changes. Human systems contain variation and always will.

Predictability means something much more modest and much more useful: that the business understands the relevant causes well enough to expect an output within a useful range, and — this is the half that gets forgotten — to *notice* when performance moves outside that range.

That second half is the actual product.

> **A machine does not remove uncertainty. It converts uncertainty from a fog into monitored variation.**

In a fog, a bad month is an argument. Somebody says the market is soft, somebody else says the team has gone flat, somebody else says the product needs work, and everybody is drawing on the same absence of evidence. With monitored variation, a bad month is a signal with a location. You know which range was breached, and where, and when it started.

**The second warning: structure does not guarantee success.**

A perfectly managed machine can execute a bad business model with beautiful efficiency. Danaher's system would not have saved Webvan, because Webvan's problem was not that it executed poorly. It executed impressively. Its problem was that it had industrialized an assumption.

You can build a superb machine for producing something nobody wants, at a price that does not work, for a market that is not large enough. The machine will do exactly what you built it to do, and it will do it reliably, all the way down.

But the converse is also true, and it is the reason the machine still comes first: **without structure, even a good model becomes dependent on memory, personality, and force** — and those three things do not scale, do not transfer, and are not for sale.

---

### 9

So how do you know whether you actually have one?

There is a single test, and it is deliberately demanding.

> **The machine is successful when normal output does not require exceptional rescue.**

That does not mean emergencies never happen, or that individual excellence stops mattering. It means recurring performance should come from the architecture rather than from one person repeatedly compensating for its absence.

In practice it decomposes into questions you can ask this afternoon.

If invoices are collected only when the founder calls, collection is not yet in the machine. If quality survives only because one expert checks everything personally, quality is not yet in the machine. If every customer exception has to reach the same leader, the decision system is not yet in the machine.

And the cost of being wrong about this is not theoretical.

On 1 August 2012, Knight Capital Group was one of the largest market makers in American equities, handling a substantial share of all retail trading volume on the New York Stock Exchange. It had been in business for seventeen years.

At half past nine in the morning the market opened. At quarter past ten it was over. In forty-five minutes Knight lost approximately four hundred and forty million dollars — more than its entire market capitalization. The company was effectively destroyed.

The cause was not an attack, a rogue trader, or a market crash. It was a deployment.

A technician was installing new software onto the eight servers that ran Knight's order routing system. He copied the new code onto seven of them.

He missed the eighth.

On its own that should have been survivable. What made it fatal was a second thing, sitting in the code for nine years. The new software reused a flag that had once controlled a retired function called Power Peg. Power Peg had been switched off in 2003 — but its code was never deleted from the servers. On the seven updated machines the flag meant one thing. On the eighth, still running old code, the same flag switched on a nine-year-old function with no position limits and no stop.

At 9:30 the flag went live and the eighth server began firing orders into the market, buying high and selling low, thousands of times a second, with nothing in it that knew when to stop.

There had been a warning. In the pre-market, Knight's systems generated error messages — reportedly ninety-seven of them, emailed to a group of staff. They were not designed as alerts. Nobody treated them as alerts. And when the losses started, nobody could simply press a button and restore the previous state, because there was no documented rollback procedure.

The regulator's subsequent order reads as a catalogue of absences. No written procedure requiring a second technician to verify the deployment. No automated check that all servers matched. No process for removing dead code. No escalation path for pre-market errors.

Seventeen years of business, and the whole thing rested on one person, working from memory, at night, correctly copying a file onto eight machines.

Put that deployment on the ladder and the whole thing becomes obvious in retrospect. It was Level 1. It had worked — many times, for years — because a competent person did it correctly on each occasion. It had never been standardized, never been checked by a second pair of eyes, never had a rollback written, and never been tested by anybody who had not done it before.

A process that works because a good person is doing it is not a Level 2 process, no matter how many years it has been working. It is a Level 1 process with a long run of luck attached, and the luck is the person.

So ask it about your own company, for every step that matters: **if the person doing this were distracted for ten seconds tonight, what would catch it?** If the honest answer is *nothing, but they're very good* — then that step is not in the machine, no matter how long it has been working.

---

Which returns us, finally, to the uncomfortable thing I left at the end of the first section.

The Danaher Business System is not a secret. It is taught. It is written about. Its principles came from Toyota, who have published them, run tours of their factories, and spent decades explaining themselves to anybody who asked. General Motors owned half of NUMMI and sent hundreds of managers through it.

Almost nobody copies it successfully.

Not because the ideas are difficult. You have most of them already, and none of them required a degree to understand. They are not copied because copying them means doing a very large amount of unglamorous, invisible, un-photographable work — writing down how things are done, deciding what good looks like, checking that it happened, and refusing to change any of it until you have evidence.

Which is the subject of the next chapter, and it is where this book stops being philosophy.
