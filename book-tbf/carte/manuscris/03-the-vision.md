# 3. The Vision

### Buzz Aldrin, Andre Agassi, and the Trouble with Victories

---

### 1

Buzz Aldrin was thirty-nine years old when he walked on the moon.

He had a doctorate from MIT in orbital rendezvous — his thesis is one of the reasons Apollo worked at all. He had flown sixty-six combat missions in Korea. He was, by any measure, among the most capable human beings alive. And on 20 July 1969 he became the second person in history to stand on another world.

Standing there, looking at the lunar surface, he said two words that have been quoted ever since as poetry: *magnificent desolation.*

What is less often quoted is what those words came to mean.

He came home to the largest welcome any human being has ever received. Parades. Presidents. A world tour. And everywhere he went, for months, people asked him the same question, which is the only question anybody could think to ask: what was it like on the moon?

He had no idea how to answer it. And underneath the question was a second one that nobody said out loud but that he could hear perfectly: *what are you going to do now?*

He was thirty-nine. The single most extraordinary thing that would ever happen to him had already happened. Every remaining decade of his life was going to be lived on the far side of it.

In his 1973 memoir *Return to Earth*, Aldrin did something almost unthinkable for an American national hero of that era. He described the depression and the drinking honestly. Days when he did not get out of bed. A marriage he damaged. A career at NASA that no longer had a shape, because the thing it had been shaped around was finished. He went into psychiatric care. He stopped drinking in 1978, nine years after the moon, and stayed sober for the rest of a very long life.

He was clear, later, that the moon did not cause his depression — there was family history, there was alcoholism in his line. But he was equally clear about what the *structure* of his life had done to him. He had spent his entire adult existence pointed at a single event. The event happened. And the machinery of his life had been built to arrive somewhere, not to live anywhere.

Read what happened to the other eleven. One became a painter. One found God and left to start a healing ministry. One sold cars for a while. Several marriages ended. Almost all of them describe some version of the same problem: what do you do on a Tuesday, when the summit is behind you?

If you think this is specific to astronauts, consider Andre Agassi.

In July 1992 he won Wimbledon. It is hard to overstate what that meant at that point in his life. He was twenty-two. He had lost three Grand Slam finals. The entire tennis establishment had written him off as an image with a forehand — the hair, the denim shorts, the commercial in which he said *image is everything*, which followed him around like a criminal conviction. Wimbledon was the tournament supposedly least suited to him, on the surface supposedly designed to expose him, in the sport's most conservative cathedral.

He beat Goran Ivanišević in five sets, fell to his knees on the grass, and wept.

Years later, in his memoir *Open*, he wrote about the hours afterwards. He described it as being let in on a dirty little secret: winning changes nothing.

And then the sentence that follows, which is the most quietly devastating thing an athlete has ever written about achievement:

> *A win doesn't feel as good as a loss feels bad, and the good feeling doesn't last as long as the bad. Not even close.*

That is not a description of a bad day. It is a description of an asymmetry that is structural, and that anyone who has ever chased a target will recognize the moment they stop lying to themselves about it. The victory gives you a few hours of relief and then dissolves into the new baseline. The defeat gives you months.

Describing the same win later, he said that at that stage of his career Wimbledon felt more like relief than celebration — and he was careful to add that this was the unfortunate part, not the noble part.

### 2

The obvious response to all of this is that Aldrin and Agassi were unusual men with unusual pressures, and that a well-adjusted person would simply enjoy the victory properly.

That response is wrong, and there is a study that shows why.

In 1978, three psychologists — Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman — published a paper with a title that reads like a provocation: *Lottery Winners and Accident Victims: Is Happiness Relative?*

They found twenty-two people who had won major prizes in the Illinois State Lottery, from fifty thousand dollars to a million — serious money in 1978. They found twenty-nine people who had been paralysed in accidents within roughly the previous year, most of them permanently. And they found a control group of ordinary people who had neither won nor lost anything remarkable.

Then they asked all three groups the same things. How happy are you now? How happy were you before? How happy do you expect to be in a couple of years? And, crucially: how much pleasure do you take in a list of perfectly ordinary daily events — talking with a friend, watching television, eating breakfast, hearing a joke, receiving a compliment?

The headline finding is the one everybody remembers. The lottery winners were not significantly happier than the controls in their ratings of present happiness. Something every human being on earth believes will transform their life had, within a year, largely failed to.

But the second finding is the one that belongs in this chapter, and it is stranger.

The lottery winners rated the ordinary daily pleasures as *less* enjoyable than the control group did. Not the same. Less. Talking to a friend, reading, having breakfast — all of it had lost some of its flavour.

The explanation the researchers offered has held up reasonably well: contrast. Once you have experienced an enormous positive event, everything ordinary is now being measured against it. The peak does not merely fail to raise your baseline. It can devalue everything below the peak.

Forty-five years of argument have complicated the details — the sample is small, the design is cross-sectional, later work has refined it. What has survived all of it is the core observation, now boringly well established under the name hedonic adaptation. Human beings absorb changes in their circumstances and return, mostly, to something near where they were.

Which means that the entire architecture of the goal-based life is fighting a documented property of the human nervous system.

You are not weak for failing to stay happy after the victory. You are a normally functioning animal. The problem is not you. The problem is that you have organized your life around events, and events wash out.

### 3

Now let me bring this back into a company, because the same structure produces the same result at organizational scale, and there it costs money as well as peace of mind.

At the start of the 2015–16 season, bookmakers offered odds of 5000-1 on Leicester City winning the English Premier League. To understand how absurd that number is: the same bookmakers offered shorter odds on Elvis Presley being found alive.

Leicester had finished the previous season fourteenth, having spent most of it bottom of the table. They had been in the third tier of English football seven years earlier. Their entire squad had cost a fraction of what their rivals spent on one player.

They won it.

It is, by consensus, the greatest single achievement in the history of English club football and probably in any major league sport. Grown men who had supported Leicester for fifty years wept in the street. Their striker, Jamie Vardy, had been playing non-league football and working in a factory five years earlier.

Now here is what happened next.

The manager was sacked nine months later. The core of the team was sold. The club oscillated for a few seasons — a genuinely good FA Cup win in 2021, some strong league finishes, and mounting losses. In 2023 they were relegated, seven years after winning the league. They came straight back up, and went down again in 2025. In 2026 they were relegated to League One — the third tier, where they had been before the fairytale started.

Ten years from champions of England to the third division.

The temptation is to read this as tragedy or bad luck, and there was certainly bad luck — the pandemic destroyed the revenue of the owners' duty-free business, and the money stopped. But that explanation is itself the point.

Leicester's title was a victory. It was not a capability.

What Leicester had in 2015–16 was an extraordinary alignment of temporary conditions: a squad of undervalued players all hitting career-best form simultaneously, an unusually low injury count, a season in which every large club underperformed at once, and a manager whose approach happened to suit that specific group of men. Every one of those things was true once.

Contrast that with what a machine looks like in the same industry. Brighton and Hove Albion, and before them Brentford, were built by owners who came out of professional gambling and therefore had a professional's contempt for the one-off. They did not build a squad. They built a system for identifying undervalued players, developing them, selling them at a large profit, and replacing them with the next one from the same pipeline. Brighton has repeatedly sold its best players for enormous fees and finished *higher* the following season.

Neither Brighton nor Brentford has ever won the Premier League. Both will still be in it, in some form, long after the Leicester fairytale has become a documentary.

Leicester owned a season. Brighton owns a method.

### 4

So here is where I have arrived, after twenty years of doing this the other way.

I no longer believe in the classical form of vision, when what it actually is is a long-term objective wearing inspirational clothing.

Traditional vision pulls you back toward goals, whether those goals are dressed as indicators, milestones, targets, or a list of future victories. And both targets and victories do the same thing: they move satisfaction into the future. *I will be happy, proud, fulfilled when this result is achieved.* Which teaches, quietly and continuously, that the present is not good enough and that life becomes meaningful only after the next win.

When I defined vision as a collection of victories, my mind naturally organized itself around what had not yet happened. Those victories encouraged me to postpone contentment until the next milestone. And there was always a next milestone, because that is what milestones are for.

The definition I use now is this:

> **Vision is the description of the default output we want the machine to produce.**

Not a list of extraordinary one-time results. A description of the reality we want the business to produce *repeatedly, predictably, and at a desired cadence*.

The purpose of a vision is not to identify the next result that would make the founder feel successful. Its purpose is to define what should eventually become **normal** for the machine.

That single change moves the object of ambition. Instead of asking *what extraordinary result do we want to achieve?*, we ask:

> **What should this machine be capable of producing by default?**

The distinction is between a result that happened once, and a result the organization has learned how to reproduce.

A one-time victory proves that something is possible. A desired default output requires that you own the repeatable cause. Which is a far more demanding standard, and it is also, unlike the victory, an asset.

It is the difference between setting out to cross an ocean and setting out to build a boat.

If my objective is to cross the ocean, everything is justified by the crossing. It does not matter that it endangeres my health, or that I need to leave my family for months, or that the whole thing rests on my own capacity to endure — because victory is defined as arriving on the other side.

In the other philosophy, the goal is not the crossing. The goal is a vessel that can make the crossing and come back, ten times a year. And it puts you in a completely different frame of mind, and you build completely differently, from the first day.

There is also a version of this that runs through the rest of your life, and I would be dishonest if I left it out.

Most of us hold a vision for our personal lives that is a moment, or a set of moments. When I find my partner. When I have the house. When I no longer have to work. When I have the car. When the children get into that school.

None of those moments hold. Within a few months of arriving they become normal, and they stop having any effect on how your life actually feels.

A marriage should not be a moment. It should be a machine that feeds you across a lifetime.

Which means that the vision for a life is not an event either. It is the set of repeatable processes that produce love, safety, and a sense of meaning predictably. That is what the default should be. Maybe you can feel happy for a short while by betraying your spouse with a person that you are attracted to today. Does that build a machine that produces love, safety and fulfilment in your life repeatedly?

Obviously not.

### 5

So what does a vision written this way actually look like on paper?

I call it the **Ordinary Great Quarter** — or the Ordinary Great Year, depending on how long your business takes to complete one meaningful cycle.

> **Vision is the quantified description of what the machine should produce, without extraordinary intervention, during its shortest meaningful and reasonably predictable operating cycle.**

Two words in that name are doing all the work.

*Great*, because this should be genuinely ambitious. Nothing here is an argument for a small life or a small company.

*Ordinary*, because if producing it requires anybody to perform a miracle, then it is not a default. It is a stunt, and stunts do not repeat.

Here is a filled-in example, for a publishing house.

> **In an ordinary great year, without extraordinary intervention, our machine normally produces:**
>
> - Revenue — €38–42M per year
> - Operating profit — €7.5–8.5M per year
> - International bestsellers — 4 per year
> - New bookstores reaching operating standard — 4 per year
> - Major authors signed — 1 per year
>
> **We count these outputs as success only when they are produced within the following non-negotiable constraints:**
>
> - *Simplicity and leverage* — no more than 100 full-time employees
> - *Economic independence* — net debt does not exceed 1× EBITDA
> - *Portfolio resilience* — no single author represents more than 15% of annual gross profit
> - *Reader trust* — no title is published without passing the defined editorial quality gate

Several things in that document are deliberate, and each of them is the answer to a mistake I made for years.

**It is quantified.** Not because numbers are sacred, but because a vision that cannot be compared to reality cannot be used in management, and a vision that cannot be used in management is a slogan.

**The outputs are ranges, not points.** Reality has weather in it. Markets move, suppliers fail, people leave, a competitor does something unexpected. A default that cannot survive an ordinary bad month is not a description of a machine — it is a wish with a decimal place. The range is where the honesty lives.

**It says *without extraordinary intervention*.** This is the phrase that makes the whole thing work, and it is the one people delete first. If the four bestsellers only happen when the founder personally spends nine months on them, the machine does not produce four bestsellers. The founder produces four bestsellers, and the founder is not a machine, the founder is a person with a finite number of years.

**It contains more than one indicator.** A single number can always be bought at the expense of something else. Revenue can be purchased with margin. Growth can be purchased with debt. Bestsellers can be purchased by neglecting the ordinary volume of books that pays the rent — which is exactly why the ordinary volume belongs in the document too, on its own line, where it can be seen. A vision with one line in it is an invitation to game it.

The line most people stall on is the bestsellers, because it looks like the one thing a publisher cannot promise. And the instinct underneath that is worth examining, because it is usually wrong.

Publishing, like every industry, tells a romantic story about its own unpredictability. But if you sign authors who already have an audience, on subjects that are demonstrably being read this year, four bestsellers out of a full list is not a miracle. It is close to engineerable. Not guaranteed — close. The genuinely unpredictable portion of most industries is much smaller than the people inside them enjoy believing, and a good deal of what gets called luck is simply a cause nobody has bothered to identify.

Where the market really is out of your hands, that is what the ranges are for. Forty-two million at the top and thirty-eight at the bottom is not vagueness. It is the amount of weather your machine is built to absorb without anybody panicking.

### 6

Now the second half of that document, which almost nobody writes, and which is in my experience the most valuable part.

The constraints.

Every company on earth says it values quality. Integrity. Simplicity. Independence. Those words are on the wall in the reception area, in the induction deck, on the careers page. And they mean precisely nothing, because a value that has never been converted into a boundary is not a value. It is a preference you hold when it is convenient.

> **Constraints are the operational expression of values.**

*Reader trust* is a word. *No title is published without passing the editorial quality gate* is a management instrument. One of them can be checked. One of them can stop a decision that would otherwise have been made. One of them will cost you money in a specific quarter that you can name in advance.

Here is what the absence of one costs.

For a few years around the turn of the millennium, Krispy Kreme was the most exciting retail brand in America. Its stores were built like small theatres — you could watch the doughnuts float down a river of hot oil and pass under a curtain of glaze — and a neon sign in the window lit up when a fresh batch came out: HOT DOUGHNUTS NOW. People camped overnight for new store openings. In tents. In Ohio, in February. The company went public in 2000 and the stock rose more than five hundred percent while the rest of the market was being incinerated by the dot-com collapse.

I want to be careful here, because the easy reading of what happened next is wrong, and the easy reading is the one everybody uses.

Krispy Kreme did not have a bad machine. It had an excellent one. The production line worked. The product was genuinely remarkable and genuinely consistent. The store design did exactly what it was built to do. If you had audited that company in 2001 against everything in the previous chapter, it would have passed.

What it did not have was a limit.

In 2000 there were about 140 stores. By 2004 there were around 360, with hundreds more announced, plus doughnuts on the shelf of every supermarket and filling station the company could reach. And in 2004 it reported its first quarterly loss, then another; same-store sales fell about twenty percent; franchisees who had paid for territories said publicly that head office was putting new stores on top of existing ones; roughly two and a half billion dollars of value evaporated and about a quarter of the stores closed.

The standard explanation is that they grew too fast, which is not wrong, exactly — it is just useless. Every company that fails while growing is described afterwards as having grown too fast, in the same way that every man who drowns is described as having gone too far out. It tells you the outcome. It does not tell you the mechanism.

The mechanism is that what made Krispy Kreme extraordinary was never the doughnut alone. It was a combination: a remarkable product, a theatrical store, a real event when a batch came out — and the fact that there was one location within a hundred miles, so getting there was an occasion.

The occasion was load-bearing. It was not a side effect of scarcity. It *was* the scarcity. And scarcity is the one ingredient in that recipe that cannot survive unlimited multiplication.

So the company multiplied everything it could see and dissolved the thing it could not, because the thing it could not see was the only part that limited growth.

Now ask the question this chapter is built on. What would have had to be written down, in 2000, for that not to happen?

Not a better process. Not a better product. A **constraint** — a line in the vision saying something like *no more than one location per X people within a Y-mile radius*, or *wholesale never exceeds Z percent of volume*. A boundary that would have cost real, visible, forecastable money in a specific quarter, and that somebody would have had to be told no because of.

That constraint was never written, and so there was no instrument in the company capable of refusing the next store. Not because the leadership was greedy. Because nothing in the vision was shaped to say **enough**.

Now look at what a single well-chosen constraint does when it *is* written down.

Costco caps its markup on branded goods at fourteen percent. On its own Kirkland products, fifteen. Not as a target — as a ceiling. If a buyer negotiates a spectacular deal, the saving is not converted into margin. It is converted into a lower shelf price, because the ceiling does not move. Ordinary retail markups run from twenty-five to fifty percent and up. Costco's actual average across the store, by its own reporting, sits around eleven.

The rule appears in no regulatory filing. It is internal, which makes it more interesting rather than less, because nobody outside forces them to keep it. It has been kept, without wavering, since Jim Sinegal ran the company.

There is a story about Sinegal and a supplier who brought him a deal that would have let Costco keep a few extra points of margin without raising the shelf price. Free money. He refused, and his reasoning was that if you allow yourself to do it once you will do it again, the number will creep, and in ten years you will be an ordinary retailer with a nice story about your founder. He described it in the language of addiction. Raise the price three cents because you can, and you will need the next three cents.

Now watch the cascade.

Because Costco cannot make money on markup, it has to make money somewhere else — so the profit engine becomes the membership fee, which structurally requires the whole company to make renewing feel obviously correct. The renewal rate runs above ninety percent.

Because margin per item is capped, the only way to grow gross profit is volume — so the company must carry a small number of items in enormous quantities. Costco carries around four thousand items where a supermarket carries fifty thousand.

Because it needs volume on few items, it must be brutal with suppliers, and suppliers accept it because the orders are absurd.

Because shelf price is protected, all the cost pressure lands on operations — concrete floors, pallets, no bags, almost no advertising.

One constraint, and it has determined the product range, the profit model, the supplier relationship, the store design, and the marketing strategy of a company with a quarter of a million employees.

That is a value that became a machine.

### 7

Which brings us to the test — the one thing that tells you whether you have written a vision or a slogan.

> **A vision becomes useful on the day it can refuse an attractive opportunity.**

I am not against slogans. Slogans are extremely useful in marketing, in branding, in a keynote. There is nothing dishonest about them. They are simply not usable in management, and management is where the slogan either becomes true or does not.

If an opportunity arrives that would add eight million in revenue but requires going to a hundred and forty employees, and your vision says one hundred — then your vision has just done something no motivational sentence has ever done in the history of commerce. It has made a decision, and it has made it before the room got emotional.

The most extreme demonstration of this I know comes from a French kitchen.

Sébastien Bras runs Le Suquet, in Laguiole, high in the Aubrac in southern France. It is not a restaurant you pass by; you drive up into the hills to reach it. His father built it, and it had held three Michelin stars for eighteen years — a run that puts it among the most consistently excellent restaurants on earth.

In September 2017, Bras made a video and asked Michelin to remove Le Suquet from the guide.

He was not in trouble. He had not lost anything. He was fifty, at the height of his powers, running one of the great restaurants in France. He simply did not want to keep paying the price.

What he described was very precise. Michelin inspectors arrive unannounced, two or three times a year. Which means every plate that leaves the kitchen might be the plate being judged. Five hundred meals go out. Any one of them could be the one that costs you the thing your family has held for eighteen years.

*You're inspected two or three times a year, you never know when. Every meal that goes out could be inspected.* And then: *I will be able to feel free.*

Michelin eventually agreed. Le Suquet did not appear in the 2018 guide — the first time in the guide's history a restaurant had been allowed to withdraw.

He did not close. He did not lower his standards; by every account the food did not get worse. He removed a constraint that was not his — one imposed by an external verdict, from an audience he had not chosen, that had nothing to do with what his machine was actually built to produce.

Almost everyone reading this has a Michelin star somewhere in their business. A metric you never chose, imposed by an audience you never selected, that you are now organizing your life around defending.

### 8

Now I want to deal with something that makes me genuinely angry, because it has damaged a very large number of good people.

There is a widely held belief — pushed hardest by people who make a living from stages — that a vision is only worthy if it will change the world.

I think it is idiotic. I think it is completely unrealistic. And frankly, I think it is one of the ways that toxic people demonstrate moral superiority over everyone else in the room.

A vision can be a default output that gives one human being a certain income while spending a certain amount of time in the business. *I want my machine to produce thirty thousand a month in profit and I want to work four days a week.*

Why is that a bad vision, exactly? Because it does not change the world? How the hell would you know? What if that entrepreneur becomes the launchpad for someone who genuinely does change the world? What if he helps, at some point, one person who helps another person who does? A teacher can make a difference so large in the life of one child that the child cures a disease.

So get off the high horse about moonshots being the only thing that counts.

Now, let me be careful, because there is a version of this argument that is also wrong. I am not saying it is bad to want to change the world. Of course it is not bad. The tragedy is not ambition. The tragedy is people who choose a vision that is not theirs, and spend a life chasing someone else's dream, and arrive with nothing.

A billion-euro vision, or getting to Mars, or changing how people do some particular thing, does not make you more valuable than the person who wants to serve an exceptional meal to a hundred people a day in his restaurant. Maybe that restaurant feeds the next Einstein. Maybe it gives him the place where he thinks, detaches from the work, and comes back more productive.

Too many people choose their vision according to what is being celebrated in society at that moment. Sometimes it is rock stars. Sometimes athletes. For the last fifteen years it has been entrepreneurs.

And the world has a habit: after it lifts a category of people onto a pedestal, it knocks them down. It celebrated entrepreneurship, elevated founders, and then discovered they were not perfect — that they are corruptible, that they contain both kindness and cruelty, that they have egos and needs that would often have been better kept private. And then it demonized exactly the category it had elevated.

For people who chose entrepreneurship because it genuinely fulfils them, that is not a problem. For those who chose it because it was in fashion, everything collapses when the wheel turns.

We cannot guess the future or predict trends with any accuracy. But we can absolutely decide what default output we want our machine to produce. And that is a far more honest way of relating to desire.

There is a line from Shane Parrish that I keep coming back to:

> *Wisdom is wanting what's worth getting in the first place.*

Which leads to what I think is the most valuable and least used word in world entrepreneurship right now.

**Enough.**

A business does not have to grow forever. It can produce, consistently and repeatably, a predictable result that its community actually needs. The dry mouth that cannot be satisfied by any quantity of water is not a sign of ambition. It is a symptom. That is what cancer does — growth until suicide.

In the year 578, a Korean carpenter named Shigemitsu Kongō was invited to Japan to build the country's first Buddhist temple. The company he founded to do that work, Kongō Gumi, was still operating in 2005.

Fourteen hundred and twenty-eight years. Forty generations. It survived the rise and fall of the samurai class, the Mongol invasions, the closing of Japan, the reopening of Japan, the Meiji Restoration, two world wars, the firebombing of Osaka, and the near-total destruction of Japanese Buddhism as a state institution.

The way it survived is itself a lesson. It did one thing — built and repaired Buddhist temples, using traditional joinery, at very high quality. When temple construction collapsed during the war, they made coffins. When a generation had no capable son, they adopted one, or leadership passed to a son-in-law who took the family name. Their guidance to successive generations reads almost like a constraints list: stay in your trade, do not drink to excess, treat people fairly, do not take on more work than you can do well, never guarantee another man's debt.

For fourteen centuries the machine produced its default output. Not spectacular growth. Temples, well built, repeatedly, at a cadence, in a market that renewed itself because wooden temples need rebuilding.

Then, in the 1980s, Japan had a property bubble. Kongō Gumi borrowed heavily and moved into real estate. Everybody was doing it. By the standards of 1988, this was obviously correct — a fourteen-hundred-year-old company finally scaling.

The bubble burst in 1992. The assets securing the debt collapsed. The debt did not. By 2006 the company owed something on the order of three hundred and forty-three million dollars, could not service it, and was liquidated.

Fourteen hundred years of enough, ended by roughly fifteen years of more.

And here is the seduction, stated precisely, because it is operating in every business that has found something that works. The temple business was not failing. It was producing exactly what it had always produced. The problem is that in 1988 it looked *insufficient* — not against its own history, and not against the needs of the people it served, but against what other people were making that year.

Nobody at Kongō Gumi asked whether the machine was working. They asked whether it was growing.

### 9

There is a price for attaching your identity to a verdict rather than to a machine, and I want to name it honestly, because it is not only financial.

Bernard Loiseau was one of the great chefs of France. By 2003 he had three Michelin stars at his restaurant in Saulieu, in Burgundy, which he had built from a provincial roadside inn into a destination. He had held a peak score of 19.5 out of 20 from the rival guide GaultMillau. He had listed his company on the stock exchange, the first French chef ever to do so.

He had, in the most literal sense, won. And he had spent his entire adult life understanding himself as a man who had won, which is a far more fragile thing.

In early 2003, two blows landed close together. GaultMillau cut his score from 19 to 17. And a critic at *Le Figaro* wrote that his cooking had slipped. Neither of these was Michelin. His three stars were intact. But a rumour started in the French food world that Michelin had privately warned him he was in danger of losing the third.

There is no evidence Michelin ever did. When the 2003 Guide Rouge was published, Loiseau still had his three stars.

It was published on the day of his funeral. On 24 February 2003 he went home in the afternoon and shot himself. He was fifty-two.

I want to be careful. A suicide has causes no outsider is entitled to summarize, and Loiseau had a documented history of depression. It would be obscene to reduce a man's death to a management lesson.

But the reason chefs across France recognized something in that story is that they were all living inside the same structure and they knew it.

A Michelin star is not a description of what your kitchen can reliably produce. It is a verdict, delivered annually, by anonymous strangers, on whether you are still who you were last year. It cannot be earned permanently. It can only be defended. And once your identity is fused to it, every service of every day for the rest of your career carries the possibility of losing the thing that makes you a person.

On the day he died, the machine was working. The food was excellent. The guests were coming. The stars were, in fact, still there.

None of it was load-bearing, because his sense of his own value had never been attached to the machine. It had been attached to the verdict.

### 10

In 1997, two men in California put a compact disc inside a greetings card, addressed the card to themselves, and posted it.

That was the founding experiment of Netflix. Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph had an idea for a business that depended entirely on a question nobody had answered — whether a disc survives the American postal system — and rather than assume, they spent the price of a stamp.

It arrived intact. So they built the thing.

The thing they built was this: you go to a website, choose a film, and a DVD arrives in your letterbox in a red envelope. You watch it. You post it back. There was no subscription at first. It was rental by the disc, with due dates, exactly like the video shop except that the shop was the mail.

If you had asked them that year to describe their vision, and if they had answered honestly rather than for an investor, it would not have been about transforming global entertainment. It would have been closer to: *get a film to somebody's door, cheaply, more conveniently than driving to a shop, and get the disc back.*

That was the entire ambition. And it is worth reading the sequence of what came afterwards, because the sequence is the whole argument.

The subscription came later. Removing due dates came later. The recommendation engine came later. Streaming did not launch until 2007 — ten years in — and even then it was a free extra bolted onto the DVD subscription, because nobody inside the company believed customers would give up the discs. The first original series aired in 2012, fifteen years after the greetings card.

Fifteen years of a machine producing its default output, reliably, and paying for the discovery of what the machine should become next.

Ten years later and four hundred miles down the coast, two designers in San Francisco could not make rent.

There was a large industrial design conference in town and every hotel in the city was full. They had three air mattresses and a floor. So they built a single-page website and called it airbedandbreakfast.com, and it was aimed at exactly one market: people attending large conferences in cities without enough hotel rooms.

That was the vision. Not *belong anywhere*. Conference overflow accommodation.

Three people stayed. They made about a thousand dollars. The following year they went to the Democratic National Convention in Denver, because conferences were still the model, and they funded that year by selling novelty breakfast cereal in commemorative boxes.

It took them years to work out that the real machine had nothing to do with conferences at all.

Both of these companies now get described as the work of founders who saw the future. They did not see the future. What they did was define, at each stage, what their machine should be able to produce by default *at that stage* — build it until it produced that reliably — and then let contact with reality tell them what the next default should be.

Which brings me to the worry that sits underneath everything in this chapter, and that I would have if I were reading it.

If your vision is only ever the next level of default output, are you not just wandering? Where is the north star? Does a company not need one enormous fixed thing on the horizon to steer by?

Look again at what those two companies actually did, and notice that the direction never wavered. Netflix was, from the greetings card to the streaming platform, a company for getting a film in front of a person with as little friction as possible. Airbnb was, from three air mattresses onward, a company for turning space people already own into places other people can stay. What changed in both cases was not the direction. It was the description of the next normal.

That is not zig-zagging.

Zig-zagging is what happens when you have no defined default at all — when there is no written description of what the machine should produce, so every attractive opportunity looks equally valid, nothing ever becomes reliable enough to fund the next question, and the company arrives at its tenth year with ten years of experience and no capability.

The company with a defined default output looks slower from outside. It is doing one thing until that thing is boring, then choosing the next one deliberately. The company without one looks busier, and is.

And this also answers the question that founders ask me most often about this, which is how far ahead they are supposed to look.

As far as you can actually see. That is the whole answer, and it is more honest than the alternative. You build a machine that produces a certain economic output with thirty people. When it does that without anybody being heroic, you have arrived, and you raise the vision to the next level.

Because that is the other thing a default output gives you that a summit never can. It tells you when you have got there.

With a summit, arrival is a moment, and the moment passes, and you are left standing on it wondering what happens now — which is where this chapter began, with a man on a stage being asked what the moon was like.

With a default, arrival has a test, and the test is unglamorous and completely unambiguous. You have reached your vision when the machine produces the vision's result repeatably and predictably, without extraordinary intervention. Not when you hit the number. When hitting the number has stopped being interesting.

That is a strange sort of finish line, because crossing it feels like nothing at all. Nobody cheers. There is no photograph.

There is just a quarter in which the thing you once thought was impossible happened on schedule, and nobody in the building thought it was worth mentioning.

### 11

There is one last question, and it is the one I now ask before anything else, because I have seen what happens to the people who skip it.

> **Can the business default be produced without destroying the life default?**

Most founders set out what they want without ever calculating what it will cost. We borrow from the life to pay the business, continuously, and mostly without noticing. That would not be a problem if we did it consciously. But most of us do not, and then one day the invoice arrives, and we look at it and realize it was not worth it.

Nothing in life is free. We pay for everything. The difference between the people who end up fulfilled and the people who end up bitter is that the fulfilled ones knew the price and accepted it *before* the invoice showed up.

So a vision for a business has to be tested against a vision for a life, and if they cannot coexist, then one of them has to change — and the honest answer is usually not the one the founder wants.

Which means you need the second document. Not a list of things you will own. A description of the default output of your life: the reality you want to be normal in it, produced repeatedly, by processes you have actually built.

The classical vision says: *one day, we will achieve something extraordinary.*

A machine-based vision says: *we will build a system in which what once looked extraordinary becomes a normal, repeatable output.*

Which is a fine sentence. And it raises the obvious question, which is what exactly a machine *is*, what is allowed inside it, how something earns its way in, and what happens to the companies that let things in too early.

Which is the subject of the next chapter, and the longest one in this book.
