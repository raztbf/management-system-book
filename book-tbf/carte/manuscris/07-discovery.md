# 7. Discovery

### The Houseboat, the Bicycle Shop, and the Question Nobody's Department Is Excused From

---

### 1

On 8 December 1903, a houseboat sat in the middle of the Potomac River with the future of aviation bolted to its roof.

The machine on top was called the Great Aerodrome, and it belonged to Samuel Pierpont Langley, Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution — which is to say, the most institutionally powerful scientist in the United States. He had fifty thousand dollars from the War Department and further funds from the Smithsonian on top of that. He had professional machinists. He had a purpose-built catapult. He had a pilot, Charles Manly, sitting in the frame. And he had the national press, invited personally, standing on the riverbank in December, waiting.

The catapult fired. The Great Aerodrome left the houseboat, folded in on itself, and dropped into the river. Manly was pulled out of the water.

It was the second time this had happened in nine weeks. Langley never tried again. The newspapers were merciless, and when he died three years later, his obituaries mentioned the Potomac.

Nine days after that second crash, on a windy beach in North Carolina, two brothers who repaired bicycles for a living flew.

The part everybody knows is the flying. The part almost nobody knows is the arithmetic. Between 1900 and 1903, Wilbur and Orville Wright spent — on everything: materials, the engine, every trip to Kitty Hawk and back — a little under one thousand dollars, paid out of the profits of a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio. Langley had roughly a hundred times the money, and lost.

Told that way, it is a satisfying story about underdogs, and told that way it is almost useless. The Wrights did not win by being plucky, and they did not win by being geniuses in a shed. They won because of what they had been doing for two years in the back of the bicycle shop — the part nobody photographed, because no newspaper thought it was news.

In October 1901, they built a wind tunnel. Six feet long. The balances inside it — the instruments that measured lift against drag — were made from bicycle spokes and scrap metal. Over the following weeks they ran model wings through it by the dozen — some two hundred shapes tested, formal data recorded on about fifty.

They were not building an aircraft. **They were building a machine that produces knowledge about aircraft.**

And then that little machine produced something that should stop you cold. Every serious person working on flight was using the same published lift tables, and inside those tables sat a value called Smeaton's coefficient — 0.005, in continuous use since the 1700s. The Wrights' 1901 glider was producing about a third of the lift the tables promised, and the obvious conclusion was that the brothers had built their glider wrong. Instead, they checked the number. They derived the coefficient from their own measurements and got 0.0033.

Modern aerodynamicists confirm they were right. An entire field had been building flying machines on top of a figure that nobody had verified for a hundred and fifty years.

On 17 December 1903, the Wright Flyer left the ground for twelve seconds and a hundred and twenty feet. Then — and this is the detail that matters more than the first flight — it did it three more times *that day*, the fourth flight lasting fifty-nine seconds. By 1905 their third machine could stay up for thirty-nine minutes and circle a field.

Langley was trying to achieve a flight. The Wrights were building something that produces flights.

> **The expensive machine on the houseboat was a bet. The cheap machine in the bicycle shop was an instrument. One of them could only be right once.**

### 2

The last chapter left you with a sheet of paper and a column full of checkboxes, and most of those boxes said the same word: **Discovery.**

That word is where the restaurant company's afternoon ended and where this chapter begins. Nine of their thirteen bottlenecks routed to it — nine places where the honest answer was *nobody in this company knows how.* Nobody knew how to make portions come out right at rush volume without a director standing in the kitchen. Nobody knew what would bring a customer back a third time in a month. Nobody knew how to open a restaurant without the development director's dinners and dawns.

The old system had an answer for that situation, and you watched it fail for an entire morning: convert the unknown into a number, attach a name and a bonus, and push. This system's answer is the subject of this chapter. When you do not know how, you do not commit to a result. **You build the bicycle shop.** You construct the small, cheap instrument that produces knowledge about your business — and you run it until the unknown becomes known, at which point it goes into the machine and stops needing you.

Everything that follows is the anatomy of that instrument. And I want to build it in front of you the same way we built it in that guesthouse — on a real experiment, field by field — because the worksheet is one page, and one page is easy to nod at and impossible to run a company on until you have seen why every line is there.

---

### 3

Take the boldest thing that came out of the restaurant audit: the menu.

It did not come from a gap. It came from the operations director finishing the sentence *I wonder what would happen if…* — the one hypothesis on the table that had been whispering for years through the waste numbers and the kitchen chaos, and that nobody had dared say out loud, because under the old system a smaller menu sounds like smaller ambition. *What would happen if we cut half the menu and kept only the top-selling fifty percent?*

Under the old system, here is what that idea would have become — if it survived at all: an objective. *Simplify the menu across the network by Q3.* An owner, a deadline, a war. Instead, we spent forty minutes turning it into an experiment, and the forty minutes consisted of filling in one sheet.

![Experiment worksheet](imagini/experiment.pdf)

**Process.** Where in the machine does this experiment live? Kitchen production and the menu itself. One line — but that line already does something the old system never did: it declares which part of the machine is being investigated, so that in ninety days nobody is arguing about what the test was *about*.

**Hypothesis.** This is the field that makes or breaks the whole instrument, and it has a required grammar: *if… then…* with numbers in it. Not *let's try a smaller menu and see* — that is a wish with a haircut. The team's final version: **if we cut the menu of one stable location to its top-selling fifty percent, then within eight weeks profitability rises by at least ten percent, while sales fall by no more than ten.** Look at what those two thresholds do. They were chosen *before* the result existed — which means the result cannot be negotiated *after* it exists. A sales drop of nine percent is survival; eleven percent is invalidation. The line between success and failure was drawn while nobody knew which side they would land on, which is the only time human beings draw honest lines.

**Source.** Where did this idea come from? The waste data, and one director's years of frustration. Two words on a sheet — and, I promise you, the most underrated field on it. Fill it in for two years and your audit gains a second layer: you learn *whose* instincts, and *which kinds* of sources — customer complaints, kitchen staff, competitors, data, hunches — actually produce validated knowledge in your company, and which reliably produce noise. Companies measure everything about their operations and nothing about their own judgment. This field is how you start.

**KPIs.** What we will measure: sales, profitability, waste, average ticket, complaints mentioning missing dishes. Decided now, tracked from day one — because measures chosen after the fact are not measurements, they are advocacy.

**Constraints.** The rules we will not break in order to win, and the field that protects everything the first five chapters built: **one location, a stable one, eight weeks, and the network's menu does not move.** However promising the early numbers look. However excited anybody gets.

**Method.** How it actually runs: which location, how the cut list is chosen, how customers are told, when the data is read.

And then the footer — **owner, team, deadline** — which surprises everybody, because it looks exactly like an objective. It is meant to. Nothing about this system is an argument for vagueness, and there is nothing soft about an experiment with your name on it and eight weeks on the clock. The difference between this sheet and an objective is not the pressure. It is *what is owed at the deadline*. The operations director did not owe the company a smaller menu that works. He owed it **an answer** — and an answer, unlike a result nobody knows how to produce, sits entirely within his power to deliver. That is the property we insisted on for every project planned in that guesthouse, and it is the property that separates this chapter from the one before it: the audit finds the questions; the experiment is the only honest way to answer one.

---

### 4

Now let me defend the two fields everybody wants to skip, because they are the two that do all the work.

The thresholds first. Why insist on *at least ten percent* and *no more than ten* before the data exists? Because of what I told you at the end of the last chapter: people fall in love with their experiments. The moment the early numbers arrive, the experiment's owner becomes its lawyer — and a result without a pre-agreed threshold can be argued into anything. Sales down fourteen percent? *But think of the kitchen simplicity.* Profit up only three? *Give it more time.* The threshold is a message from the person you were before you saw the data — the only version of you with no case to plead — to the person you become afterwards. Write it down while you are still neutral, because you will never be neutral again.

And the comparison. The menu experiment ran in *one* location while the rest of the network ran unchanged — and that design choice, which looks like caution, is actually the entire epistemology. Here is why, in one story. When Bank of America wanted to know whether televisions in branch queues made waiting feel shorter, they installed them in a test branch and measured: the share of customers overestimating their wait fell from thirty-two percent to fifteen. A clear win — until you look at the control branch, where nothing had been installed and overestimation had *risen*, from fifteen to twenty-six percent, over the same period. Something seasonal was happening to everybody. Without the untouched branch, the bank would have booked a modest improvement; against it, the effect was enormous. Run your test on everything at once and you have no untouched branch — no way to distinguish what you did from what the quarter did. This is why the restaurant network's menu did not move: the other locations were not being protected from the experiment. **They were the experiment's ruler.**

One more honest number belongs here, because I used to teach the opposite. Even a clean, well-measured experiment can lie. When researchers examined thousands of real corporate experiments, they found that roughly **one in five interventions that reach statistical significance turn out to be ineffective when actually deployed.** The signal was real; it just did not survive contact with scale, seasons and ordinary life. This is not a reason to distrust experiments — it is the reason the ladder from the fourth chapter has a rung called Level 2. *It worked once* is a fact about one place and one month. It becomes knowledge when it repeats. You already saw me apply this rule to my own best result: a first run that registers two thousand companies is a miracle; only the second run, twelve months later, tells you whether you own a method or a memory of good weather.

### 5

If all of this sounds like something invented by the software industry — something that needs platforms and dashboards and data scientists — I want to take you to a McDonald's in Cincinnati, in 1962, where one of the most consequential experiments in restaurant history was run in a single day, on paper, by men who had never heard the word *A/B test*.

Lou Groen ran the only McDonald's franchise in a heavily Catholic neighbourhood, and he was slowly going broke, because in that era observant Catholics did not eat meat on Fridays — and his entire menu was meat. His restaurant emptied out one day in seven. Groen's proposal to headquarters: a fish sandwich.

Ray Kroc hated it. *"I don't want my stores stunk up with the smell of fish,"* he told Groen — and Kroc, being Kroc, had his own idea for the meatless Friday problem: the Hula Burger. A slice of grilled pineapple with cheese, on a cold bun. He was genuinely convinced it would win.

Now stop and look at this situation with the eyes of the last chapter, because you have seen it before. The most powerful man in the company, a founder with legendary instincts, is certain. A local operator, standing closest to the actual customer, disagrees. In the old quarterly system, this ends one way: the senior opinion wins, the junior one is noted for its passion, and the company bets a menu on rank.

That is not what happened. What happened is that Kroc and Groen made a deal that is, in structure, exactly the worksheet you just watched the restaurant team fill in: **both sandwiches go on sale, same day — Good Friday — head to head, and the numbers decide.** A hypothesis each, one test, thresholds nobody could argue with afterwards.

The Hula Burger sold six. The Filet-O-Fish sold three hundred and fifty.

Kroc paid up — he had also bet a colleague a new suit — and the Filet-O-Fish went national, where it has now outlived almost everything else on that menu by sixty years. And the detail I want you to keep is not the fish. It is that **the man who was wrong was the man with the best instincts in the industry.** Ray Kroc had built the most successful restaurant company on earth on those instincts. On this question, standing next to a franchisee with a spreadsheet's worth of Friday receipts, he was not just wrong — he was wrong by a factor of nearly sixty. Booking.com would eventually measure the same thing at industrial scale: nine out of ten ideas held by experienced, well-informed experts fail when tested. The lesson is not that experts are fools. It is that **nobody's confidence — including yours, including your best director's — is data.** And the more senior the opinion, the more expensive it is to be wrong about, which means the more senior the opinion, the more it needs the test.

One day. Two sandwiches. No platform, no statisticians, a counter and a till. If a 1962 hamburger stand could afford the experiment, your company can.

### 6

Here is the question the founders asked next, and it is the question every founder asks once the sheet is understood: *fine — but how many of these do we run? When? Who decides the pace?*

The instinct behind the question is that discovery is weather — it arrives when it arrives, and scheduling it is a category error. The first chapter already showed you the man who disproved that in 1876: Edison, announcing a minor invention every ten days and a big thing every six months, then building Menlo Park so the promise could keep itself — the machine shop downstairs so a sketch could be an object by afternoon, the failures bound into notebooks as inventory. You cannot schedule an insight. **You can absolutely schedule the attempts** — and once the attempts run on a cadence, the insights start arriving on something disturbingly close to a timetable.

But cadence has a price of admission, and it is the part Edison's story hides because he built it right from the start: **the cost of a single attempt determines how many attempts you can afford, and the number of attempts determines how fast you learn.** The cleanest demonstration I know ran for eighteen years over the English Channel and ended in the California desert.

In 1959, a British industrialist named Henry Kremer offered a prize — eventually fifty thousand pounds — for the first human-powered aircraft to fly a figure of eight around two markers half a mile apart. The best aeronautical engineers in Britain attacked it for eighteen years, and their aircraft were magnificent: years in the building, exquisitely crafted, aerodynamically superb. And that was precisely the trap. A machine that takes years to build cannot afford to crash — and the one question that mattered, *how does a machine this light and slow turn?*, could only be answered by crashing. So each crash cost months, sometimes an entire programme, and in eighteen years the problem barely moved.

Paul MacCready, an American engineer a hundred thousand dollars in debt from a loan guarantee, read the problem differently. His insight was not aerodynamic — everybody knew the wings had to be huge and the speed absurdly low. His insight was that **the winning aircraft would be the one that could be wrong the most times per week.** The Gossamer Condor was, by every standard of the industry, a disgrace: aluminium tubes, Mylar film, piano wire, cardboard and tape. It crashed constantly. A crash in the morning was flying again by the afternoon; repairs were made with Scotch tape; the tail was once adjusted by taping a piece of card to it. The aircraft mutated week by week into something no committee would ever have drawn, and in August 1977 — about a year after MacCready started — a cyclist named Bryan Allen pedalled it around the figure of eight and ended eighteen years of failure in seven minutes. The Condor hangs in the Smithsonian now, tape and all, among beautiful machines that never flew the course.

> **The prize was not won by the team with the best answer. It was won by the team that could afford the most questions.**

That sentence is the design brief for your discovery function. Before you ask *what should we test*, ask *what does one attempt cost us* — in money, in time, in operational risk, in personal humiliation. Every euro and every week you strip out of the cost of a single experiment multiplies the number you can run, and the number you run is the speed at which your company learns. The restaurant team's menu test cost one location's menu for eight weeks. Their old method of learning — roll it out everywhere and see — would have bet the network. Same curiosity. A hundred times the price per question.

### 7

At this point, somebody in every audience raises the objection I have heard for twenty years, and it deserves a serious answer: *this works for websites and hamburgers. My work is creative. You cannot systematize taste.*

The film business agrees with you. The most famous sentence ever written about it is four words long, from William Goldman — a screenwriter with two Academy Awards: **"Nobody knows anything."** He meant it precisely: nobody who greenlights films can know which will work; every studio has passed on a phenomenon and financed a catastrophe. An entire industry, organized for a century around the official position that in creative work, the cause cannot be owned.

Now consider what happened at a computer graphics company in Emeryville, California, between 1995 and 2010. *Toy Story. A Bug's Life. Toy Story 2. Monsters, Inc. Finding Nemo. The Incredibles. Cars. Ratatouille. WALL·E. Up. Toy Story 3.* Eleven feature films, every one profitable, nearly every one acclaimed — a run without precedent in the history of the medium. And the man who ran Pixar, Ed Catmull, is emphatic in his book about where that run did *not* come from. "In truth," he writes, "all of our movies suck at first. I don't mean this in the sense that I'm being self-effacing… I mean this in the sense that they suck."

Sit with that pair of facts. The most reliable creative organization ever built claims no advantage in ideas. Its advantage was a machine for converting a bad film into a good one — which is to say, a discovery loop, run on art.

Two mechanisms carried it, and both are process design, not talent. The Braintrust: directors and writers watching each other's unfinished work and saying exactly what they think — with one structural subtraction that makes the whole thing run: **the Braintrust has no authority.** It cannot mandate a single change. And that subtraction is not politeness; it is the same mechanism you saw on the experiment sheet. If the feedback cannot be imposed, hearing it costs the director nothing, so the director can afford to actually hear it. Remove the verdict, and the defence goes with it. The second mechanism, dailies: showing incomplete, ugly work to colleagues every single day — not when it is ready, *every day* — because when everyone sees everyone's bad work constantly, the shame of being seen mid-failure evaporates, and problems surface while they are still cheap.

Hollywood said the cause cannot be owned. What it actually meant was that nobody in Hollywood had built the machine for finding it. The objection about creative work has the truth exactly backwards: the more creative the work, the more the first attempt sucks — and therefore the more valuable the loop that converts attempts into knowledge. Your industry's version of *nobody knows anything* is not a law of nature. It is an unbuilt bicycle shop.

### 8

Back to the guesthouse, because the second experiment shows the one thing the menu test cannot: how you design for an idea that is *big*.

The marketing director's hypothesis — restaurants co-branded with influencers, their name on the front, their favourite dishes served only there — was not an eight-week tweak. Done carelessly, it could damage the brand, the P&L and a landlord relationship in one stroke. The old system would have handled that risk in one of two ways: kill the idea in the meeting, or bet the network on it with a launch plan. Watch instead what the constraints field did to it.

The test site was not a new location, and not a good one. It was **an existing restaurant that had run below the chain's profitability standard for more than two years** — a piece of the machine already misfiring, where the honest downside was small because the status quo was already a slow loss. The rebranding was budgeted at one month. And the influencer's upside was structured to protect the company: **thirty percent of that restaurant — after the costs were recovered.** Nothing paid up front for fame; the partner earns when the machine earns. Every one of those choices came off the constraints line of one worksheet, written by the same team that, twenty-four hours earlier, had planned by promising each other numbers.

You know the early result, because I could not resist telling you at the end of the last chapter: second month after launch, top three in the entire chain by sales — and the experiment *still open*, because one spectacular month is Level 1, and the sheet's deadline says another full quarter before anybody is allowed to call it knowledge. What I did not tell you is what the Source field recorded: this idea came from the director who, ninety days earlier, had spent her quarter assembling sixty slides of self-defence. The same person. The same talent. A different question — and I want to be precise about what changed, because it was not her courage. Under the old system, a big swing was a career bet: her name, her bonus, her standing, staked on a guess. Under this one, the company's exposure was one underperforming restaurant, and hers was nothing at all — *the hypothesis* would win or lose, not the person. People do not become bolder when you demand boldness. **They become bolder when you make boldness cheap.**

---

### 9

Which brings us to the discipline that makes all of this survivable, and it is the discipline everything in this book keeps returning to: where the experiments live.

Never inside the machine. Beside it. The menu test: one location, network untouched. The influencer test: one already-failing site, brand walled off behind a co-brand. The rule is old, and the best picture of it I know is from 1943, when Lockheed's Kelly Johnson was handed an impossible deadline for a jet fighter and there was no space in the factory. He rented a circus tent, next door to a plastics plant that stank so badly nobody would come near, and built the XP-80 in there in a hundred and forty-three days — while the main factory kept producing, undisturbed, the aircraft that paid for everything. The Skunk Works became the most productive advanced-development shop in aviation history, and it never moved into the main building, because the two kinds of work poison each other: the operation's discipline suffocates the laboratory, and the laboratory's chaos infects the operation.

The fourth chapter gave you the economics of this and the restaurant audit gave you the arithmetic: their machine, remember, was sixty-five percent rescue. A company in that condition does not earn the right to experiment *by* stabilizing the machine — it needs the experiments precisely to stabilize it. But it absolutely must run them at the edge, at experiment scale, where being wrong costs a fortnight and a corner of the business, not the quarter. **The machine pays for the laboratory. The laboratory is never allowed inside the machine.** Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing, and companies die of forgetting either one.

And when an experiment ends? Then the ladder from the fourth chapter takes over, and there are only three honest outcomes. The hypothesis is invalidated: you have erased a road from the map, permanently, for the price of a few weeks — file the sheet, because that sheet is inventory; Edison's notebooks were worth more than his patents. The hypothesis survives once: Level 1 — you repeat it, somewhere else, before it touches the machine, because one in five significant results is weather. Or it survives repetition: Level 2 — and now, and only now, it crosses into the machine, through the front door, as a validated process with an indicator and a standard, exactly the way the fifth chapter taught. At which point the most important thing happens, and it is the whole point of the system: **it stops being an experiment and starts being boring.** The menu, if it holds, will not be an initiative or a transformation. It will just be the menu. Reality, produced by default.

### 10

Before I close this chapter, I want to do something different with it, and I want to tell you why.

In twenty years of teaching this system, I have watched one belief survive every argument, every study and every worksheet: the quiet conviction that experiments are for marketing. Maybe sales. Perhaps a website. And so, in company after company, the discovery column of the audit fills up beautifully for the commercial departments — while production, HR, finance and logistics keep managing their unknowns the old way: with targets, opinions and rank. As if the laws of learning stopped at the marketing department's door.

So what follows is not a story. It is a field guide — a dozen real experiments, from companies large and small, deliberately drawn from the departments entrepreneurs believe are exempt. Do not read it the way you read the rest of this book. Browse it. Steal from it. Each entry is compressed to the four lines that matter — the hypothesis, the test, the result, the decision — which you will recognise as the skeleton of the experiment worksheet. That is not a coincidence. It is the point: every one of these, from a tax office to a truck fleet, fits on the same one page your operations director filled in for the menu.

**OPERATIONS · Kohl's — the terrifying hour.** In 2013, the American retail chain was hunting for cost cuts, and somebody proposed opening stores an hour later. Executives feared a sales collapse; the argument could have run for a year. *Hypothesis:* opening one hour later cuts operating costs without meaningfully hurting sales. *Test:* a controlled trial in a subset of stores, the rest as comparison. *Result:* no meaningful sales decline. *Decision:* later opening rolled out — a cost line removed from every store, forever, for the price of one disciplined test. Notice what actually got tested: not a growth idea, but a fear.

**LOGISTICS · UPS — the left turn.** Somebody at UPS asked a question that sounds like a joke: what if our trucks almost never turned left? Left turns mean idling against oncoming traffic — fuel, time, accidents. *Hypothesis:* routes minimizing left turns beat shortest routes on total cost. *Test:* piloted in one delivery centre in York, Pennsylvania, before touching the fleet. *Result:* validated and extended into the ORION routing system — over ten million gallons of fuel saved per year, roughly a hundred million miles, hundreds of millions of dollars annually. *Decision:* network-wide rollout. A pilot, not a lab trial — but the discipline is the same: one centre first, the fleet only after the numbers.

**HR · Trip.com (Ctrip) — working from home, properly tested.** While the rest of the world argued about remote work from opinion, this Chinese travel company ran the actual experiment, with Stanford economists: call-centre volunteers randomly assigned to home or office for nine months. *Hypothesis:* home working cuts costs without hurting performance. *Result:* performance *up* thirteen percent, attrition cut in half — and two twists nobody predicted: promotions of home workers fell (out of sight, out of mind), and when employees were later allowed to choose freely, half the home group came back to the office, while the gains of those who stayed nearly doubled. *Decision:* option rolled out company-wide — with choice, because the choosing turned out to be half the effect. The twists are the lesson: no opinion, however confident, would have found them.

**HR · Microsoft Japan — the four-day week.** August 2019: offices closed every Friday for a month, full pay, meetings capped at thirty minutes. *Hypothesis:* fewer hours need not mean less output. *Result:* sales per employee up 39.9% against the previous August, electricity down 23%, and 92% of employees in favour. *Decision:* further trials. I include it with its warning label attached: one month, no control group, several changes at once — this is a Level 1 result if ever there was one. Read it as an invitation to run your own, not as proof.

**RECRUITING · Google — the interview machine.** Google treated its own hiring folklore as a pile of untested hypotheses and ran them against years of performance data. *Result:* the celebrated brainteasers — how many golf balls fit in a bus — predicted nothing at all; structured interviews with shared scoring roughly doubled predictive accuracy; and four interviews delivered 86% of the achievable confidence, so every interview beyond the fourth was pure delay. *Decision:* brainteasers banned, loops capped, structure imposed. The department where every manager trusts their gut turned out to be the department where gut feel measured worst.

**MANAGEMENT · Google — Project Oxygen.** The same company then aimed an experiment at its own founding belief: that managers do not matter — that engineers need no supervision, only harder problems. They had once acted on that belief by abolishing engineering managers entirely. *Test:* years of internal data, teams compared across manager quality. *Result:* the belief lost. Good managers measurably moved retention, satisfaction and performance; the company reversed course and built its management training on the eight behaviours the data surfaced. The bravest entry on any experiment sheet is a conviction the founders hold dear.

**RETENTION · Zappos and Amazon — paying people to leave.** The strangest HR experiment in the field guide: offer every new hire — later, every warehouse employee, once a year — money to quit. Two thousand dollars, rising to five thousand, under a banner reading *Please Don't Take This Offer.* *Hypothesis:* an employee who stays only because leaving is inconvenient costs more than the cheque that would free them — and one who refuses the cheque has, in refusing it, chosen the company again. *Result:* Amazon has published no numbers beyond "a small percentage accept," so file the outcome as unverified. I include it anyway, for the design: it shows how wide the space of testable ideas really is, in the department everybody swears runs on intangibles.

**FINANCE · The British tax office — one sentence, £2.4 million.** The least experimental organisation imaginable — a tax authority — ran a randomised trial on the wording of its late-payment letters. *Hypothesis:* telling late payers the truth about everyone else — "nine out of ten people in the UK pay their tax on time; you are currently in the very small minority" — beats the standard demand. *Test:* variants randomised across tens of thousands of taxpayers. *Result:* compliance up by around fifteen percent against the standard letter; millions of pounds accelerated into collection within weeks — from changing a paragraph. *Decision:* the wording became standard practice, and the unit that ran the trial was eventually copied by governments on every continent. Your collections process, your payment reminders, your contract renewals: all of it is testable, and almost nobody tests it.

**PRICING · Progressive — the failed experiment that built the market leader.** In 1998 the American insurer piloted something heretical: pricing car insurance on how people actually drive, measured by a device in the car. The pilot was cancelled — the hardware cost too much. Read that carefully: the experiment *failed operationally* and *succeeded epistemically* — it validated the idea and identified the single blocking constraint, hardware cost. So Progressive waited for the constraint to move, re-ran the play as TripSense in 2004 and Snapshot in 2008, and rode usage-based pricing to become America's largest auto insurer, with telematics policies estimated fifteen to twenty-five percent more profitable than the rest. Twenty-five years of strategy from one cancelled pilot. That is what "there is no fail in an experiment, only information" looks like at full scale.

**PRODUCTION · Zara — the factory as laboratory.** The Spanish fashion giant runs what may be the largest permanent experiment in physical retail: every new design is produced in a deliberately small batch — often under a thousand units — and placed in selected stores. What sells in the first week gets scaled within about two weeks; what does not is cut, at a loss too small to matter. *Result at system level:* average markdowns around ten percent against an industry average near thirty. Zara is not a company that runs experiments. It is an experiment that runs a company — and it is the physical-world proof that a production department can live on the discovery loop, not just tolerate it.

**REVENUE · Bing — the $100 million idea in the backlog.** An employee proposed a small change to how ad headlines displayed — a few days of engineering at most. The programme managers, experienced people ranking hundreds of ideas, rated it low priority, and it sat untouched for six months, until one engineer noticed the code was cheap and simply ran the A/B test. Within hours, alarms fired — revenue was up so abnormally the platform suspected a bug. It was not a bug. *Result:* twelve percent more revenue — about a hundred million dollars a year — from the idea the experts had shelved. The ranking was the failure. Nobody can see which idea is the big one, which is precisely why the queue must be cheap enough that ideas do not need to be believed in to be tested.

**NO BUDGET · Intuit in rural India — the experiment run by hand.** A team exploring services for farmers in Karnataka believed price transparency would raise incomes. They did not build an app to find out. Before writing a single line of code, employees collected market prices themselves and texted them to a small group of farmers, *by hand*, every day — a product made entirely of people, run for the price of some SMS messages. Farmers acted on the information; the hypothesis held. Only then did Intuit build Fasal — which reached over seven hundred thousand farmers, raising their prices by about twenty percent. *The lesson entrepreneurs need most:* if the hypothesis can be tested by two people and a phone, testing it any other way is not rigour. It is procrastination.

Twelve experiments. A tax office, a truck fleet, a garment factory, a hamburger stand's heirs, a call centre, an insurance actuary, a warehouse, and a man texting vegetable prices to farmers. Not one of them is a marketing campaign. Now go back to your audit sheet, to the rows where you wrote *Discovery*, and notice which departments they were in — and which departments have somehow never produced a single one.

> **If the tax office can experiment on its letters and the truck fleet on its left turns, no department of yours is excused.**

---

### 11

One last thing about this territory, and then I will tell you about its opposite.

The second chapter of this book separated the hero into three things, and promised that one of them — heroic capacity, the ability to walk into fog and stay functional — would turn out to be not a problem but an asset waiting for its right address. This is the address. Discovery is the hero's home territory: the one part of the company where the answer genuinely is not known, where no process can be written in advance because nobody yet knows what it would say, and where the appetite for the unmapped is not a personality flaw to be managed but the job description itself. The restaurant company's development director — the man whose talent could not be written down — ended his three days not diminished but *relocated*: his openings went into discovery, one extraction at a time, and his heroism got pointed at the only work in the building that actually requires it: finding out.

That is what the audit's discovery column really is. Not a to-do list. A map of where the company's unknown begins — and a standing invitation to the people who love the dark.

But discovery is only half of what the audit routes, and it is the glamorous half. The other column — multiplication — looks, next to it, almost insultingly simple: take what already works and make more of it. No fog, no hypotheses, no romance. Just copying.

Which is exactly what a small Finnish game studio believed, the year it finally produced a hit — its fifty-second try — and discovered that the one thing it could not copy was itself.
