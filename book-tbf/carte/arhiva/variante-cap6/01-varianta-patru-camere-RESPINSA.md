# 6. The Output and the Audit

### Toshiba, Four Green Rooms, and the Question a Number Cannot Answer

---

### 1

On the morning of 27 September 2012, in Tokyo, a group of senior executives at Toshiba sat down for what the company called the Monthly Meeting of Presidents.

It was a routine meeting. It had a fixed place in the calendar, a fixed agenda, and a fixed cast. The division heads reported, the president listened, and the numbers were compared against the plan. If you have ever worked in a large company you have sat in that meeting, or in one built to the same drawing.

The digital products division reported that its forecast for the first half of the financial year had deteriorated again. It now expected an operating loss of twenty-four point eight billion yen.

The president of Toshiba was a man named Norio Sasaki. What he said in response is recorded, in the eventual investigation report, under a section heading that the investigators wrote themselves and that reads, in translation: *Request for profit improvement of 12.0 billion yen with 3 days remaining.*

He asked the division to find twelve billion yen of operating profit. The half-year closed on 30 September. He wanted the results of their deliberations the following day.

Now hold the arithmetic for a moment, because the arithmetic is the entire story. Twelve billion yen is somewhere around a hundred and fifty million dollars. The division had three days. It had already spent five months producing the loss it had just reported. Nothing that a consumer electronics business does — not a price change, not a promotion, not a renegotiation, not a product launch — moves a hundred and fifty million dollars of operating profit in seventy-two hours. There is no method. There has never been a method. The request was not difficult. It was arithmetically impossible by any legitimate route, and every person in that room knew it, including the man making it.

The executives worked through the night. They presented at ten past twelve the next day, and again at two in the afternoon, to Sasaki. They brought eleven point nine billion yen of what the report calls "profit and loss measures."

Ten point four billion of it was fiction.

They had not even had time to do the thing that would normally have been done, which was to go back to the suppliers who manufactured their products and renegotiate the terms in a way that would let the numbers be booked differently. Three days was not enough for that. So Toshiba sold its own inventory to its own wholly-owned subsidiaries, at prices constructed to disguise what was happening, and recorded the margin.

The senior management approved it.

---

### 2

I want to be careful about what I have just described, because there is an easy reading of it and the easy reading is worthless.

The easy reading is that Toshiba was full of crooks. That reading is available, it is comfortable, and it lets you close the book, because you do not employ crooks and neither do I.

Here is what the investigation actually found. The commission was chaired by Koichi Ueda, a lawyer who had previously been Chief Prosecutor of the Tokyo High Public Prosecutors Office — a man whose entire professional life had been spent establishing that particular people did particular wrong things. And when he came to describe the mechanism, he did not describe a conspiracy. He described a number.

Toshiba had a practice, and the practice had a name, and the name was the company's own. They called it **Challenge**.

A Challenge was a figure. It began life as something entirely ordinary — a budget line, a forecast, a target. And then, in the commission's words:

> What in its proper nature was no more than a rough estimate — a budget, a target — had become, without anyone noticing, a mandatory profit figure that had to be achieved.

*Without anyone noticing.* That is a prosecutor writing about a company that had just restated two hundred and twenty-four billion yen of profit. He is not describing a decision. He is describing a drift.

And then the commission wrote the sentence that this chapter exists to explain, and I want you to read it slowly, because a Japanese investigative commission arrived at it from a completely different direction than I did:

> This Challenge was often issued at the Monthly Meetings held when very little time remained until the final day of the quarter. As a result, a company given a Challenge found that, in the scrap of time remaining, **it was impossible to produce any large profit improvement through even the greatest commercial effort** — and so, in order to achieve the Challenge, it was in many cases pushed into a situation where it had no option but the improper method of inflating apparent profit.

Read what that says. It does not say the people were dishonest. It says they were given a result and no way to produce it, and then the clock ran out.

Sasaki gave evidence to the commission. He said he had known that most of the large quarterly profits were coming from those transactions, and that he had never instructed anyone to inflate anything, and that he had repeatedly told them the practice was unhealthy and should be reduced.

The commission accepted that he said it. It also found that by setting Challenges of that size, at that point in the quarter, and by refusing to allow the previously inflated profit to be unwound, he had cornered them anyway.

No villain is required. That is the uncomfortable part.

---

### 3

So Toshiba is one kind of company. Let me put it on a shelf for a moment and describe four.

Imagine four companies. Same industry, same size, same quarter. On the last day of the quarter, the leadership of each one looks at the numbers, and in all four the numbers are good. Revenue on plan. Margin on plan. The chart points the right way. In each of the four rooms, somebody says some version of *well done, everybody.*

**In the first room, it is true.** The business is genuinely producing what it should be producing, by the means it is supposed to produce it. Nothing has been borrowed. Nothing is hidden. The number is an honest description of a healthy machine.

**In the second room, the number was bought.** It was achieved at the cost of something nobody measures — maintenance not done, a market not developed, people not trained, a relationship allowed to thin. The measured thing went up. Something unmeasured went down by more. Nobody in the room can see this, because the thing that fell is not on any chart in the building.

**In the third room, the number was hit and the point was missed.** The measure and the purpose used to point in the same direction, and under pressure they came apart. Everybody did exactly what the indicator asked for. The indicator was no longer asking for the right thing.

**In the fourth room, the number was not achieved at all.** It was constructed. Not necessarily by fraud — sometimes by a definition quietly widened, a classification moved, a cost pushed into next month.

Four rooms. One number. And from outside — from a board pack, from a dashboard, from a quarterly report — **the four rooms are indistinguishable.**

That framing is not mine. It comes from a 2006 paper by Gwyn Bevan of the London School of Economics and Christopher Hood of Oxford, who were trying to work out what had actually happened when the British government spent a decade running its health service by targets. They laid out the four possible states behind a good number, and the whole force of their argument is in the fact that they had to list four.

Here is what that means for you, and it is worse than it first sounds.

**Nobody in those four rooms knows which room they are in.** Not the founder, not the board, not the finance director. The people in room two are not lying to you; they cannot see the thing that fell. The people in room three are not cynical; they are doing precisely what they were asked. Even in room four, the people who widened the definition mostly told themselves a reasonable story about why the old definition was misleading.

Toshiba, in the end, was room four. But nobody in that meeting on 27 September walked in knowing that. They walked in thinking they were in room one, and they walked out having quietly moved.

So the question this chapter is about is not *how do we get a better number*. It is:

> **How would you know which room you are in?**

That is what an audit is for. It is not a reporting meeting and it is not a review. It is the one moment in the quarter when a company tries to find out which of the four rooms it is standing in — and everything else in this chapter is machinery built to answer that one question.

Let me take the rooms in reverse order, because they get harder to see as you go.

---

### 4

Room four is the easy one, and I want to explain why, because the reason is not flattering to anybody.

Room four is easy because eventually it shows. A number that has been constructed has to be constructed again next quarter, and then again, and the construction gets larger each time, because the previous construction has to be carried. Toshiba's inflated balance was around fourteen billion yen in 2008. By the time one president left it was twenty-seven. By the time the next one left it was sixty-five. It grows until something breaks, and then there is an investigation, and then there is a report, and the rest of us read about it.

So room four produces evidence. Late, expensively, and at the cost of a great many careers — but it produces evidence.

The clearest illustration I know is not a fraud at all. It is a hospital.

Mid Staffordshire was an English NHS trust that became, in 2013, the subject of a public inquiry running to three volumes and one thousand seven hundred and eighty-two pages. The indicator at the centre of it was something called the Hospital Standardised Mortality Ratio, and I want you to notice its structure, because you have seen it before in this book.

The HSMR is the number of deaths that actually occurred, divided by the number of deaths that would have been expected, multiplied by a hundred. Observed over expected. Reality over default. It is, in the language of this book, an output compared against a vision — with the vision supplied by the national average rather than by the hospital.

Mid Staffordshire's was 127. Twenty-seven percent more deaths than expected.

Now here is what the trust did, reported in its own words to the regulator:

> Its mortality rate has improved from 127 in 05/06 to c 101 between May and August 2007 **as a result of significant focus on coding and mortality.**

Coding. The way in which a patient's condition is recorded. The trust ran an audit of its own coding and found that eighty percent of the diagnoses required recoding.

The number came down. Almost none of the reason was clinical.

And the inquiry's finding about this is one of the most useful sentences in the whole of this book, because it is not about the hospital. It is about everybody above it:

> The Trust, the West Midlands Strategic Health Authority and the Department of Health **all focused attention on coding issues, at the expense of considering whether a high HSMR indicated concerns about patient care.**

Everybody attacked the instrument. Nobody went to look at the wards.

The inquiry, incidentally, refused to say how many people died unnecessarily at Stafford. A figure circulated in the press for years and is still quoted; it does not appear anywhere in the report. Robert Francis wrote that it was not possible to say that any specific number of deaths was from an avoidable cause. And he found something more damning than a body count: that the two-year argument *about the statistic* was itself the thing that stopped anyone from walking into the hospital and looking.

There is a line in the inquiry's summary that I would put on the wall of every board room I have ever worked in:

> **It took false assurance from good news, and yet tolerated or sought to explain away bad news.**

One more detail, because it is the part that haunts me. A director at the regional authority argued for an independent analysis of the mortality data. His stated reason was that he wanted, in his words, *to understand this, not to explain it away.*

He was overruled.

### 5

Room three is harder, because in room three nobody has done anything wrong.

At around seven in the evening on 20 April 2010, four executives sat in a third-floor conference room on a drilling rig in the Gulf of Mexico. Two were from Transocean, which owned and operated the rig; two were from BP, which had leased it. They had flown out that morning for what the industry calls a management visibility tour — twenty-four hours on the rig, meeting the crew.

The rig was called Deepwater Horizon.

One of the BP executives described it afterwards as *the best performing rig that we had in our fleet and in the Gulf of Mexico*, and among the top performers in all of BP's floating fleet for both safety and drilling. And the crew had a statistic to go with that, which the presidential commission that later investigated the disaster recorded in a single sentence:

> Despite all the crew's troubles with this latest well, they had not had a single "lost-time incident" in seven years of drilling.

Seven years. On an offshore drilling rig. Not one injury serious enough to keep anybody off work for a shift.

That is a genuinely extraordinary number and it was not fabricated. Nobody constructed it. It was measured accurately, reported honestly, and it described something real: the crew of the Deepwater Horizon were careful, and their supervisors had built a culture in which people did not slip, trip, fall or get struck by things.

And in the same meeting, in the same hour, in the same room, there was a second document.

A BP safety audit from September of the previous year had produced a thirty-page list. Three hundred and ninety items of maintenance outstanding on the rig, requiring three thousand five hundred and forty-five man-hours of work. The managers discussed it. They reviewed the maintenance schedule. They talked about reducing dropped objects and personal injuries.

Two instruments. One table. One said the rig was excellent. The other said the rig was degrading.

Two hours later the well blew out and eleven men died.

Now, if that were the whole story it would be a tragedy and not a lesson. Here is what makes it a lesson.

The distinction the Deepwater Horizon needed had already been written down, twice, by the same company, in documents BP itself had signed.

In 2000, after three serious incidents at a BP complex at Grangemouth in Scotland, the British safety regulator concluded that the company had made incorrect assumptions about its own performance based on lost-time accident frequencies. BP accepted it, in writing:

> The use of occupational safety indicators such as "Days Away From Work Case Frequency" **can obscure other factors, and provide a false sense of security if it indicates an overall improving trend.**

In 2007, after an explosion at BP's Texas City refinery killed fifteen people, an independent panel chaired by former US Secretary of State James Baker made it a formal finding:

> **BP mistakenly interpreted improving personal injury rates as an indication of acceptable process safety performance at its U.S. refineries.** BP's reliance on this data, combined with an inadequate process safety understanding, **created a false sense of confidence** that BP was properly addressing process safety risks.

Published in 2000. Restated in 2007. Not applied in 2010.

The distinction underneath it is worth having in your own head, whatever business you are in. There are indicators that measure the things that happen often and hurt a little — a slip, a trip, a dropped tool, a returned order, a complaint. And there are indicators that measure the things that happen rarely and destroy you. They are not the same, they do not correlate, and the first kind is enormously more comfortable to watch, because it moves every week and you can be pleased about it.

The US Chemical Safety Board, investigating Macondo, put it in a sentence that ought to be uncomfortable for anybody who runs a dashboard:

> **History has repeatedly proven that good personal safety statistics have, in fact, often preceded major accident events**, yet industry and regulators still rely on personal safety metrics to indicate good process safety performance.

They then listed the cases. A refinery where twenty-three people died, which had run several million work hours without a lost-time incident. A refinery that had been given national recognition for safety on the basis of a year in which it had a major fire. A refinery that suffered an explosion killing seven men *a few weeks after winning the same national safety award.*

That last one was on 2 April 2010. Deepwater Horizon was on the twentieth. In the three weeks between them, twenty-nine men died in a coal mine in West Virginia whose chief executive wrote, six weeks later, that his company had achieved a ninety percent reduction in its lost-time accident rate and had won three national safety awards the previous autumn. Investigators subsequently found more than two dozen unreported injuries at that mine alone, and the Department of Labor concluded the company had misrepresented its injury data by as much as thirty-seven percent.

Three catastrophes, three weeks, three sets of instruments that all looked good.

And then there is the document that I find harder to shake than any of the rest of it. Transocean's proxy statement, filed with the American securities regulator on 1 April 2011 — that is, a year after eleven of its people died on the Deepwater Horizon:

> …notwithstanding the tragic loss of life in the Gulf of Mexico, we achieved an exemplary statistical safety record as measured by our total recordable incident rate… **As measured by these standards, we recorded the best year in safety performance in our Company's history.**

Safety was worth a quarter of the executive bonus. The formula, run on the year's data, produced a calculated payout of a hundred and fifteen percent on the safety component.

It took a human being, exercising discretion outside the formula, to reduce it. And even after that intervention, the safety component paid out at sixty-seven percent.

Eight months before any of this, the president of Transocean had written an internal email to his own senior management:

> I am not convinced at all that we have the right leading indicators. The leading indicators we report today are **all just different incident metrics — they have nothing to do with actually preventing accidents.**

He was right, he was the president, he said it in writing, and nothing changed. Which tells you something about how much of this is a knowledge problem and how much of it is an architecture problem.

### 6

Room two is the hardest of the three, because in room two there is no incident at all. Nothing happens. That is the entire problem: the cost is composed of things that did not occur.

In 2003, a committee of the British Parliament took evidence from a doctor named Richard Harrad, the clinical director of the Bristol Eye Hospital. The health service had a target for how long a patient could wait for a *first* outpatient appointment. It had no target at all for follow-up appointments — for the patients already in the system, being monitored for conditions like glaucoma and diabetes, whose eyes were being watched.

He wrote:

> The waiting time targets for new outpatient appointments at the Bristol Eye Hospital have been achieved at the expense of cancellation and delay of follow-up appointments. **At present we cancel over 1,000 appointments per month.** Some patients have waited 20 months longer than the planned date for their appointment. We have kept clinical incident forms for all patients, mostly those with glaucoma or diabetes, who have lost vision as a result of delayed follow-up; **there have been 25 in the past 2 years. This figure undoubtedly underestimates the true incidence.**

Twenty-five people went blind, or partly blind, because a number that was being watched was met by sacrificing a number that was not.

And then he described one of them:

> One particularly sad case was that of an elderly lady who was completely deaf and relied upon signing and lip-reading for communication. She lives with her disabled husband who like her is completely deaf. Her follow-up appointment for glaucoma was delayed several times and during this time her glaucoma deteriorated and **she became totally blind.**

Nobody committed fraud. Nobody was even inefficient. The waiting-time figures for that hospital were accurate and they were good and somebody was almost certainly congratulated for them.

Another doctor, giving evidence to the same committee about his own area, said that the ophthalmology unit had cancelled nineteen thousand five hundred follow-up appointments in six months so that new patients could be seen inside the target.

The committee's own verdict was this: *When targets put target setting before clinical need, they are clearly inverting priorities rather than advancing them.*

Now, you do not run a hospital, so let me show you the same room in a business.

In December 2000, a man named James McNerney arrived at 3M as chief executive. He came from General Electric, and the market liked it: the share price rose about twenty percent in the days after his appointment. He was the first outsider to run 3M in its hundred-year history.

He did what he had been trained to do, and much of it was correct. He cut eight thousand jobs. He sharpened the performance review. And he imported Six Sigma — a serious, disciplined, well-evidenced methodology for reducing variation in a process.

He imported it into the laboratories as well.

The financial results were real and they were good. Profits grew by an average of twenty-two percent a year. Operating margin went from seventeen percent in 2001 to twenty-three percent in 2005.

And the proportion of 3M's sales coming from products introduced in the previous five years — for a company that had built its entire identity on that number — fell from about a third to about a quarter.

McNerney left after four and a half years. His successor, George Buckley, removed the obligation on 3M's research scientists to work to Six Sigma objectives. Not from the rest of the company. From the laboratories. And he explained why in terms that belong in the fourth chapter of this book:

> Invention is by its very nature a disorderly process. You can't put a Six Sigma process into that area and say, well, I'm getting behind on invention, so **I'm going to schedule myself for three good ideas on Wednesday and two on Friday.** That's not how creativity works.

One of the vice presidents who ran the research side described the actual mechanism, and it is more banal and more instructive than the philosophy:

> In some cases in the lab it made sense, but in other cases, **people were going around dreaming up green-belt programs to fill their quota of green-belt programs for that time period.** We were letting, I think, the process get in the way of doing the actual invention.

The metric was how many improvement projects you had completed. So people produced improvement projects.

And here is what happened afterwards, which I did not expect when I went looking and which I now think is the most instructive part of the story.

3M measures its own capacity to explore. It has an indicator called the New Product Vitality Index — the percentage of total sales coming from products introduced in the last five years. It is, as indicators go, an unusually honest one. It is hard to fake and it measures precisely the thing that a company like 3M must not lose.

In February 2010, the board's compensation committee made it a bonus target. Twenty percent of the executives' performance shares came to depend on it. Then, over the following years, the committee raised the bar: the level required for a full payout went from twenty-nine percent to thirty-three, and then to thirty-four.

3M's own filings record the result:

> **No shares were earned based on 2015 and 2016 performance for this metric.**

And after 2017, the word *vitality* disappears from 3M's filings altogether.

They measured exploration, which was right. They turned the measurement into a target. They raised the target. They missed it twice. They stopped measuring.

I do not think anybody at 3M intended any of that, in the same way that nobody at Bristol intended to blind a deaf woman, in the same way that nobody at Toshiba walked into a meeting intending to commit accounting fraud. Room two is not built by decisions. It is built by a series of individually defensible responses to a number.

### 7

So: four rooms, and from the outside they look identical, and the people inside cannot tell which one they occupy.

At this point in the argument most people reach for the same instrument. If the numbers can mislead, they say, then we need better numbers, and we need to hold people to them harder.

I spent fifteen years selling that answer. I want to tell you what I found when I finally measured it.

I ran my life by objectives and I ran my businesses by objectives. I built a complete management system with an OKR framework at its centre, and I taught that system to more than thirty thousand companies. I was directly involved in growing more than three hundred organisations across twenty-three industries, and I used objectives for growth in every one of them. I wrote over a hundred articles about the power of goal setting.

And when I began to see the instrument failing — and I saw it for years before I admitted what I was seeing — I did what any believer does. I assumed the problem was the version. So I built two variations of my own, which I called Dynamic OKRs and Progressive OKRs, each designed to repair a specific place where I could watch objectives producing damage.

I spent ten years sharpening the knife before I asked what I was cutting.

Then I did something I should have done a decade earlier. I took the three hundred highest-performing organisations using my system — not a random sample of the market, the good ones, where the system was properly installed and the people actually did the work — and instead of asking whether they hit their objectives, I asked what distinguished the ones they hit from the ones they missed.

Fifty-six percent of the objectives set were achieved. Forty-four percent were not.

That first number is respectable. In a room full of consultants you could build a career on it, and I had.

Then I cut the data a second way.

**Ninety-seven percent of the objectives that were achieved were objectives where the method was already known.** A validated process — Level 2 or Level 3 in the language of the fourth chapter — being implemented somewhere new, or multiplied, or simply turned up. The company already knew how. The objective pointed at a capability that was already there.

**Eighty-eight percent of the objectives that were missed were objectives where nobody in the company knew the method.** *Grow sales by fifty percent.* How? Nobody knew. That was the entire content of the objective: a number, a deadline, and a hole where the cause should have been.

I should say plainly what this is. It is not a published study. Nobody peer-reviewed it. It is my analysis of my own client base, with all the selection bias that implies. I report it because the alternative is to hide it, and because I had fifteen years of professional identity riding on the result coming out the other way.

Read the two figures next to each other and the conclusion arrives without help.

> **In three hundred companies, an objective never once produced a method.**

Where a method existed, it got applied, and the objective took the credit. Where no method existed, the objective sat in a document for ninety days, generated a great deal of anxiety, and then generated an explanation.

The first chapter of this book drew a line between a result and a cause: a result is what happened, a cause is what makes it happen again. An objective is a **result written in advance**. It is a result with a date on it and somebody's name beside it. It belongs to the same family as the thing it is supposed to produce, which is exactly why it cannot produce it.

---

### 8

Now I have to be careful, because there is a version of this argument that overreaches and it would be both wrong and easy to dismiss.

Objectives are not useless. They are not a scam.

Look again at that ninety-seven percent. It is not evidence that objectives fail. It is evidence of **where they work.** An objective is an instrument for concentrating resources and attention on something you already know how to do, and that is a real job. An organisation has more possible activities than it has capacity, and something has to decide where the capacity goes. A clear, owned, time-bound objective, pointed at a process the company can actually execute, does that job well.

Google ran on OKRs for years and it helped them. It would be foolish to pretend otherwise, and every founder reading this knows a company that got somewhere real with a number on a wall.

**And that is exactly the danger.**

Because it works — visibly, sometimes spectacularly — we conclude that it is *the* method rather than *a* method with a domain of validity. And we apply it everywhere, including the places its own inventors marked as the places where it degrades.

> **The trap is not that objectives fail. It is that they succeed often enough to look universal.**

The fourth chapter gave you the boundary in the language this book has been building. Everything a company does is either **known** or **unknown**. The known is managed through processes, indicators, standards, roles, training and evaluation. The unknown is managed through experiments. Managing the known through experiments is waste. Managing the unknown through targets is damage.

So: objectives belong in the known. Where the method exists and simply needs to be pointed somewhere or done more of, an objective is a good tool.

Objectives do not belong in the unknown. Where the method does not exist, an objective is not a plan. It is a wish with a deadline attached and somebody's name on it.

And there is one more thing the fourth chapter established that I need to bring back here, because it answers the question every reader is now silently asking. When a company wants more output, it has exactly two moves: turn some of the unknown into known, or make more of the known. Discovery, or multiplication.

**There is no third move.**

What looks like a third move — pushing harder on the machine you already have — is not growth. It is borrowing. It is the discount that lifts revenue and destroys margin, the contract pulled forward from next quarter, the three weeks of overtime that protect this delivery and consume the energy that would have produced the next one.

An objective set against an unknown method is that borrowing, formalised, with a name attached to it.

---

### 9

There is a reason we reach for it anyway, and it has nothing to do with management.

When we do not like reality, we have two options. We can examine the machine that produced it — which is slow, technical, occasionally humiliating, and yields nothing that feels good for months. Or we can set an objective, which is a way of announcing that from next quarter things will be different, because we have decided that they will be.

*This quarter I lose the weight. This quarter I fix the marriage. This quarter we double the client base.*

Notice what those sentences do. They change nothing whatsoever about the system that produced the current weight, the current marriage, or the current client base. What they change is how you feel, immediately, in the moment of saying them. They convert a depressing present into an exciting future at no cost and with no requirement to look at anything unpleasant.

I call this **therapy by objectives**, and I mean it precisely. It is not a management technique with emotional side effects. It is an emotional technique wearing management clothes.

You have met this instrument before, in the second chapter. It is the hero's favourite tool, because it generates the emergency and casts the rescuer in the same document.

And here is the thing nobody says about objectives, which is the exact opposite of what everybody says about them.

> **An objective carries less responsibility than a process.**

You can set a goal to lose thirty kilograms in three months while knowing absolutely nothing about how weight is lost, and feel genuinely proud of yourself that evening for having set it. The setting is the achievement. The pride arrives immediately and the accountability arrives in twelve weeks, by which point there will be reasons.

A process offers no such comfort, because a process is entirely within your power. If the process says four training sessions a week and you did one, there is no supplier to blame and no market that moved. There is nowhere for the explanation to go.

Which brings me to the most common sentence in my professional life. *I know what to do, I just can't stick to it.*

No. You do not know what to do. You have a system you cannot apply, and a system you cannot apply is the same object as not knowing how. What that sentence actually means is that you have not yet run enough experiments to find the version that works for **you**, in your week, with your constraints and your temperament. That is not a character failure. It is a quarter of missing work.

I told you in the second chapter how I finally stopped the cycle of gaining weight and dramatically losing it: I stopped setting a target weight, audited the machine that was producing the body I had, ran experiments on the things the audit implicated, and built in whatever survived. I am not going to tell it again. I mention it only because it is the same procedure I am about to describe at the scale of a company, and because it is the reason I know it works on something that matters to you personally.

### 10

So. You have four rooms, you cannot tell them apart from the number, and the instrument everybody reaches for is the wrong shape.

What actually tells you which room you are in?

Not the output. The output is evidence, and evidence has to be interrogated, not admired. The fourth chapter had a sentence about what a machine actually buys you, and this is the moment it gets spent:

> **A machine does not remove uncertainty. It converts uncertainty from a fog into monitored variation.**

In a fog, a bad quarter is an argument. Somebody says the market softened, somebody says the team has gone flat, somebody says the product needs work, and everybody is drawing on the same absence of evidence — which means the argument is settled by whoever is most senior or most confident. With monitored variation, a bad quarter is a signal with a location. You know which range was breached, and where, and when it started.

That is what makes the four rooms distinguishable. Not a better headline number. A machine underneath it made of processes that were validated before they were allowed in, each with its own indicator and its own standard, so that when the headline moves you can walk backwards through the chain and find out which link moved first.

Everything the fourth and fifth chapters asked of you — the discipline, the documentation, the refusal to let anything into the machine before it had earned its way in — is paid for here. It reads like administrative fussiness. It is actually the only thing that makes it possible for eight people to look at a bad number and not turn on each other.

Which means the quarterly audit is not really a meeting. It is the moment you find out whether you built something that can be audited.

I run one. Three days, four times a year, away from the offices — usually a rented house or lodge in the off-season rather than a hotel, because we want a place we can arrange the way we like. Tables in a horseshoe. Flipcharts. And one rule that I introduced fifteen years ago and have never softened: **the telephones stay upstairs, in the bedrooms.** Not on the table. Not face down. Upstairs.

Three days, because the first half-day is spent arriving. People are still in last week, still checking messages, still answering the question they were asked in the corridor. Real analysis does not begin until the room has settled, and a room does not settle in ninety minutes.

Away from the offices, because context leaks. In your own building everybody is sitting inside their department, their status, yesterday's meeting and tomorrow's. Somewhere else, with the phones upstairs, people become slightly different and considerably more honest.

And in the room: the leadership, obviously. But also — and this is a requirement, not a courtesy — **anybody who can answer a question with data, whatever their title.** If the bottleneck turns out to be in lead generation, is the person who actually looks at the lead data every week in the room? Or is the room full of people who have been told about the lead data by somebody who was told about it? Because if it is the second, then the experiments and the multiplications will be chosen from feeling and seniority, and seniority in that room is usually just the feeling with the loudest voice.

I built almost all of that fifteen years ago and I would build it again tomorrow. The rhythm was right. The room was right. The telephones were right.

What I put inside it was wrong.

---

### 11

Day one begins with a sheet of paper.

![Vision worksheet](imagini/vision.pdf)

Across the top it says: *Vision is an ordinary great quarter — a good quarter that can be repeated, without heroics or last-minute saves.* Then five columns. The indicator. What it did last quarter. What it did this quarter. What the vision says it should do. And the gap.

The indicators are not chosen at the meeting. They come from the vision — the Ordinary Great Quarter you wrote in the third chapter, with its ranges rather than its points, because reality has weather in it and a default that cannot survive an ordinary bad month is a wish with a decimal place.

And now the sheet gets read twice, which is the whole point of it.

**Read it once, comparing this quarter with last quarter.** This is a genuine comparison and it matters. It tells you what improved, what deteriorated, what became unstable, what became normal. It is information about the machine's direction, and a company that does not look at it is throwing away a quarter of data.

**Then read it again, comparing this quarter with the vision.** And that is a completely different question. It is not *are we better than we were.* It is *is this machine producing what we have said it should produce when nobody is being a hero.*

In a good company, on a good sheet, you will often see something that no objectives review can accommodate. Every indicator improved on the quarter before. And every indicator is still short of the vision.

> **Nobody in that room failed, and there is a great deal of work to do.**

Both of those are true at the same time, and in the old room they could not coexist. An indicator was green or it was red. A person was the hero of the quarter or the disappointment. There was no available state in which somebody had done real, measurable, improving work and the company was still meaningfully short of where it needs to be — which is, incidentally, the state that most good companies are in most of the time.

That is not a mood I am asking you to adopt. It is a structural property of the sheet. Because the comparison in the fourth column is not against a promise a human being made three months ago, it is against a description of what this machine ought to do in ordinary weather.

Which is why the column is headed **Vision** and not **Target**. Whatever number sits there is not a stretch. It is not what the company hopes for in a very good quarter with a push at the end. It is what the machine ought to produce on an ordinary Tuesday in an ordinary month, with nobody working weekends. Which means the gap is not a measure of insufficient effort.

**The gap is a measure of something structurally missing.**

Three things about this sheet that people get wrong.

**The verdict lands on the machine, not on a person.** This is the one I care about most, and it took me longest to learn. When we look at a shortfall and conclude that a particular person is the reason for it, we have said something without noticing what we said: that the person is more important than the machine. We have made them a hero. And the whole of the fourth chapter argued the other way round — that when the output is not what it should be, the process is almost always what needs correcting, and treating it as a people problem is the single most expensive misdiagnosis available to a founder, precisely because the evidence for the wrong conclusion is standing in front of you every day in the form of people who are visibly not performing.

That does not mean a person is never the problem. A person is the problem when they have not delivered a responsibility that sat entirely within their power — and the fifth chapter defined a responsibility exactly that way: an indicator, with a standard, that the person controls. Outside of that, you are looking at a role that was wrong, or a recruitment process that was wrong, or a standard that was never derived. When you say the man is at fault, you hand him all the power. When you find that the process is at fault, you hand the power back to the machine — which is the thing you own.

**There has to be more than one row.** The third chapter explained why, and it applies with full force here: a single number can always be bought at the expense of something else. Revenue can be purchased with margin. Growth can be purchased with debt. A vision with one line in it is an invitation to game it, and a sheet with one line on it cannot distinguish room one from room two. The rows are not thoroughness. They are the only defence against a good number that was paid for somewhere off-camera.

**And a number that is too good is information too.** If an indicator has overshot the vision, that is not a reason for applause. Capacity went somewhere. It came from a budget that belonged to something else. In a company I worked with, a division consistently beat its build target — and it turned out to be borrowing people from the process that fed the pipeline, which meant that this year's overachievement was manufacturing next year's shortfall. On this sheet, that shows up as two rows moving in opposite directions. On a scoreboard, it shows up as a hero.

The reading takes about forty minutes. Most of it is spent arguing about whether the numbers can be trusted, which is the correct thing for the first morning of a quarterly meeting to be about.

At the end of it you have a list of gaps. That is the entire output of the first session.

### 12

Then the second sheet goes on the table, and this is where the quarter is actually decided.

![Quarterly Audit worksheet](imagini/audit.pdf)

*For each gap: name the bottleneck, the processes behind it, and mark the solution path.* Four columns — the gap, the bottleneck that limits it, the processes involved, and two checkboxes: **multiplication** or **discovery**. One row for each gap on the first sheet.

Notice first what this sheet does not ask.

It does not ask how we are going to close the gap. There is no field anywhere on it for a commitment, a stretch, or a plan to try harder. It asks one question, which the first chapter promised and which this sheet finally makes operational:

> **What is blocking this machine from producing the output described in the vision?**

And the answer is not allowed to be an opinion.

The answers come out of the indicators of the validated processes inside the machine. That is what the third column is for, and the third column is the hardest one on the sheet. It decides whether this meeting is management or theatre.

Here is what filling it in actually looks like, and it is the same arithmetic the fifth chapter ran forwards, now run backwards. Suppose the gap is in new clients. The sales process has a chain: companies identified, people reached, qualifying conversations held, proposals sent, contracts signed. Each of those has an indicator, and each indicator has a standard that was derived from the output the vision requires — a hundred calls a day was not chosen because it sounded ambitious, it was calculated backwards from forty contracts.

So you walk the chain and you find the link that is below its standard. Perhaps qualification is running at standard and lead generation is not. Perhaps both are at standard and the channel mix has moved. Whatever it is, it has a location, and the location can be pointed at.

If you can fill that column with real, named, validated processes, the conversation is about mechanism, and conversations about mechanism converge.

If you cannot — if the honest entry is *sales, generally* — then you do not have a machine there. You have activity. And a room full of senior people looking at a gap with no processes behind it will do the only thing available to them, which is argue.

> **A meeting without validated processes is not an audit. It is a debate, and the best talker wins.**

The best talker is almost never the person closest to the data. I have sat in hundreds of those meetings. I used to run them.

An empty Processes column is not a failure of the meeting. It is the single most valuable diagnostic in this whole system, and it should be written down as a finding: *we cannot answer this, because there is no validated process here.* That is a real result. It tells you where the machine has a hole in it, and it is usually the most important thing the audit produces in a company's first year of doing this.

Now, one thing this sheet is not, and I need to say it because the first chapter set an expectation.

In the first chapter I offered a diagnostic question — *if this machine had to produce twice the output, where would it break first?* — as the lens the audit needed. I no longer use it, and the reason is that the vision does the job better. A vision written properly is already a description of a machine substantially larger than the one you have; the gaps on the first sheet already point at everything that would break. Adding a hypothetical doubling on top of a real gap adds a second imaginary problem to a room that has just been handed a list of actual ones. If your vision is so close to your current output that it exposes nothing, the problem is not that you need a better question. The problem is your vision.

Then the two checkboxes, and there are only two, because there are only two moves.

**Multiplication.** Something already in the machine solves this, and there is not enough of it. The sales conversation that works exists, four people run it, and it works when they run it. Put it in front of more people. This is not exciting, and it is where most of the growth is. We will take this road wherever it is available, because it is the safest and the most predictable — and I say that as somebody who, like most entrepreneurs, would far rather reinvent the wheel. That is how we keep the hero's role. I am not telling you to give up the hero's role. I am telling you not to spend it where the return is small or negative.

**Discovery.** Nothing in the machine solves this. We do not know how. In that case the quarter's work on this row is to find out.

To answer that honestly you look at two things: the processes currently in the machine, and **the data from every experiment you have finished so far.** The second source is the one companies forget, and it is frequently where the answer already is. An experiment run eighteen months ago and filed away has often already eliminated three of the five roads you are about to argue about.

And there is one rule governing the multiplication box that people break constantly, always with good intentions.

> **You may only multiply Level 2 and above.**

If the process that would close the gap has only ever worked once — for one person, in one place, on one occasion — then it is Level 1, and next quarter's job is not multiplication. It is taking that process from Level 1 to Level 2. And that work happens in the discovery branch, beside the machine, not inside it.

This costs something every single time it comes up. There is a thing that worked, there is a gap it appears to fit, and the room wants to roll it out on Monday. Webvan wanted to roll it out on Monday.

If you want a number for how expensive that instinct is, take it from the most disciplined validation system human beings have ever built. In pharmaceutical development, where the ladder is legally enforced and there are billions of dollars behind getting it right, roughly seventy percent of drugs that succeed in Phase 2 do not survive Phase 3. Not because anybody cheated. The Phase 2 result was real. It simply did not generalise.

Seven out of ten things are lost between *it worked* and *it keeps working*, by an industry designed around nothing else.

Now consider what your one good quarter proves.

And when you do decide to multiply, remember what the fifth chapter said about how: you do not build the new unit from scratch. You take a few people out of the existing team and move them into the new one, because they already live the standard and they carry the parts of the process that never made it into the document. There are always parts that never made it into the document. The team you took them from then recruits and replaces them, which is itself a test — if it can lose two people and return to standard, its processes really are at Level 2, and you have just proved it for free.

Sometimes the honest entry in the bottleneck column is *we do not know why this is happening.* That is a legitimate finding, not an evasion, and it routes straight to discovery. No amount of commitment converts not knowing into knowing.

> **A quarter whose output is knowledge is not a lost quarter.**

And look at what has happened to the size of the decision. A handful of rows. One checkbox each. That is the entire strategic content of ninety days of a company's life, and it fits on one side of one sheet of paper.

### 13

There is one thing this sheet cannot do, and if you do not solve it separately the sheet becomes an expensive way of being wrong on paper.

It cannot tell you *why*.

The first chapter told you about Taiichi Ohno drawing a circle in chalk on the floor of a Toyota plant, putting a young engineer inside it, and coming back four hours later to ask what he had seen. The point of that exercise was never endurance. It was that the first hour of observation shows you what you already believe, and that somewhere in the third hour the model in your head breaks and you start seeing what is actually there. The operator walking four steps for a part that could be at his elbow, eleven hundred times a shift. The small stack of components that is never zero, which means something upstream produces faster than something downstream consumes. The little jig somebody made out of tape.

None of that is in the data. All of it is in the room.

Which means the audit cannot be three days in a rented house with three pieces of paper. The indicators will tell you *where* — which process, which step, which link in the chain. They will almost never tell you why that link is failing, because the why is usually a physical fact about how the work actually happens, and physical facts do not survive the trip into a spreadsheet.

So the field work happens **before** the meeting, not in it. In the weeks before an audit, the people who own the processes that the indicators have implicated go and stand in their own chalk circles. They watch the work. They sit with the person doing it. They follow one order, or one client, or one shift, all the way through.

And then they arrive at the audit having seen something, rather than having read a report about a report.

That changes what the meeting is. It is not where the investigation happens. It is where the investigations are put side by side, and where the company decides what to do about them.

It also changes who needs to be there, and it is the reason for the rule I gave earlier about titles. A person who has stood in the circle has something no chart contains. A person two levels above them has a summary of a summary.

### 14

Everything I have described so far depends on one thing that no worksheet can supply.

It depends on people telling the truth about their own processes, in front of their colleagues, in a room where their colleagues can hear.

For years I treated the absence of that as a character problem. My managers spent the fortnight before every quarterly meeting assembling explanations rather than analysis — and I want to be precise about that distinction, because everybody in the room understood it and nobody was in a position to produce the first kind. An explanation is a sequence of true facts arranged to produce the most favourable reading of an unfavourable number. It is skilled work. Some people are exceptionally good at it. And in a culture built on objectives, those are the people who get promoted, which is how the disease becomes hereditary.

I used to address this with a speech about accountability on the morning of day one.

It was not a character problem. It was a reporting-system problem, and I had designed the reporting system.

In December 1974, a TWA aircraft descending toward Dulles airport in Virginia struck a mountain and killed everyone on board. In the investigation that followed, something came to light that had nothing to do with the crew of that flight. Six weeks earlier, a United Airlines crew had experienced almost exactly the same confusion about almost exactly the same approach chart, on the same approach, and had come close to the same outcome.

They had reported it. Their report had gone into United's internal safety system, where it did what it was designed to do: it made United safer.

The official history of what happened next records the fate of that report in four words. It was **shared within company only.**

A report existed that could have saved that aircraft. It was locked inside one company.

In May 1975 the Federal Aviation Administration set up a provisional programme, and in April 1976 it was replaced by something with a very strange design: the Aviation Safety Reporting System, which is run not by the FAA but by NASA.

The FAA explained why in its own advisory circular, and I have never read a better sentence about the architecture of honesty:

> The FAA determined that ASRP effectiveness would be greatly enhanced **if NASA, rather than the FAA, accomplished the receipt, processing, and analysis of raw data.** This would ensure the anonymity of the reporter… and, consequently, **increase the flow of information.**

The regulator handed the data to somebody else so that people would tell it things.

The system has three properties. It is voluntary. It is confidential — NASA strips every identifying detail before anybody analyses anything. And it is non-punitive: filing a report promptly, for a violation that was inadvertent and not deliberate, means that the FAA will not impose a penalty or suspend a certificate, even where a violation is found. The FAA's own language for why is worth having:

> The FAA considers the filing of a report with NASA… to be **indicative of a constructive attitude. Such an attitude will tend to prevent future violations.**

And then the sentence that made the whole thing work, from the same document:

> The FAA will not seek, and NASA will not release or make available to the FAA, any report filed with NASA under the ASRS… **There has been no breach of confidentiality of the ASRS under NASA management.**

There is a physical detail I find more persuasive than any of the policy. The report form has a detachable identification strip. NASA time-stamps it and posts it back to the person who filed, so they hold proof that they reported. Then the originals are destroyed.

Since 1976 the system has collected more than two point three million reports and issued more than eight thousand safety alerts. In 2025 it received a hundred and twenty-one thousand reports — about four hundred and eighty-five on every working day. In nearly fifty years it has never once breached the identity of a reporter, and that record is what makes the promise worth anything. A pilot filing today is not trusting a policy. They are trusting fifty years of a policy kept without exception.

Now go back to my rented house.

My managers were preparing for a hearing because I had built a hearing. I had constructed a room in which the honest report — *the number is short, and here is the step where I believe it breaks* — was the single most dangerous sentence a manager could say. And then I was disappointed that nobody said it.

> **You cannot audit output that people have had to survive.**

Understand that this is not an argument for softness. The aviation safety system is not soft. It exists because aviation takes failure more seriously than any other industry on earth. It is a choice between two things you can optimise the room for. Optimise for accountability and you will get careful people producing careful accounts. Optimise for truth and you will get information, and information is the only thing that improves a machine.

There is one specific diagnosis that only becomes possible in a room built the second way, and it is the one that matters most in a performance conversation. When something did not work, there are exactly two explanations and they require opposite responses. **Either the process was wrong**, in which case the person did what they were supposed to do and the machine needs work. **Or the process was not followed**, in which case the machine is fine and something else is going on — capacity, clarity, capability, fit.

From the outside those look identical. Both produce a missed number and an uncomfortable conversation. And in a room where missing is dangerous you will never reliably find out which one you are looking at, because the honest answer to the second is *I did not do it*, and nobody says that at an awards ceremony.

Which is what my old meeting was. I have thought about it for a long time and that is the most accurate description I have: it was an awards ceremony for heroes. Who was the hero this quarter, and who was not.

And the strangest part is that everybody knew. I knew. We did it four times a year for fifteen years, in a house with the phones upstairs, because we were serious people who took the business seriously and this was what serious people did.

### 15

Every row marked *discovery* becomes a sheet of its own.

![Experiment worksheet](imagini/experiment.pdf)

At the top: the experiment's name, and the bottleneck it solves — so that no experiment can exist in the company without a stated reason for existing. Then six blocks.

**Process** — where in the machine this happens.

**Hypothesis** — written as *if… then…*. And I want to be exact about why the form matters, because it is usually explained wrongly. It is not about being able to be proven wrong, as though the point were humility. It is about truth. A hypothesis stated in that form makes a claim about how reality works that reality can answer. Anything vaguer than that is a wish, and reality has nothing to say to a wish.

**Source** — where the idea came from. Data. An insight. A client request. Somebody's experience. A consultant's advice. A friend's suggestion. Something copied from another organisation. A book, a course, a conference.

**KPIs** — what we will measure. Decided now, before we know the answer, which is the only moment at which that decision can be made honestly.

**Constraints** — budget, time, and the rules we will not break in order to win. This field does two jobs. It is where the values from the third chapter stop being a poster and become a boundary on a specific piece of work. And it is where the economics of the experiment live, which matters more than people expect: you do not want an experiment so expensive that it strains the company's finances, and you do not want one that can damage the brand or reach a large number of clients before you know what it does. **The machine pays for the laboratory.** An experiment that endangers the machine has eaten its own funding.

**Method** — how we will actually run it.

And then the footer, which is the part people expect to be missing and are surprised to find.

**Owner. Team. Deadline.**

Exactly like an objective. A person's name, a group of people, and a date. Nothing here is an argument for vagueness and nothing here is an argument for taking the pressure off. There is nothing gentle about an experiment with your name on it and a date six weeks away.

The difference is two fields higher up the sheet. Because there is a Hypothesis and there are KPIs, what the owner has undertaken to deliver is not a result. It is **evidence**.

And evidence can be delivered honestly on a deadline. A result whose method you do not yet possess cannot be — which is precisely why, when you demand it anyway, you get a fortnight of explanation-assembling, and a division in Tokyo selling its own stock to itself with three days to go.

Which produces the sentence that changed my quarterly meetings more than any other.

> **You cannot fail at an experiment. The only way to fail is to measure it badly.**

The reason is not encouragement. It is structural. Nobody in the room has any stake in the experiment coming out one way rather than the other. There is no pressure for it to work, because nothing was promised except the finding. What everyone wants is real information from reality, so that it can be used to improve the machine. And a properly designed, properly measured experiment always delivers that, because knowledge accumulates in both directions. A hypothesis that turns out to be false has removed a road from the map. *This channel does not work at our price point* is worth as much as its opposite — sometimes more, because it is permanent, and because nobody would ever have paid to learn it deliberately.

Understand what that does to the room. In the old meeting, the person who proposed something and did not deliver it stood up on day one and explained themselves. In this one, the person who ran a clean experiment on a hypothesis that turned out to be wrong reports a finding, the finding goes into the company's knowledge, and there is nothing to explain.

The fear is not managed. It is structurally absent, because there is no longer a mechanism to generate it. And the fortnight of preparation is not reduced. It is gone, along with the work that went into it, because there is nothing to defend. You bring what happened.

Now the Source field, which looks like bureaucracy and is not.

Most of what enters a company comes from one of those sources — somebody's experience, a consultant, a book, a competitor, a training course. And what we normally do with information from those sources is treat it as true and put it straight into the machine. That single habit has cost every one of us more than we would like to count: employees hired against an idea that did not work, productivity lost, trust spent, brands damaged.

The Source field makes that visible. After two years you can sort every experiment the company has run by where the idea came from, and see which sources produced validated methods and which produced nothing at all. That is how a company finds out whether its own instincts are any good, and the answer is different in every company.

Almost nobody has ever checked. There is a story I like about this: when Tesco first ran the data from its new loyalty card past the board, the chairman — a man who had spent three decades in the business — said, *what scares me about this is that you know more about my customers after three months than I know after 30 years.*

He had thirty years of instinct. Nobody had ever measured whether it was right.

### 16

Most experiments come off the audit sheet, from a row marked discovery. That is the disciplined majority and it is correct.

But if all of them do, the company will only ever solve the problems it already has.

So on the third day there is a session that did not exist in my old meeting at all, and it asks a completely different question. Not *what is blocking us*, but: **what would be genuinely interesting to know?** What idea do we have that everybody in this room would bet against, but where we would really like to find out whether there is something in it?

It is the loudest part of the three days.

I know exactly what that question is worth, because the most successful thing I have ever built came out of it and would not have survived any other question.

About twelve years into my career, having been involved in hundreds of businesses across dozens of industries, I founded TBF. The purpose was to take what I had learned from growing companies and give it to as many Romanian entrepreneurs as possible.

There was one problem and it was fundamental. I had no brand with them. I had plenty of success stories and a strong reputation inside the companies I had worked with directly. But in education — as somebody an entrepreneur would come to in order to learn — I was nobody. For two years we did everything I knew worked. Press. Webinars. Newsletters. The whole validated playbook of building an audience, executed competently.

It moved slowly, the way things move when the method is correct and the position is wrong.

Then, one autumn, somebody put an idea on the table. Thirty days. Live. Every single morning, ninety minutes, from ten until half past eleven. Teaching the actual systems I had used to manage and grow businesses. Not a taster, not a sequence of previews with the real material behind a payment page. The whole thing, given away.

Almost nobody in the company believed it could work, and I need to be precise about this because it is the load-bearing part of the story: **I did not believe it either.** The shared assumption in that room was that after three or four days it would be me, a camera and five viewers, in public, for another twenty-six mornings.

And we would almost certainly never have done it if it had needed to hit a sales target.

Not out of cowardice. Out of arithmetic. Somebody would have had to stand up and own a revenue number for an idea that the entire room, including the person who proposed it, privately expected to collapse in week one. No sane manager owns that number. The idea dies in the meeting, politely, and everyone goes back to press and webinars.

Every company I have ever worked with has a graveyard of ideas killed by that question, and not one of them can tell you what was in it.

So I did not propose it as a project. I proposed it as an experiment. The deliverable was not revenue. It was an answer to a question: does teaching everything, free, in public, for thirty consecutive days build the position that two years of correct marketing had not built?

Close to two thousand companies registered. Eleven hundred were still attending the final session, on day thirty.

And then something happened that made no rational sense at all. Although I had taught the complete systems across those thirty days, over six hundred of them bought the paid course that explains the same material.

I could not have predicted that. Nobody could. It is the kind of thing you can only find out by running it.

Then came the part I am proudest of, and nobody applauds it.

I did not scale it.

I looked at a result that any sensible person would have immediately built a department around, and I said: this is a Level 1 process. It worked once. There were a hundred uncontrolled variables in it — the season, the novelty, the mood of the market, my own energy, the fact that nobody had done this before in Romania. I did not know whether I had discovered a method or caught a wave.

**Once is not a method. Once is a story.**

So the following year we ran it again, deliberately, for the specific purpose of finding out which of those two it was.

Eight thousand companies registered. More than five thousand watched every day.

At that point it was Level 2, and it went into the machine. It has now run five times, more than twenty thousand companies have been through it, and it is documented and standardised and run by people other than me.

Notice what the objective would have cost, precisely. Not the eight thousand. **The two thousand** — the first run, the one nobody believed in, the one whose only defensible justification was curiosity. Under a target it never gets proposed. Under an experiment it costs thirty mornings and returns a business line.

James Clear has a line I keep coming back to: losers and winners have the same goals. What they do not have is the same method for finding out what is true.

### 17

Two ways of getting all of this wrong, and they are mirror images of each other.

**The large organisation has a machine and is frightened of it.** Touching it wrongly can cost hundreds of millions, so nobody touches it. Instead the company sets objectives — which sound like ambition and function as its opposite, because when you demand a result and forbid risk, everybody reaches for what is already in the machine. Nobody explores. Not because they are incurious, but because they feel they cannot afford to be. Learning dies quietly and on schedule, while every indicator looks fine.

And then, because the instinct is fundamentally sound, the exploration migrates outside the walls — into corporate venture arms, incubators, acquired startups, innovation labs several floors and one legal entity away from the operation. That instinct is correct: discovery does belong beside the machine rather than inside it. It is simply an extremely expensive way to buy something the company could have built for the price of a few experiments a quarter, if only its own people had been permitted to be wrong on a Tuesday.

**The startup has the opposite problem.** No machine at all, and a culture that tells it constantly to move fast. So it takes its assumptions and its founders' previous experience and treats them as validated. The sales approach that worked at somebody's last company. The pricing model that seems obviously right. All of it goes into the operation and gets hired around and built on — declared Level 2 without ever having been honestly run at Level 1, because in that culture Level 1 sounds like hesitation.

Then it scales something that only ever worked because the founder was in the room.

Two failures at opposite ends of the scale, and they are the same failure. Both have got the relationship between experimentation and the machine wrong. One has a machine and will not experiment. The other does not experiment at all and calls the result a machine.

Which lets me say something precise about the advice the startup was given. I am not against speed. I am against speed in the wrong direction.

> **Move fast in experimentation and validation. Do not move fast in putting things into the machine.**

That is where the damage is, and it is the only place in this entire system where slowness is a virtue. A company that runs twenty experiments in a quarter and adds two validated processes is moving faster than a company that adds twenty unvalidated ones — even though the second looks busier and feels braver.

### 18

Now let me go back to the beginning, and to the thing I have not yet told you.

I described four rooms and said that nobody inside them knows which one they are in. That was true of the four rooms as I described them, because in all four the only instrument on the table was the number.

Here is a fifth room.

It is a rented house in the off-season, somewhere outside the city, with empty corridors and a restaurant that opens just for us. Tables in a horseshoe. Two flipcharts. Every telephone in the company upstairs in a bedroom.

Taped to the wall at the front is a single sheet of paper. Several rows, five columns, and the last column is the gap. If you read down it, every entry is negative. Not one indicator is where the company said it should be.

And the room is in a good mood.

I want you to sit with how strange that is.

At the far end of the horseshoe, two people are arguing about one of the rows with real energy — the way people argue about a crossword clue, not the way people argue about blame. The woman who owns four of those rows is not defending anything. She is at the second flipchart, drawing the sequence of steps that produces the number in the third row, and she has just crossed out her own first attempt because somebody pointed out that she left out a step.

She looks pleased about it. Somebody laughs.

Over the next three days, nobody in this room will say the word *unfortunately*. Nobody will begin a sentence with *in fairness*. Nobody will produce a slide explaining that the market was difficult. Two people will volunteer for work that has a serious chance of producing nothing.

And at the end of it, they will know which room they are in. Not because the numbers were good — the numbers are all short. Because every gap has a location, every location has a process behind it, and every process has a person who went and stood in front of it before they came.

That is the whole difference, and it comes down to which question the first morning is organised around.

In the old room, the first question was, in effect: *who missed their number?*

In this one, the first question is: *what is blocking the machine?*

Those are not two ways of asking the same thing. One of them has a person as its answer. The other has a mechanism. And everything that people describe as culture — whether anybody brings bad news early, whether anybody proposes something that might not work, whether the quiet person with the actual data says anything at all — follows from which of those two questions the room is built around. You do not fix that with values on a wall. You fix it by changing what the first question is.

There is one more consequence, and it is the one I did not see coming.

The fifth chapter described what Kahneman and Klein established about skilled intuition: that it requires an environment regular enough to be predictable, plus prolonged practice with rapid feedback. And that a company which changes its rules every quarter is a low-validity environment, in which nobody can ever become genuinely good at anything.

A culture of objectives is exactly that environment. Every ninety days the priorities reset, a new number arrives, and the thing that mattered in the spring is not mentioned in the autumn. The people inside it never accumulate real judgement, because the thing they were learning keeps being dismantled.

The audit does the reverse. The vision holds. The machine holds. The processes hold. What changes from quarter to quarter is which experiments are running *beside* the machine, in a bounded space built for exactly that. Stability everywhere, variation in one controlled place.

That is not only how you protect the machine. It is the only arrangement in which human beings become good at their work.

---

### 19

So the audit ends, and it produces three things, not two.

**A list of multiplications** — things the machine already knows how to do, which need more of.

**A list of experiments** — things nobody knows how to do yet, which need to be found out.

And the third one, which almost every company forgets, and which the first chapter promised you.

Look at the diagram again and at the thing in it that surprises everybody. The two branches do not point back at the machine. They point back at the **vision**.

When an experiment succeeds, or when a multiplied unit finally holds the standard, you have produced two different things and they are easy to confuse. The first is a method or a piece of capacity, and that does belong inside the machine — built beside, validated, merged. The second is **information about what this business is now capable of**, and that does not belong in the machine at all.

Because you wrote the vision using what you knew at the time, and what you knew at the time is precisely what the quarter has just changed.

You discover a way of acquiring customers at half the cost you had assumed was structural. That does not merely make this quarter better. It means the profit range in your vision was written against a constraint that no longer exists, and leaving it there is now a failure of ambition. Or you multiply a unit and find that the economics quietly degrade at the fourth location, for reasons nobody could have predicted from the first three. That is not only an operational problem. It means the number of locations in your vision was a guess, and you now have evidence.

So the last thing that happens in the three days is that somebody asks whether the vision is still true.

This is what stops a vision from turning into a framed piece of paper nobody reads. It is not revised because somebody got excited in a meeting. It is revised because the company learned something and can point at what it learned.

And it is also how you know when you have arrived. The third chapter gave the test and it is unglamorous: you have reached your vision when the machine produces the vision's result repeatably and predictably, without extraordinary intervention. Not when you hit the number. **When hitting the number has stopped being interesting.** At which point every gap on the first sheet is zero, and the honest thing to do is to raise the vision to the next level and start again.

There is one company I keep thinking about in connection with all of this, and it is the last thing I will tell you in this chapter.

Toyota ran a suggestion system for decades — the most famous employee suggestion system in the world, copied by everybody, quoted in every management book. It grew, year after year, and in 1986 it received two million six hundred and forty-eight thousand suggestions. Nearly forty-eight per eligible person.

Then, in the early nineties, Toyota looked at its own famous number and did not like what it saw. Its own account of the period says that the competitive fever over the quantity of proposals had made it difficult for supervisors to guide their people through actual improvement.

So Toyota abolished the company-wide numerical targets and shifted the criteria to the quality of what was proposed.

In 1991 the system received two million and seventy-six thousand suggestions. By 1993 it received nine hundred and twenty-nine thousand.

The company most celebrated in the world for a metric cut that metric by more than half, on purpose, because the metric had begun to lie to it.

That is what an audit is. It is not a meeting where the numbers are presented. It is the recurring willingness to ask whether the instrument is still telling you the truth — and to say out loud, four times a year, in a room where saying it costs nothing, that you do not know.

In the first chapter I wrote that a company can have its best quarter and become a worse business.

The reverse is also true, and this chapter has really been about the reverse.

> **A company can miss every single number in its vision and become a better business** — if what it learned in those ninety days is now inside the machine, working through other people, on an ordinary Tuesday.

Which is the room I have just described. Every row short. Nobody failed. A great deal of work to do. Some of those rows will be marked *multiplication*, and the company will go and do more of what it already knows how to do.

And some of them will be marked *discovery* — which means that for the next ninety days a small group of people will be trying to find out something the company does not currently know.

*That is the harder branch, and it is the one nobody is taught: what to do when the answer is not in the machine, and somebody has to go and find it.*
