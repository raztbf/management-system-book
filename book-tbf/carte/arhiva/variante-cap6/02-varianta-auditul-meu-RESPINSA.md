# 6. The Output and the Audit

### Three Thousand and Twenty-Three Companies, and the Thing I Had Not Wanted to Find

---

### 1

A few years ago, for reasons that had more to do with my personal life than with my work, I decided to audit my own system.

I want to be honest about the spirit in which I did it, because the spirit matters and it was not heroic. I was not looking for a flaw. I had no thesis. I had built something over fifteen years, thousands of companies were running it, and I wanted to know which parts of it were carrying weight and which parts were decoration. That is all. It was the kind of housekeeping a person does when they have been doing something long enough to suspect that some of it is habit.

I measured a lot of things that year. Most of them told me what I expected.

One of them did not, and it took me the better part of two years to accept what it was telling me.

---

### 2

Let me describe what I had built, because otherwise none of what follows lands.

Over the first fifteen years of my working life I was directly involved in the management and growth of more than three hundred companies, across twenty-three industries. Somewhere in the middle of that I started writing down what I was doing, because I kept doing the same things and I got tired of reinventing them. What came out of it was a management system.

The architecture was simple enough to draw on a napkin, and I drew it on a great many napkins.

It began with **vision, mission and values.** Then it split into two branches.

The first branch was **operational management**. If you have read the fourth and fifth chapters of this book you already know most of it — processes, indicators, standards, roles, the discipline of only letting validated things into the operation. It has been improved since, and some of it has been rebuilt, but the bones are the same. That branch is what I now call the machine.

The second branch was **growth management**. Everything a company needed to do in order to get bigger, better, more profitable. Everything that was not simply running the thing you already had.

And the principal instrument of that second branch — the engine of the whole growth side of the system — was **objectives.**

---

### 3

Objectives need a rhythm, and the rhythm I built was the quarterly meeting.

Three days, four times a year, away from the offices. Tables in a horseshoe. Flipcharts along one wall. And one rule that I introduced at the very beginning and never softened: **the telephones stayed upstairs, in the bedrooms.** Not on the table. Not face down beside the notepad. Upstairs.

The three days had a shape.

We evaluated the quarter that had ended, and then we planned the quarter that was starting.

The evaluation worked like this. Each of the top managers went to the front of the room, one at a time, and presented the objectives they had personally owned. Where they had succeeded. Where they had not. What had happened. The room asked questions.

Then we planned. We decided what we wanted to happen in the coming ninety days, we turned those wants into objectives, each objective got an owner who accepted it in front of everybody, and we went back into the business with a strategy.

I taught that meeting to thousands of companies. If you have ever sat in a quarterly planning session anywhere in Eastern Europe, there is a reasonable chance you were sitting inside a room I designed.

---

### 4

And it worked.

I need to be emphatic about this, because everything in this chapter depends on it and because the version of this argument in which the tool never worked is both false and useless.

The system is used today by more than three thousand companies. Fifty of them are leaders in their markets. I have watched businesses go from one or two million to over a hundred million running it. Separately from that — and these are different populations, not a conversion rate — more than thirty thousand companies have implemented parts of it, picked up from my lectures and courses.

I did not just use objectives. I extended them. When I could see the instrument straining in particular places, I did what a craftsman does with a tool he loves: I built better versions. I invented two variations of my own, which I called Dynamic OKRs and Progressive OKRs, each designed to repair a specific failure mode I kept meeting. I wrote well over a hundred articles about the power of goal setting.

And I was not alone, and it was not a Romanian eccentricity. Google ran on OKRs for years and it helped them. Half the technology industry runs on some descendant of the same idea.

So when I tell you what the audit found, understand that I am not a critic who never used the tool. I am the person who handed it to you.

---

### 5

The first thing I measured was the simplest thing available: of the objectives set in those quarterly meetings, how many were actually achieved?

I ran it across **three thousand and twenty-three companies.** Not a sample, and not the good ones — essentially the whole population running the full system.

Fifty-six percent of the objectives were achieved. Forty-four percent were not.

I want to tell you exactly how I reacted to that number, because it matters.

I did not react at all.

There was no moment of recognition. Nothing jumped. Fifty-six percent, in a portfolio of ambitious quarterly objectives, across three thousand real companies with real markets and real staff turnover, is a perfectly respectable figure. Every consultant alive has an explanation ready for the other forty-four: stretch goals are supposed to be missed, seventy percent attainment is the healthy range, if you hit all of them you did not aim high enough.

I had used those explanations. I had taught them.

So I wrote the number down and moved on to the next thing on the list.

---

### 6

And then — I genuinely do not know why, and I have thought about it since — I went back to it.

I did not want a better headline figure. I wanted to know something else. **What did the objectives that were achieved have in common with each other? And what did the ones that were missed have in common?**

So I kept going. I went back into the data and I sorted the two piles by every variable I could think of. Industry. Company size. Seniority of the owner. Whether the objective was financial or operational. How ambitious it was. How long the company had been running the system.

Most of those told me nothing, which is how you know you have found something when one of them finally does.

**Ninety-seven percent of the objectives that were achieved shared a single property: the company already possessed a validated method for producing that result.**

Let me make it concrete, because in the abstract it sounds like a technicality and it is not.

A company has three locations. All three work. The economics are known, the layout is known, the hiring is known, the path to break-even is known, because they have walked it three times. The objective for next year is to open two more.

They open two more.

The method existed. The experience existed. The processes existed. What the objective did was point at a capability that was already sitting in the building, and schedule it.

Now the other pile.

**Eighty-eight percent of the objectives that were missed shared the opposite property: nobody in the company knew the method.**

*Grow sales by thirty percent.* How? Nobody knew. That was the entire content of the objective — a number, a deadline, an owner, and a hole where the cause should have been. *Enter two new markets.* How? *Cut churn in half.* How?

Read the two figures next to each other and the conclusion arrives without any help from me.

Where a method existed, it got applied, and the objective took the credit. Where no method existed, the objective sat in a document for ninety days, generated a great deal of anxiety, and then generated an explanation.

> **In three thousand companies, an objective never once produced a method.**

The first chapter of this book drew a line between a result and a cause: a result is what happened, a cause is what makes it happen again. It took me until that spreadsheet to see that the instrument I had built my professional life around sat on the wrong side of that line.

An objective is a **result written in advance**. It is a result with a date on it and somebody's name beside it. It belongs to the same family as the thing it is supposed to produce, which is precisely why it cannot produce it.

---

### 7

There is an obvious question sitting in the middle of that finding, and it is the question that turned this from a statistic into a chapter.

If eighty-eight percent of the missed objectives were things nobody knew how to do — **why did anybody accept them?**

These were not naive companies. These were organisations with capable leadership teams, sitting in a room, in a structured meeting, with three days and no distractions. Somebody stands up and proposes growing sales by thirty percent with no method. The correct response, from any of the eight other people in the room, is: *how?*

Nobody says it. Or somebody says it once, receives an answer made of adjectives, and lets it go.

I know why, because I built the room it happens in.

**People want to be heroes. And most organisational systems are built to reward them for it.**

Look at what actually happens in that moment. An objective with no method is on the table. It is difficult, it is important, and everybody privately knows it is a wish. And into that silence steps somebody — there is always somebody — who says, in effect: *I'll do it.*

That person is not being reckless. They are responding correctly to the incentives in front of them. Taking on the impossible number is how you get noticed. It is how you get the promotion, the budget, the reputation, the sense that you matter here. And if you fail, you will have failed at something hard, which is a far better thing to have on your record than having declined something hard.

The second chapter of this book defined an organisational hero as the person whose exceptional intervention repeatedly compensates for a capability the system has not yet built. It said that every hero in your company is standing in front of a hole — that the hero is not the solution to the hole, the hero is the *shape* of the hole, and that as long as they keep standing there, nobody can see how big it is.

What I had not understood, until I sorted that data, was **where the hole comes from.**

It comes from a planning meeting. Four times a year. Conducted by serious people with the best intentions, using a methodology I wrote.

An objective set on ground where nobody knows the method *is* the hole. And the person who volunteers for it is the shape of it.

---

### 8

My first instinct, when I saw the two numbers, was that I had made a mistake somewhere.

That is a reasonable instinct and I recommend it. But it did not survive the arithmetic. Three thousand and twenty-three companies is not a sample that produces an accident of that size. Ninety-seven and eighty-eight are not the kind of numbers that come out of a coding error or a bad year. If the effect had been ten points I would have argued with it for a decade. It was not ten points.

So the next thing I did was go and look for somebody who had seen it before me.

---

### 9

I want to tell you what I found, because it was not what I expected and it changed how I understood my own contribution.

**The problem was not undiscovered. It had been documented for decades, in good journals, by serious people. What nobody had built was a replacement.**

In 1987, the *Journal of Applied Psychology* published a meta-analysis covering a hundred and twenty-five studies from the previous two decades. It asked a single question: does the effect of a goal change depending on how complex the task is?

It does. The effect shrinks, steadily and measurably, as the work gets harder. Simple, well-understood tasks respond strongly to a target. Complex ones — business simulations, engineering, scientific work, research productivity — respond much more weakly.

And the authors' own explanation of *why* is the thesis of this entire chapter, written in 1987, in academic language, thirty-odd years before I stumbled into it with a spreadsheet:

> On complex tasks, the effect of a goal depends on whether the person can discover an appropriate strategy — and people vary enormously in that ability.

The strategy is the method. On complex work, a goal does nothing except reveal whether the person already had a way to do it.

That is my three thousand companies, arrived at from a completely different direction, with real statistical machinery behind it.

And then I looked at the third author on the paper.

It was **Edwin A. Locke** — the man who, with Gary Latham, founded goal-setting theory. The architect of the entire field. The person whose work every corporate target-setting seminar on earth is downstream of.

This is not a critic of objectives demonstrating that objectives fail. **This is the founder publishing the boundary condition of his own theory, himself, in a top-tier journal, where it has sat in plain sight ever since.**

> **The founder published the limit. What travelled was the part that fit on a slide.**

I had been shipping the headline result — specific difficult goals beat do-your-best — into every context I could reach, including the contexts its inventor had explicitly marked as the ones where it degrades. So had everybody else.

There was more, and some of it was even closer.

In 2019, a team of researchers published a study in *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* that named the mechanism. Outcome goals — *hit this number* — trigger what psychologists call a **prevention focus**: a mental state organised around not failing rather than around succeeding. Those sound like the same thing and they are not. A person organised around succeeding scans for opportunity. A person organised around not failing scans for exposure. And prevention focus increases unethical conduct aimed specifically at avoiding the failure. Worse: **the effect intensifies as the goal gets harder**, which is the exact opposite of the folklore in which stretch targets bring out people's best.

But that paper also tested something else. It compared outcome goals against **learning goals** — framed as *find out how to do this* rather than *deliver this number*.

Learning goals reduced prevention focus. They reduced unethical behaviour. And they did it **without reducing performance**, even when the goals were difficult.

Somebody had put the piece on the table. In 2019. In a peer-reviewed journal.

Nobody had built the meeting around it.

That, I eventually decided, was the work. Not discovering that objectives fail on unknown ground — that was known, and I was late. **The work was building something a company could actually run on Monday morning instead.**

---

### 10

The sentence that made all of it click came from somewhere else entirely, and it came from my personal life rather than my professional one.

James Clear, in *Atomic Habits*: **winners and losers have the same goals.**

I had read that line years earlier and nodded at it the way you nod at something clever. I had even, without connecting it to anything, already proved it on myself — the second chapter of this book describes how I finally stopped a twenty-year cycle of gaining weight and dramatically losing it, and the thing that worked was not a smaller target. It was auditing the machine that produced the body I had, running experiments on what the audit implicated, and building in whatever survived.

I had solved it on myself before I understood what I had solved.

Winners and losers have the same goals. What they do not have is the same method for finding out what is true.

---

### 11

Here is what I finally understood, and it is the simplest idea in this book and it cost me fifteen years.

**Objectives are an excellent instrument when you know the method. When you are working in the known.**

**And they are toxic when you are working in the unknown.**

The fourth chapter gave you that distinction as a principle of architecture: everything a company does is either known or unknown, the known is managed through processes and indicators and standards, the unknown is managed through experiments, and *managing the unknown through targets is damage.*

I stated it there as though it were obvious.

I want you to know what it cost to find. It was not obvious. It was the conclusion of an audit I ran on my own life's work, hoping to find something to polish, and it arrived as a verdict rather than an insight.

And it explains something I had watched for years without understanding.

In 2023, a study in *Organization Science* mapped what challenging goals do to the choice between exploiting what you know and exploring what you do not. The chain is recognisable from any real meeting. People working under challenging goals interpret weak early feedback as failure **faster**. Having called it failure, they abandon promising alternatives **sooner**. They exploit more and explore less. And when the environment shifts, they adapt slowly — not because they are slow, but because they have nothing prepared. They spent the whole period pushing on the one thing.

The authors' conclusion is the most useful sentence about objectives I have read: that concentration on exploitation is **beneficial in stable environments and harmful in unstable ones.**

Nobody reading this is operating in a stable environment.

---

### 12

Now the part that is genuinely difficult to write.

How did I not see it?

I invented two OKR methodologies. I wrote over a hundred articles about goal setting. I taught the system to thirty thousand companies. I sat in that quarterly meeting, four times a year, for fifteen years, watching capable people accept objectives with no method attached and then explain themselves ninety days later. The gap was in front of me the entire time.

There are two honest answers and neither of them is flattering.

**The first is that it worked.** Not sometimes — more than half the time. And the half that worked was visible, celebratory, attributable and repeatable. Every quarter, in every company, some objectives landed, and each one was a small piece of evidence that the instrument was sound. The failures were explained by circumstances. The successes were explained by the method.

An instrument that succeeds fifty-six percent of the time and has a ready-made excuse for the rest is almost impossible to falsify from the inside. That is not a weakness in me. It is a structural property of the thing.

**The second answer is worse. I wanted to be a winner.**

I built a system that produced winners and losers, and I built it because I was one. I was extremely good at the game I had designed. I could take an impossible objective, own it in front of a room, and deliver it through sheer capacity and hours and force. That is what I was best at, and I built an entire management methodology that put that skill at the centre of the quarter and taught thirty thousand companies to do the same.

The second chapter of this book says that companies become what they celebrate. I wrote the thing that decided what got celebrated.

---

### 13

So let me say plainly what an objective does, now that I have stopped being defensive about it.

**An objective applies pressure. It does not produce learning.**

Watch what it does to a person. It pushes them to please the room and to please themselves. It rewards the sentence *yes, I will hit that number* — a number nobody in the room believes is reachable — and it rewards saying it with a date attached.

What it never, under any circumstances, pushes anybody toward is this: **I want to run experiments until I find the best method.**

That sentence does not survive in a room organised around objectives. It sounds like hedging. It sounds like somebody managing their own exposure. It has no date in it, no promise, nothing to celebrate in ninety days.

And it is almost always the correct answer.

The second chapter described the loop that manufactures heroes: goal, action, output, dissatisfaction, new goal. With the part nobody plans for — that the dissatisfaction arrives **even when the goal is achieved**, shortly after everyone has got used to the result. The impossible client becomes a client who now expects delivery. The record quarter becomes next quarter's baseline.

What I can add now, from the data, is that the loop does not merely exhaust people. **It systematically produces nothing.** In three thousand companies, the objectives that succeeded were the ones the company could already have done. The loop was spinning at full speed and generating no new capability at all.

---

### 14

I see this most clearly in large organisations, and the pattern is so consistent that I can now predict it from the outside.

**A large company milks.** It takes the systems it already has and it squeezes them, quarter after quarter, harder each time. It does not explore. It does not go looking for better methods. It optimises the thing it has until the thing it has stops working, and then it is surprised.

And the mechanism is not stupidity. It is the quarterly objective, operating exactly as designed.

Every ninety days, serious objectives are set. Difficult ones. And people take them on — because they want to be heroes, because that is how you demonstrate value, because that is how you earn more and get the better opportunities. And once you have taken on a difficult number with a date attached, you will reach for the only thing that can reliably deliver it, which is whatever is already validated and sitting in the machine.

Nobody explores, because exploration cannot promise a number by December. Learning dies quietly and on schedule, while every indicator on the dashboard looks fine.

In December 2000, a man named James McNerney became chief executive of 3M. He came from General Electric, and the market approved: the share price rose about twenty percent in the days after the announcement. He was the first outsider to run the company in its hundred-year history.

He brought Six Sigma with him — a serious, well-evidenced, entirely legitimate methodology for reducing variation in a process. He installed it across the company.

Including in the laboratories.

The financial results were real. Profits grew by an average of twenty-two percent a year. Operating margin went from seventeen percent in 2001 to twenty-three percent in 2005.

And the proportion of 3M's sales coming from products introduced in the previous five years — for a company whose entire identity, for a century, had been built on that one number — fell from about a third to about a quarter.

McNerney left after four and a half years. His successor, George Buckley, explained what had gone wrong in terms that belong in the fourth chapter of this book:

> Invention is by its very nature a disorderly process. You can't put a Six Sigma process into that area and say, well, I'm getting behind on invention, so **I'm going to schedule myself for three good ideas on Wednesday and two on Friday.** That's not how creativity works.

And one of the vice presidents who ran the research side described the actual mechanism, which is more banal and more instructive than the philosophy:

> In some cases in the lab it made sense, but in other cases, **people were going around dreaming up green-belt programs to fill their quota of green-belt programs for that time period.** We were letting, I think, the process get in the way of doing the actual invention.

The metric was how many improvement projects you completed. So people produced improvement projects.

Now here is the part that makes this a story about the known and the unknown rather than a story about Six Sigma.

**Buckley did not abolish Six Sigma.** He removed the obligation on 3M's research scientists to work to Six Sigma objectives. Not from manufacturing. Not from logistics. Not from the rest of the company, where it stayed, and where it belonged, and where it was doing exactly what it was designed to do.

He took the instrument for the known out of the one place in the company that was working on the unknown.

That is the whole distinction, executed by a chief executive on a hundred-year-old company, and it cost 3M about five years to learn.

---

### 15

Which brings me to the thing I got most wrong, and it is not a technique. It is what the room was for.

I have thought for a long time about how to describe my old quarterly meeting honestly, and the most accurate description I have is this:

**It was an awards ceremony for heroes.**

Who was the hero this quarter, and who was not. That was the actual content of day one, underneath the slides and the market context and the discussion of what had got in the way. Everything else was decoration hung on those two questions.

And the strangest part is that everybody knew. I knew. We did it four times a year, for fifteen years, in a house with the phones upstairs, because we were serious people who took the business seriously and this was what serious people did.

Now compare the two questions a room can be organised around.

*Let us find out who did best.*

*Let us find out the most we can.*

Those are not two moods. They are two different machines, and they produce two different companies.

Because here is the thing that took me fifteen years to see: **we do not actually need heroes. What we need is to know which methods work for what we are trying to do.** A hero is what you reach for when you do not have that. And a room organised around finding the hero is a room that will never go looking for the method, because the method is not what is being rewarded in it.

If you want to see what that costs when it is taken to its conclusion, look at what happened to the Atlanta public school system.

Beverly Hall ran it from 1999 to 2010. In 2009 she was named National Superintendent of the Year by the American Association of School Administrators, for what the citation described as significant gains in student achievement over the previous decade.

In 2011, a special investigation ordered by the governor of Georgia found cheating on the state competency tests in **forty-four of the fifty-six schools it examined**. It identified a hundred and seventy-eight educators as involved. Eighty-two of them confessed. **Thirty-eight were principals.**

The investigators' report has a section headed, simply, *Targets*. Their finding:

> The unreasonable pressure to meet annual "targets" was the **primary motivation** for teachers and administrators to cheat.

Hall's own formulation, quoted in the report, was: *No exceptions. No excuses.* If principals did not meet targets within three years, she said, they would be replaced, and *I will find someone who will meet targets.* She replaced ninety percent of the principals during her tenure.

And then there is the detail that I cannot get out of my head, because it is my quarterly meeting with the volume turned all the way up.

Every year the district held a system-wide celebration at the Georgia Dome. **The faculty of schools that hit their targets sat on the floor of the stadium. The faculty of schools that did not were seated in the uppermost sections.**

An awards ceremony. Made of architecture. Once a year, for everybody to see.

I want to be careful here, and so were the investigators, and their care is worth borrowing:

> **We do not express any opinion as to the merits of targets.** However, targets were implemented by APS in such a way that teachers and administrators believed that they had to choose between cheating to meet targets or failing to meet targets and losing their jobs.

That is not a story about bad teachers. Eighty-two people confessed. Those are not the actions of a criminal class; they are the actions of ordinary people who were placed in a room where the only two available identities were winner and loser, and who found the third door.

And it is not a story that requires a scandal, either. The fifth chapter of this book already showed you the quieter version. W. Edwards Deming used to run a factory in front of a room full of executives — a box of four thousand beads, eight hundred of them red, a paddle with fifty holes in it, and a standard of no more than three red beads. Then he would praise the best worker, coach the worst, put a chart on the wall, introduce merit pay, announce a stretch goal, and eventually lay off the three worst performers.

Every one of those interventions is a reasonable management action. I have taken all of them. And not one of them changes anything, because there are eight hundred red beads in the box.

**My quarterly meeting was the red bead experiment, run four times a year, for fifteen years, with real people.** And everything I did in it was reasonable.

---

### 16

So what do you put in a room instead?

Not a softer version of the same thing. Not gentler objectives, or fewer of them, or objectives with a nicer conversation attached. The problem is not the tone of the meeting. The problem is what the meeting is *for*.

**You build a room organised around learning, and the extraordinary thing about experiments is that they do not produce winners and losers at all.**

I mean that structurally, not emotionally. In an experiment, nobody in the room has any stake in the result coming out one way rather than the other. Nothing was promised except the finding. Nobody's competence is attached to a particular answer. What everybody in the room wants is real information out of reality, so that it can be used to improve the machine.

There is no winner available. There is also no loser.

And once you have built that room, something happens to objectives that surprised me when I first watched it: **they mostly become irrelevant.**

Not wrong. Not forbidden. Irrelevant — in the specific sense that there is nothing left for them to do in that part of the work. When the question on the table is *what is actually true here*, a promise about a number is not a contribution.

But they do not disappear from the company, and I want to be precise about where they stay, because this is the part people get wrong in both directions.

**Objectives belong in the machine. In the known. Their job is to focus resources.**

Suppose that in a given quarter you need to concentrate more of the company's capacity on one part of the machine — a validated process that needs to run at higher volume, in more places, through more people. An objective does that job extremely well. It converts a general intention into a specific number that a specific person will be asked about on a specific date, and that conversion is most of what management was invented for.

And notice what happens to attainment there. In that territory, people hit their objectives — because the method is validated, the process exists, and the work is genuinely within their power.

Which is exactly what my audit found. Ninety-seven percent.

**It is the other territory that matters. The place where we do not know how.** That is where the largest problems in every company live, and it is where an objective does the most damage — because there, you do not want a room that produces winners and losers.

> **When you do not know how, the goal is not the result. The goal is learning.**

And the reason is mechanical rather than philosophical. The moment you attach a required result to ground where no method exists, you have created a context with exactly two exits. People either carry ninety days of stress for a number they have no way to produce — or they take their best current guess, commit to it publicly, and defend it for the rest of the quarter because their competence is now attached to it.

The first chapter of this book called that converting uncertainty into premature commitment. I wrote that sentence before I could prove it.

I can prove it now. Eighty-eight percent.

---

### 17

So here is what the three days became.

The container did not change. Same length, same rule about the telephones, same reason for being out of the office — three days because the first half-day is spent arriving, and out of the building because context leaks. In your own offices everybody is sitting inside their department, their status, yesterday's meeting and tomorrow's.

What changed is that nobody presents.

On the first morning, what goes on the table is not a person's slides. It is **the indicators from the vision** — the Ordinary Great Quarter from the third chapter, the description of what this machine should produce when it is running normally and nobody is being a hero.

And each indicator gets three columns, not one.

**What it did last quarter. What it did this quarter. What the vision says it should do.**

The first comparison tells you direction: what improved, what deteriorated, what became unstable, what became normal. That is real information about the machine and a company that skips it is throwing away a quarter of data.

The second comparison tells you distance. And that is a completely different question — not *are we better than we were*, but *is this machine producing what we have said it should produce by default.*

The gap between the third column and the fourth is the entire output of the first session.

Three things about that sheet that took me a long time to get right.

**The vision column is a range, not a point.** Reality has weather in it. A default that cannot survive an ordinary bad month is not a description of a machine, it is a wish with a decimal place.

**It says "without extraordinary intervention," and that phrase is the one everybody deletes first.** If the number only arrives when the founder spends nine months personally producing it, the machine does not produce that number. A person does. The gap measures what the machine does **without heroics** — which means a quarter rescued by somebody's heroic effort has not closed the gap. It has hidden it.

**And there has to be more than one line.** The third chapter explained why and it applies with full force here: a single number can always be bought at the expense of something else. Revenue can be purchased with margin. Growth can be purchased with debt. A sheet with one line on it cannot tell you whether you had a good quarter or an expensive one.

Then something happens on that first morning that could not happen in my old room, and the first time I saw it I did not know what to do with it.

Every indicator has improved on the quarter before. And every indicator is still short of the vision.

> **Nobody in this room failed, and there is a great deal of work to do.**

Both true at once. In the old room those two facts could not coexist — an objective was hit or missed, a person was the hero of the quarter or the disappointment, and there was no available state in which somebody had done real, measurable, improving work and the company was still meaningfully short of where it needs to be.

Which is, incidentally, the state that most good companies are in most of the time.

---

### 18

And now the rule that governs everything else in the three days, and that I get asked about more than any other thing in this book.

**A gap is not a bad sign about a person. It is information about the machine.**

I need to be exact about why, because the polite version of this — *let's not point fingers* — is not what I mean and is not strong enough.

When we look at a shortfall and conclude that a particular person is the reason for it, we have said something without noticing that we said it. We have said that the person is more important than the machine. **We have made them a hero.**

And that is not a compliment we can afford, because it hands all the power to a human being and takes it away from the thing the company actually owns. The fourth chapter called this the single most expensive misdiagnosis available to a founder — an architecture problem wearing a people problem's clothes — and it is expensive precisely because the evidence for the wrong conclusion is standing in front of you every day, in the form of people who are visibly not performing.

Does that mean the person is never at fault? No.

The fifth chapter defined a responsibility precisely: an indicator, with a standard, **that is within that person's power.** A person is at fault when they did not deliver something that sat entirely inside that boundary. That happens, and when it happens you deal with it.

But it is rarely the reason a machine is not producing. Far more often the role was wrong, or the responsibilities were wrong, or the recruitment process for that role was wrong and the wrong person is sitting in it — which is, again, a fact about the machine.

Say the man is at fault and you have given him all the power. Find that the process is at fault and you have given the power back to the machine, which is the asset you own, and which you can actually change.

---

### 19

So you have a list of gaps. The second sheet turns each of them into a question:

> **What is blocking this machine from producing the output described in the vision?**

And the answer is not allowed to be an opinion. It comes out of the indicators of the validated processes inside the machine.

Here is what that actually looks like, and it is the arithmetic from the fifth chapter run backwards. You have a gap in new clients. The sales process is a chain: companies identified, people reached, qualifying conversations, proposals sent, contracts signed. Each link has an indicator, and each indicator has a standard that was **derived** from the output the vision requires — a hundred calls a day was not chosen because it sounded ambitious, it was calculated backwards from the number of contracts.

So you walk the chain until you find the link running below its standard. That is the bottleneck. It has a location, and a location can be worked on.

If you cannot fill that column with real, named, validated processes — if the honest answer is *sales, generally* — then you do not have a machine there. You have activity. And a room full of senior people looking at a gap with no processes behind it will do the only thing available to them, which is argue. The best arguer wins, and the best arguer is almost never the person closest to the data.

That is what my old room was for fifteen years.

But the indicators will only take you so far, and this is the part that cannot be done in the meeting.

The first chapter told you about Taiichi Ohno drawing a circle in chalk on a factory floor, putting a young engineer inside it, and coming back hours later to ask what he had seen. The point was never endurance. It was that the first hour of observation shows you what you already believe, and somewhere in the third hour the model in your head breaks and you begin to see what is actually there. The operator walking four steps for a part that could be at his elbow, a thousand times a shift. The small stack of components that is never zero.

None of that is in the data. All of it is in the room where the work happens.

**Which means the indicators tell you where. The chalk circle tells you why.** And the chalk circle cannot be drawn in a rented house three hours from your offices.

So the field work happens **before** the three days, not during them. In the weeks beforehand, the people who own the processes the indicators have implicated go and stand in front of the actual work. They watch it. They follow one order, or one client, or one shift, all the way through.

And then they arrive at the meeting having seen something, instead of having read a report about a report.

That also settles the question of who is in the room, and my answer to it has changed completely. Top management, obviously. But also — as a requirement, not a courtesy — **whoever can answer the question with data, regardless of title.** A person who has stood in the circle has something no chart contains. A person two levels above them has a summary of a summary.

There is a failure mode here that is worth naming, because it is what a company does when it cannot answer the question honestly and does not want to say so.

An English hospital trust, some years ago, was found to have a mortality indicator far above what would have been expected — the number of deaths that actually occurred divided by the number that should have occurred, which is exactly the structure of an output compared against a vision. It reported, to its regulator, that the figure had improved substantially. The stated reason, in its own words, was *significant focus on coding.*

An audit of its own records found that eighty percent of the diagnoses required recoding.

The number came down. Almost none of the reason was clinical. And the public inquiry that eventually examined all of it found that the trust, the regional health authority and the national department had **all** focused their attention on the coding, at the expense of considering whether the indicator was telling them something about patient care.

They repaired the instrument. Nobody went to look at the wards.

That is available to every one of us, four times a year, and it does not feel like dishonesty while you are doing it. It feels like rigour.

### 20

Once you have the bottleneck, there are exactly two things you can do about it, and the fourth chapter already told you why: a company can turn some of the unknown into known, or it can make more of the known. **There is no third move.** What looks like a third move — pushing harder on the machine you already have — is not growth. It is borrowing.

**Multiplication** means something already in the machine solves this and there is not enough of it. Take it wherever you can get it. It is the safest and most predictable road, and it is where most of the growth is.

I want to be honest about why I resisted that for years. Entrepreneurs like me want to reinvent the wheel. It is more interesting, and it is how we keep the hero's role. I am not telling you to give up the hero's role — I am telling you not to spend it where the return is small.

And there is one rule on this road that people break constantly, always with good intentions: **you may only multiply Level 2 and above.** If the process that would close the gap has only worked once, then next quarter's job is not multiplication. It is taking that process from Level 1 to Level 2 — and that work happens on the discovery branch, beside the machine, not inside it.

If you want a number for how expensive that instinct is, take it from the most disciplined validation system human beings have ever built. In pharmaceutical development, where the ladder is legally enforced and there are billions of dollars behind getting it right, roughly **seventy percent of drugs that succeed in Phase 2 do not survive Phase 3.** Not because anybody cheated. The Phase 2 result was real. It simply did not generalise.

Seven out of ten things are lost between *it worked* and *it keeps working*, by an industry built around nothing else.

Now consider what your one good quarter proves.

**Discovery** means nothing in the machine solves this. We do not know how. And the honest thing to write in that box is: *we do not know how.* No amount of commitment converts not knowing into knowing.

### 21

Every row marked discovery becomes an experiment, and an experiment has a sheet of its own.

On it: where in the machine this happens. The **hypothesis**, written as *if this, then that*. The **source** — where the idea came from. The **KPIs** — what we will measure. The **constraints**. The **method**.

And at the foot of the page, three fields that people expect to be missing and are surprised to find.

**Owner. Team. Deadline.**

Exactly like an objective. A name, a group, and a date. Nothing here is an argument for vagueness and nothing here is an argument for taking the pressure off.

The difference is two fields higher up the page. Because there is a hypothesis and there are KPIs, what the owner has undertaken to deliver at the deadline is not a result. It is **evidence.**

And evidence can be delivered honestly on a date. A result whose method you do not possess cannot be.

A word about the hypothesis, because it is usually explained wrongly. The point of writing it as *if this, then that* is not humility, and it is not about being willing to be proven wrong. **It is about truth.** A hypothesis in that form makes a claim about how reality works that reality is able to answer. Anything vaguer is a wish, and reality has nothing to say to a wish.

A word about the constraints, too. They are partly the values from the third chapter, finally attached to a specific piece of work — the rules we will not break in order to win. But they are also the **economics of the experiment**, and that matters more than people expect. You do not want an experiment expensive enough to strain the company's finances, and you do not want one that can damage the brand or reach a large number of clients before you know what it does. **The machine pays for the laboratory.** An experiment that endangers the machine has eaten its own funding.

And a word about the source, which looks like bureaucracy and is not. Ideas arrive from somewhere — somebody's previous experience, a consultant, a friend, a competitor, a training course, a book. And what we normally do with information from those sources is treat it as true and put it straight into the machine.

That single habit has cost every one of us more than we would like to count: people hired against an idea that turned out not to work, productivity lost, trust spent, brands damaged. Writing the source down means that in two years you can sort every experiment the company has run by where the idea came from, and find out which sources produce validated methods and which produce nothing.

Almost nobody has ever checked whether their own instincts are any good.

Which brings me to the sentence that changed my quarterly meetings more than any other:

> **You cannot fail at an experiment. The only way to fail is to measure it badly.**

And the reason is not encouragement, it is structure. Nobody is applying pressure for the experiment to come out one way rather than the other. What we want is real information from reality, so that we can improve the machine with it. A properly designed, properly measured experiment always delivers that, because knowledge accumulates in both directions — a hypothesis that turns out to be false has removed a road from the map, permanently, and nobody would ever have paid to learn that deliberately.

There is no winner in that room. There is no loser either.

### 22

I know exactly what this is worth, because the most successful thing I have ever built came out of it, and it could not have survived a single one of my old meetings.

About twelve years into my career, having been involved in hundreds of businesses across dozens of industries, I founded TBF. The purpose was to take what I had learned from growing companies and give it to as many Romanian entrepreneurs as possible.

There was one problem and it was fundamental. **I had no brand with them.** I had plenty of success stories and a strong reputation inside the companies I had worked with directly. But in education — as somebody an entrepreneur would come to in order to learn — I was nobody.

For two years we did everything I knew worked. Press. Webinars. Newsletters. The whole validated playbook, competently executed.

It moved slowly.

Then, one autumn, somebody put an idea on the table. Thirty days. Live. Every single morning, ninety minutes, from ten until half past eleven. Teaching the actual systems I had used to manage and grow businesses. Not a taster, not a sequence of previews with the real material behind a payment page. **The whole thing, given away.**

Almost nobody in the company believed it could work, and I need to be precise about this because it is the load-bearing part of the story: **I did not believe it either.** The shared assumption in that room was that after three or four days it would be me, a camera and five viewers, in public, for another twenty-six mornings.

And we would almost certainly never have done it if it had needed to hit a sales target.

Not out of cowardice. Out of arithmetic. Somebody would have had to stand up and own a revenue number for an idea that the entire room — including the person who proposed it — privately expected to collapse in week one. No sane manager owns that number. The idea dies in the meeting, politely, and everybody goes back to press and webinars.

Every company I have ever worked with has a graveyard of ideas killed by that question, and not one of them can tell you what was in it.

So I did not propose it as a project. I proposed it as an experiment, and the deliverable was not revenue. It was an answer: does teaching everything, free, in public, for thirty consecutive days build the position that two years of correct marketing had not built?

Close to two thousand companies registered. Eleven hundred were still attending the final session, on day thirty.

And then something happened that made no rational sense at all. Although I had taught the complete systems across those thirty days, **over six hundred of them bought the paid course that explains the same material.**

I could not have predicted that. Nobody could. It is the kind of thing you can only find out by running it.

Then came the part I am proudest of, and nobody applauds it.

**I did not scale it.**

Every instinct in the building said to build the next twelve months around it. I looked at a result that any sensible person would have immediately made into a department, and I said: this is a Level 1 process. It worked once. There were a hundred uncontrolled variables in it — the season, the novelty, the mood of the market, my own energy, the fact that nobody had done this in Romania before. I did not know whether I had discovered a method or caught a wave.

So the following year we ran it again. Deliberately. Not to grow it — to find out which of those two it was.

Eight thousand companies registered. More than five thousand watched every day.

At that point it was Level 2, and it went into the machine. It has now run five times, more than twenty thousand companies have been through it, and it is documented and standardised and run by people other than me.

Notice what an objective would have cost, precisely. Not the eight thousand. **The two thousand** — the first run, the one nobody believed in, the one whose only defensible justification was curiosity.

### 23

So the audit ends, and it produces three things.

**A list of multiplications** — things the machine already knows how to do, which need more of.

**A list of experiments** — things nobody knows how to do yet, which need to be found out.

And a third one that almost every company forgets, and that the first chapter promised you.

Look at the diagram again and at the one thing in it that surprises everybody: the two branches do not point back at the machine. They point back at the **vision**.

Because when an experiment succeeds, it produces two different things and they are easy to confuse. The first is a method, and that belongs inside the machine — built beside it, validated, merged. The second is **information about what this business is now capable of**, and that does not belong in the machine at all.

You wrote the vision using what you knew at the time, and what you knew at the time is exactly what the quarter has just changed. Discover a way of acquiring customers at half the cost you had assumed was structural, and the profit range in your vision was written against a constraint that no longer exists. Leaving it there is now a failure of ambition.

So the last thing that happens in the three days is that somebody asks whether the vision is still true. Not because anybody got excited in a meeting. Because the company learned something, and can point at what it learned.

That is the whole difference, and in the end it comes down to which question the first morning is built around.

In the old room, the first question was, in effect: **who missed their number?** The answer to that question is always a person.

In this one, the first question is: **what is blocking the machine?** The answer to that question is always a mechanism.

Everything people describe as culture — whether anybody brings bad news early, whether anybody proposes something that might not work, whether the quiet person with the actual data says anything at all — follows from which of those two questions the room is organised around. You do not fix it with values on a wall. You fix it by changing what the first question is.

I built the first room and taught it to thirty thousand companies. I built it because it was rigorous, and because it worked more than half the time, and because I was very good at winning in it.

It took auditing my own life's work to find out what it was.

In the first chapter I wrote that a company can have its best quarter and become a worse business.

The reverse is also true, and this chapter has really been about the reverse.

> **A company can miss every single number in its vision and become a better business** — if what it learned in those ninety days is now inside the machine, working through other people, on an ordinary Tuesday.

Which leaves the two lists. One of them says multiply, and the company will go and do more of what it already knows how to do.

The other says find out.

*That is the harder branch, and it is the one nobody is taught: what to do when the answer is not in the machine, and somebody has to go and get it.*
