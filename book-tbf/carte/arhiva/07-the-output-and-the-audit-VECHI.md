# 7. The Output and the Audit

### Four Sheets of Paper, Three Days, and the Only Correct Thing to Do With a Number

---

### 1

We are six chapters in, and this is the point where the book stops describing and starts instructing. So before anything else, let us go back to the drawing and see where we are.

![Sistemul TBF: Vision → The Machine → Output → Audit → Discover / Multiply → Vision](imagini/sistemul-tbf.png)

Here is what we have covered.

**The vision** is the description of the default output — the Ordinary Great Quarter. Not a summit, not a list of victories. A quantified description of what this business should produce, within its constraints, when it is running normally and nobody is being a hero. That was the third chapter, and it is the first sheet of paper in this chapter.

**The machine** is the predictable part of the business: every process validated to Level 2 or above, with its indicators, its standards, the roles derived from those standards, and the responsibilities inside those roles. The known, managed through processes rather than experiments. That was the fourth and fifth chapters.

And in the last chapter we cleared away the instrument that most companies would reach for at exactly this point. Objectives belong in the known, where they focus resources on a method you already possess. They do not belong on ground where nobody knows how, because a target cannot manufacture a method — and because a room organised around who hit their number will not go looking for one.

Which leaves the two stations we have named repeatedly and never opened.

**The output.** And **the audit.**

Everything from here is mechanical. There is a rhythm, there is a room, and there are four sheets of paper. If you take nothing else from this chapter, take the four sheets, because between them they are the entire quarterly decision of a company.

---

### 2

Start with what the output actually is, because most companies get this wrong before they have measured anything.

Ask a founder what their business produced last quarter and you will get revenue, profit, and possibly the number of new customers. Three numbers, all financial, all arriving at the end.

That is not the output. **That is the part of the output that reaches the bank.**

> **The output of a business is everything the machine produced.**

At minimum it contains every indicator named in the vision — the Ordinary Great Quarter, in full, including the constraints. And beyond that, it contains every indicator attached to every validated process inside the machine. The lagging measures that tell you what has already happened, and the leading measures that tell you what is currently being built.

The reason this matters is not completeness for its own sake. It is diagnosis, and it is the same argument the fifth chapter made about a single process, now made one level up.

If you measure only the end of a chain, every failure in that chain looks identical.

Revenue is down. Why? Fewer leads? Worse qualification? Longer sales cycle? Smaller deals? Delivery problems producing churn? A price change nobody tracked? Measure only revenue and all six of those produce exactly the same signal — a number lower than you wanted — and the conversation that follows is not a diagnosis. It is a negotiation between people defending their departments, settled by whoever is most senior or most confident.

Measure the whole chain and a bad quarter has a **location.**

This is where the discipline of the fourth chapter gets paid for. Only validated processes are allowed inside the machine, and every one of them carries an indicator and a standard. That reads like administrative fussiness when you are doing it. It is actually the thing that makes it possible for eight people to look at a bad number without turning on each other, because there is somewhere specific to look.

---

### 3

Now the rule that governs this entire chapter, and the one I get wrong more often than any other principle in this book.

> **Output is not something you manage. It is information about the machine.**

Not a target to be pushed. Not a result to be improved. **Evidence, to be read.**

The first chapter said it in four words: output is evidence, the machine is the asset. The moment you begin operating on the output directly — pressuring it, defending it, setting targets against it — you have stopped working on the cause and started working on the readout.

And I want to be precise about why the two produce such different companies, because they can sound similar in a meeting.

**One of them pushes you toward a one-time result. The other pushes you to change the machine so that it produces a repeatable result.**

One ends with a discount, a push, three weeks of overtime and a founder ringing clients at midnight. The other ends with a process being multiplied or an experiment being designed.

> **We improve the machine. We do not rescue the result.**

There is a way this goes wrong that does not feel like dishonesty at all while you are doing it. It feels like rigour, which is what makes it dangerous.

An English hospital trust some years ago found itself with a mortality indicator far above what would have been expected. The indicator was the number of deaths that actually occurred divided by the number that would have been expected given the patients — which is, structurally, exactly an output compared against a default. The national baseline was a hundred. The trust was at a hundred and twenty-seven.

Some time later the trust reported to its regulator that the figure had improved substantially — down to around a hundred and one. The stated reason, in the trust's own words, was **a significant focus on coding.**

An audit of its own records found that eighty percent of the diagnoses required recoding.

The number came down. Almost none of the reason was clinical.

The public inquiry that eventually examined all of this reached a finding that has nothing to do with hospitals and everything to do with this chapter. It found that the trust, the regional health authority and the national department had **all** concentrated their attention on the coding — at the expense of considering whether the indicator was telling them something about the care of patients.

Everybody went to work on the instrument. Nobody went to look at the wards.

That option is available to every one of us, four times a year. When a number is bad, the cheapest available response is to attack the number: question the definition, adjust the classification, argue about the methodology. It is intellectually respectable, it is often partly justified, and it produces a better figure without producing a better business.

The inquiry also recorded that one director had argued for an independent analysis, and the reason he gave was, in his words, that he wanted **to understand this, not to explain it away.**

He was overruled.

### 4

So: once a quarter, the company stops and looks at reality on purpose.

Not a review of activity. Not a report on how hard everybody worked. An audit of the machine.

The word I want you to hold on to is **inevitable.**

Companies do not drift because people are lazy. They drift because nothing ever forces the whole system to be looked at at once. Left to themselves, organisations review when something goes wrong — which means they review under stress, which means they review badly, and which means the review is really a search for somebody to blame.

Putting it on the calendar removes all of that. The audit happens whether the quarter was good or bad. It happens whether anybody feels like it. It is not triggered by a problem, which means its arrival is not evidence that there is one.

Introducing that rhythm is one of the best structural decisions I have made in a company, and I would make it again tomorrow. The last chapter described in some detail what I put inside it for fifteen years, and why that part was wrong. The container was never the problem.

Ninety days is the right interval for a reason that is more arithmetic than philosophy. It is roughly the shortest period over which a real business tells you anything — shorter than that and you are reading noise, panicking at weather. And it is roughly the longest you can afford to go without asking, because a machine that has been drifting for six months has usually drifted somewhere expensive.

And there is a second reason for the quarter that I did not appreciate until recently. The previous chapter described a field experiment in which employees moved to a longer evaluation cycle showed measurably higher stress hormones than colleagues on a shorter one — and could not feel the difference. The mechanism was anticipation. A long gap does not spare people the evaluation. It gives them more time to dread it and more material to prepare a defence with.

**Frequent, unremarkable, structurally unthreatening evaluation is not softer than an annual reckoning. It is the thing that stops the dread from accumulating.**

Three practical matters, and they are not administrative details.

**It takes three days.** The first half-day is spent arriving. People are still in last week, still checking messages, still answering the question they were asked in the corridor on the way out. Real analysis does not begin until the room has settled, and a room does not settle in ninety minutes.

**It does not happen in your offices.** Context leaks. In your own building everybody is sitting inside their department, their status, yesterday's meeting and tomorrow's. Somewhere else — we mostly use a rented house or a lodge, out of season, where we can arrange the place the way we want — people become slightly different and considerably more honest. And the telephones stay upstairs, in the bedrooms. Not on the table. Not face down. Upstairs.

**And the right people have to be in the room, which is not the same as the senior people.** The leadership attends, obviously. But the question this meeting exists to answer is answered from data, and the people who hold the data are frequently not on the leadership team.

If the bottleneck turns out to be in lead generation, is the person who actually looks at the lead data every week in that room? Or is the room full of people who have been told about the lead data by somebody who was told about it?

Because if it is the second, then the experiments and the multiplications for the next ninety days will be chosen from memory and feeling — and in that room, seniority is usually just the feeling with the loudest voice.

> **Invite whoever can answer the question with evidence, regardless of their title.**

### 5

Here is the structure of the three days, and it is the whole chapter in one paragraph.

**The audit is four worksheets.**

| Sheet | What it produces |
|---|---|
| **Vision** | the gaps |
| **Audit** | the bottleneck behind each gap, and which road it takes |
| **Experiment** | the plan for each thing we do not know how to do |
| **Multiplication** | the plan for each thing we already know how to do |

Day one is the first two sheets: what the machine produced, and what is blocking it. **What we found out.**

Days two and three are the other two: one sheet for every row that came out of the audit. **What we are going to do about what we found out.**

That is it. There is no fifth sheet and there is no fifth activity. If something is happening in those three days that does not end up on one of those four pieces of paper, it is either a conversation that belongs somewhere else or it is theatre.

Let me take them one at a time.

---

### 6

![Vision worksheet](imagini/vision.pdf)

The first sheet is the vision, and you have met it before — it is the document from the third chapter, the Ordinary Great Quarter, with two more columns added.

Across the top it says: *Vision is an ordinary great quarter — a good quarter that can be repeated, without heroics or last-minute saves.*

Then five columns. **The indicator. What it did last quarter. What it did this quarter. What the vision says it should do. And the gap.**

The indicators are not chosen in the meeting. They come from the vision, which was written long before and which nobody is allowed to edit on a Tuesday because the numbers came in badly.

And then the sheet gets read twice, which is the entire point of it.

**Read it once, comparing this quarter with last quarter.** What improved. What deteriorated. What became unstable. What became normal. This is real information about the machine's direction, and a company that skips it is throwing away a quarter of data.

**Then read it again, comparing this quarter with the vision.** That is a different question altogether. Not *are we better than we were*, but *is this machine producing what we have said it should produce by default.*

The first comparison gives you direction. The second gives you distance. You need both, and they answer different questions.

Three things about this sheet that took me a long time to get right.

**The vision column holds a range, not a point.** Reality has weather in it. Markets move, suppliers fail, people leave, a competitor does something unexpected. A default that cannot survive an ordinary bad month is not a description of a machine — it is a wish with a decimal place. The range is where the honesty lives, and the gap is measured against the edge of it.

**The vision says "without extraordinary intervention," and that phrase is the one everybody deletes first.** If a number only arrives when the founder personally spends nine months on it, the machine does not produce that number. A person does. Which means a quarter that was rescued by somebody's heroic effort has not closed the gap. It has hidden it — and next quarter it will be back, slightly larger, with a slightly more tired person in front of it.

**And there has to be more than one line on the sheet.** The third chapter explained why and it applies with full force here: a single number can always be bought at the expense of something else. Revenue can be purchased with margin. Growth can be purchased with debt. Volume can be purchased by neglecting the quality gate. A sheet with one line on it cannot tell you whether you had a good quarter or an expensive one.

Which is also why a number that is *too good* is information. If an indicator has overshot the vision, something went somewhere. Capacity was borrowed from a budget that belonged to something else. On this sheet that shows up as two rows moving in opposite directions. On a scoreboard it shows up as a hero.

Reading the sheet takes about forty minutes. Most of that time is spent arguing about whether the numbers can be trusted, which is exactly the right thing for the first morning of a quarterly meeting to be about.

At the end of it you have a list of gaps. That is the entire output of the first session — not a decision, not a plan, not a commitment. A list of distances.

### 7

Before the second sheet, there is a rule that governs everything that happens for the rest of the three days, and it is the one I am asked about more than anything else in this book.

> **A gap is not a bad sign about a person. It is information about the machine.**

I need to be exact about why, because the polite version of this — *let's not point fingers* — is not what I mean and is nowhere near strong enough.

When we look at a shortfall and conclude that a particular person is the reason for it, we have said something without noticing that we said it. We have said that the person is more important than the machine.

**We have made them a hero.**

And that is not a compliment the company can afford, because it hands all the power to a human being and takes it away from the thing the company actually owns. If the reason your machine underperforms is a person, then your machine is a person, and you do not have a machine.

The fourth chapter called this the single most expensive misdiagnosis available to a founder — an architecture problem wearing a people problem's clothes. It is expensive precisely because the evidence for the wrong conclusion is standing in front of you every day, visibly not performing.

You saw what it looks like when it is corrected. A country decides that the road is dangerous and stops trying to build better drivers. A car plant in California reopens with the same workforce everybody had called the worst in the industry, and within months is producing cars at near-perfect quality. Nothing about those people changed. The machine they were standing in did.

Does this mean the person is never at fault? No.

The fifth chapter defined a responsibility precisely: an indicator, with a standard, **that is within that person's power.** A person is at fault when they did not deliver something that sat entirely inside that boundary. That happens, and when it happens you deal with it directly.

But it is rarely why a machine underperforms. Far more often the role was wrong, or the responsibilities were never derived, or the recruitment process for that role was wrong and the wrong person is sitting in it — and every one of those is a fact about the machine.

**Say the man is at fault and you have given him all the power. Find that the process is at fault and you have given the power back to the machine, which is the asset you own and the only thing you can actually change.**

### 8

![Quarterly Audit worksheet](imagini/audit.pdf)

Every gap from the first sheet becomes one row on the second.

*For each gap: name the bottleneck, the processes behind it, and mark the solution path.* Four columns — **the gap · the bottleneck that limits it · the processes involved · and two checkboxes, MULTIPLICATION or DISCOVERY.**

Notice first what this sheet does not ask.

It does not ask how we are going to close the gap. There is no field anywhere on it for a commitment, a stretch, or a plan to try harder. It asks one question, which the first chapter promised and which this sheet finally makes operational:

> **What is blocking this machine from producing the output described in the vision?**

And the answer is not allowed to be an opinion. It comes from the indicators of the validated processes inside the machine.

Here is what filling in that third column actually looks like, and it is the arithmetic from the fifth chapter run backwards.

Say the gap is in new clients. The sales process is a chain: companies identified, people reached, qualifying conversations held, proposals sent, contracts signed. Each link has an indicator, and each indicator has a standard that was **derived** — a hundred calls a day was not chosen because it sounded ambitious, it was calculated backwards from the number of contracts the vision requires.

So you walk the chain until you find the link running below its standard. Perhaps qualification is at standard and lead generation is not. Perhaps both are at standard and the mix of channels has moved underneath you. Whatever it is, it has a location, and a location can be worked on.

**If you can fill that column with real, named, validated processes, the conversation is about mechanism, and conversations about mechanism converge.**

If you cannot — if the honest entry is *sales, generally* — then you do not have a machine there. You have activity. And a room full of senior people looking at a gap with no processes behind it will do the only thing available to them, which is argue.

> **A meeting without validated processes is not an audit. It is a debate, and the best talker wins.**

The best talker is almost never the person closest to the data. I have sat in hundreds of those meetings. For fifteen years I ran them.

And an empty Processes column is not a failure of the meeting. It is the single most valuable diagnostic this whole system produces, and it should be written down as a finding in its own right: *we cannot answer this, because there is no validated process here.* That is a real result. It tells you where the machine has a hole in it, and in a company's first year of doing this it is usually the most important thing the audit produces.

### 9

There is something this sheet cannot do, and if you do not solve it separately the whole exercise becomes an expensive way of being wrong on paper.

**It cannot tell you why.**

The first chapter told you about Taiichi Ohno drawing a circle in chalk on a factory floor, putting a young engineer inside it, and coming back hours later to ask what he had seen. The point was never endurance. It was that the first hour of observation shows you what you already believe, because your head has a model and your eyes go looking for it — and that somewhere in the third hour, if you are honest and have nothing else to do, the model breaks and you start seeing what is actually there.

The operator walking four steps for a part that could be at his elbow, a thousand times a shift. The small stack of components that is never zero, which means something upstream produces faster than something downstream consumes. The little jig somebody made out of tape, which is either an improvement nobody captured or a symptom nobody fixed.

None of that is in the data. All of it is in the room where the work happens.

**The indicators tell you where. The chalk circle tells you why.**

And a chalk circle cannot be drawn in a rented house three hours from your offices.

So the field work happens **before** the three days, not during them. In the weeks beforehand, the people who own the processes the indicators have implicated go and stand in front of the actual work. They watch it. They follow one order, or one client, or one shift, all the way through. They talk to the person doing it.

Then they arrive at the meeting having seen something, instead of having read a report about a report.

That changes what the three days are. **The audit is not where the investigation happens. It is where the investigations are put side by side and the company decides what to do about them.**

It also settles the question of who needs to be in the room, and settles it more firmly than any argument about hierarchy. A person who has stood in the circle has something no chart contains. A person two levels above them has a summary of a summary.

### 10

Then the two checkboxes, and there are only two because there are only two moves.

The fourth chapter established this and I will not re-argue it: a company that wants more output can turn some of the unknown into known, or it can make more of the known. Discovery, or multiplication. **There is no third move.** What looks like a third move — pushing harder on the machine you already have — is not growth. It is borrowing, and the first chapter described what the repayment looks like.

**Multiplication** means something already in the machine solves this, and there is not enough of it. The sales conversation that works exists; four people run it; put it in front of more people. The onboarding sequence works in one city; run it in the second.

We take this road wherever it is available, because it is the safest and the most predictable, and because it is where most of the growth actually is.

I want to be honest about why I resisted that for years. Entrepreneurs like me want to reinvent the wheel. It is more interesting. And it is how we keep the hero's role. I am not telling you to give up the hero's role — I am telling you not to spend it where the return is small or negative.

**Discovery** means nothing in the machine solves this. We do not know how. And the honest entry in that box is the sentence most rooms have been avoiding for a decade: *we do not know how.*

That is a legitimate finding, not an evasion. No amount of commitment converts not knowing into knowing.

To route honestly you look at two things: the processes currently in the machine, **and the data from every experiment you have finished so far.** The second source is the one companies forget, and it is frequently where the answer already is. An experiment run eighteen months ago and filed away has often already eliminated three of the five roads the room is about to argue about.

And there is one rule governing the multiplication box that people break constantly, always with good intentions.

> **You may only multiply Level 2 and above.**

If the process that would close the gap has only ever worked once — for one person, in one place, on one occasion — then it is Level 1, and next quarter's job is not multiplication. It is taking that process from Level 1 to Level 2. And that work happens on the discovery branch, beside the machine, not inside it.

This costs something every single time it comes up. There is a thing that worked, there is a gap it appears to fit, and the room wants to roll it out on Monday.

If you want a number for how expensive that instinct is, take it from the most disciplined validation system human beings have ever built. In pharmaceutical development, where the ladder is enforced by law and there are billions of dollars behind getting it right, roughly **seventy percent of drugs that succeed in Phase 2 do not survive Phase 3.** Not because anybody cheated. The Phase 2 result was real. It simply did not generalise.

Seven out of ten things are lost between *it worked* and *it keeps working*, by an industry designed around nothing else.

Now consider what your one good quarter proves.

When the second sheet is full, the company has its quarter. A set of multiplications and a set of experiments. Nothing else was decided, and nothing else needed to be.

And look at the size of that decision. A handful of rows, one checkbox each. **That is the entire strategic content of ninety days of a company's life, and it fits on one side of one sheet of paper.**

### 11

![Experiment worksheet](imagini/experiment.pdf)

Every row marked discovery becomes one of these, and it is filled in on day two or three.

At the top: **the experiment's name, and the bottleneck it solves** — so that no experiment can exist in this company without a stated reason for existing. Then six blocks.

**Process** — where in the machine this happens.

**Hypothesis** — written as *if… then…*

**Source** — where the idea came from. Data. An insight. A client request. Somebody's previous experience. A consultant. A book.

**KPIs** — what we will measure.

**Constraints** — budget, time, and the rules we will not break in order to win.

**Method** — how we will actually run it.

And then the footer, which is the part people expect to be missing and are always surprised to find.

**Owner. Team. Deadline.**

Exactly like an objective. A person's name, a group of people, and a date. Nothing on this sheet is an argument for vagueness, and nothing on it is an argument for taking the pressure off. There is nothing gentle about an experiment with your name on it and a date six weeks away.

**The difference between this sheet and an objective is two fields.**

Because there is a Hypothesis and there are KPIs, what the owner has undertaken to deliver at the deadline is not a result. It is **evidence.**

And evidence can be delivered honestly on a date. A result whose method you do not yet possess cannot be — which is why, when you demand it anyway, you get the fortnight of explanation-assembling that the last chapter described.

The previous chapter also made a claim I want to connect to this sheet, because the sheet is where the claim gets cashed. A failed experiment is only a gain for the company if the failure lands on the idea rather than on the person. **The Hypothesis and KPIs fields are the mechanism that does that.** They are not paperwork. They are the two lines that let somebody stand up in front of the room and say *the hypothesis did not hold* instead of *I did not deliver.*

That is everything I am going to say here about how to build an experiment. **How to design one that cannot lie to you — the falsifiability of the hypothesis, why the measures must be chosen before you know the answer, what the Source field is worth after two years, and how to keep the whole thing from endangering the machine that pays for it — is the next chapter.** This sheet is simply where a discovery row goes.

---

### 12

![Multiplication worksheet](imagini/multiplication.pdf)

And every row marked multiplication becomes one of these.

At the top: **the process to be multiplied**, and a small field that matters more than it looks — **which multiplication this is.** Number one, number two, number five.

Then the sheet asks four things.

**People to recruit** — the roles, and how many of each.

**People to transfer** — from the processes we already run, if any.

**Standards** — the targets on the process indicators after the multiplication, at **three months, six months and twelve months.**

**Owner, budget, and a deadline** — with the deadline defined precisely as *team complete and running.*

Three things about this sheet deserve explanation, because each of them encodes something the earlier chapters proved the hard way.

**Why the number matters.** The fourth chapter defined Level 3 as a process that works through other people. The fifth chapter tightened it: a Level 3 process is one you have multiplied at least once **and for which you now have a validated multiplication process** — meaning that next time, you know exactly what to do.

Which means the first multiplication of anything is half an experiment. You are not only copying the process; you are finding out how to copy it. It will be slower and more expensive than the second one, and that is not a failure, that is what the first one is for. Writing the number on the sheet sets everybody's expectations correctly before the work starts.

**Why there is a row for people to transfer.** This is the field that saves multiplications, and it is where most companies go wrong by omission.

The instinct is to build the new unit from scratch — hire a full team, train them on the documentation, turn it on. It rarely works, because there are always parts of a working process that never made it into the document, no matter how carefully it was written.

So you take a few people out of the existing team and move them into the new one. They already live the standard. They do not have to be taught what good looks like, because they have been producing it. And they carry the undocumented parts with them, in their hands.

The team they came from then recruits and replaces them — **which is itself a test.** If that team can lose two people and return to standard, its processes really are at Level 2, and you have just proved it at no extra cost. If it cannot, you have learned something enormously important before you found it out the expensive way.

The fifth chapter described how Toyota does this: each new plant abroad is assigned an established plant that supports it and exchanges people with it. And it described what General Motors did instead, four hundred miles from a factory it half-owned and could not replicate — it copied the visible artefact and scattered the people who had actually learned the system.

**Send the carriers, not the document.**

**And why the standards have three columns instead of one.** Because a multiplication has a ramp. You do not ask a new unit to produce at full standard in its first month, and pretending otherwise produces exactly the panic and rescue this whole system exists to remove.

What you ask is that the new unit reaches the standard of the unit it was copied from, within a defined period. Three months, six months, twelve. Written down in advance, before anybody is disappointed.

Notice that this sheet has numbers and dates on it, and that this is entirely legitimate. **The method here is known.** That is the whole point of the multiplication branch. The last chapter argued at length that a target set where nobody knows the method is a wish; the corollary is that a target set where the method is validated and documented is simply a specification. This is that.

As with the experiment sheet, this is where a multiplication row goes, not how a multiplication is done. **How you actually transfer a process without damaging the unit you took it from, what makes a unit copyable in the first place, and what to do when the thing that made it work turns out to be invisible — that is the chapter after next.**

### 13

So the three days end, and the audit produces three things.

**A list of multiplications.** Things the machine already knows how to do, which need more of.

**A list of experiments.** Things nobody knows how to do yet, which need to be found out.

And a third one that almost every company forgets, and that the first chapter promised you.

Go back to the drawing one more time and look at the thing in it that surprises everybody. The two branches do not point back at the machine. **They point back at the vision.**

That is deliberate, and it took me a long time to understand it.

When an experiment succeeds, or when a multiplied unit finally holds the standard, you have produced two different things and they are easy to confuse.

The first is a method, or a piece of capacity. That does eventually belong inside the machine, and it gets there exactly the way this book has described throughout — built beside, validated, then merged.

The second is **information about what this business is now capable of.** And that does not belong in the machine at all.

Because you wrote the vision using what you knew at the time, and what you knew at the time is precisely what the quarter has just changed.

You discover a way of acquiring customers at half the cost you had assumed was structural. That does not simply make this quarter better. It means the profit range in your vision was written against a constraint that no longer exists, and leaving it there is now a failure of ambition.

Or you multiply a unit and find that the economics quietly degrade at the fourth location, for reasons nobody could have predicted from the first three. That is not only an operational problem. It means the number of locations in your vision was a guess, and you now have evidence, and the honest thing to do is change the document.

So the last thing that happens in the three days is that somebody asks whether the vision is still true.

This is what stops a vision from becoming a framed piece of paper nobody reads. **It is not revised because somebody got excited in a meeting. It is revised because the company learned something and can point at what it learned.**

> **The machine is updated by validated methods. The vision is updated by validated knowledge.**

And it is also how you know when you have arrived. The third chapter gave the test and it is deliberately unglamorous: you have reached your vision when the machine produces the vision's result repeatably and predictably, without extraordinary intervention. Not when you hit the number. **When hitting the number has stopped being interesting.**

At which point every gap on the first sheet is zero, and the honest thing to do is raise the vision and start again.

---

### 14

Four sheets of paper. Let me put them back together, because the sequence is the point.

**The vision sheet** compares what the machine produced against what it is supposed to produce, and yields a list of gaps. It has no opinions in it.

**The audit sheet** takes each gap, finds the process behind it, names the bottleneck, and routes it down one of two roads. It also, when it cannot be filled in, tells you where your machine is not yet a machine.

**The experiment sheet** takes every discovery row and turns it into evidence with an owner, a team and a date.

**The multiplication sheet** takes every multiplication row and turns it into a plan for producing more of something that already works, with the people, the ramp and the date written down.

And the whole thing runs on one rule, which the first chapter stated and which every chapter since has been paying for:

> **Build beside the machine. Validate. Then merge.**

Discovery happens beside the operation, at a scale where being wrong is survivable. Multiplication is built beside it too — extracted, recruited, inducted, trained and tested separately, merged only when it can hold the standard without degrading the original.

Neither branch is allowed to touch the machine on the strength of enthusiasm. **Protect the machine**, because the machine is what pays for the laboratory, and a company that damages its operation in the name of growth has damaged the thing that finances growth.

Which brings me back to where this chapter started, and to the difference between the room I ran for fifteen years and the room I run now.

It is not the length. It is not the location. It is not the telephones, though I would still fight anyone over the telephones.

It is the first question of the first morning.

In the old room, the first question was, in effect: **who missed their number?** The answer to that question is always a person.

In this one, the first question is: **what is blocking the machine?** The answer to that question is always a mechanism.

Everything people describe as culture — whether anybody brings bad news early, whether anybody proposes something that might not work, whether the quiet person with the actual data says anything at all — follows from which of those two questions the room is built around. You do not fix it with values on a wall. You fix it by changing what the first question is.

And there is one consequence I did not see coming.

The fifth chapter described what Kahneman and Klein established about skilled intuition: it requires an environment regular enough to be predictable, plus prolonged practice with feedback. A company that reinvents its rules every ninety days is a low-validity environment, and in a low-validity environment nobody can ever become good at anything.

The audit does the opposite. **The vision holds. The machine holds. The processes hold.** What changes from quarter to quarter is which experiments are running beside the machine, in a bounded space built for exactly that.

Stability everywhere, variation in one controlled place.

That is not only how you protect the machine. It is the only arrangement in which the people inside it can get good at their work.

---

So the audit ends, and it leaves two lists on the table.

One of them says multiply. The company will go and do more of what it already knows how to do, and most of its growth will come from that list, and almost nobody will find it exciting.

The other says find out.

*That is the harder branch, and it is the one nobody is taught: what to do when the answer is not in the machine, and somebody has to go and get it.*
