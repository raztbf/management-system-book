### 1

In 2013, a group of engineers at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center went looking for an engine.

The engine was the F-1, the enormous first-stage motor, five of which lifted every Saturn V off the pad. They found one sitting in storage. They found another in the Smithsonian, on display, with members of the public walking past it on their way to the gift shop. The engineers took a gas generator out of the first and a gas generator out of the second, pulled the components apart on a bench, and ran a structured-light 3D scanner over the pieces to produce drawings.

That is how the most advanced engineering organization in the history of our species got back to its own design. It went to a museum and measured it.

To understand how strange that is, you have to hold the original scale in your head, and the original scale is almost impossible to hold. At its peak, the Apollo program employed something like four hundred and ten thousand people, spread across roughly twenty thousand companies and universities. In 1966 — three years before anybody landed — NASA was consuming 4.4 percent of the entire federal budget of the United States. Not of the science budget. Of everything the country spent. The total bill came to around $25.8 billion in the money of the day, which is somewhere north of $300 billion now.

An effort of that size does not simply evaporate. Something has to remove it. And something did: Apollo 18, 19 and 20 were cancelled, the Saturn V production line was shut down, and the tooling was scrapped. The four hundred thousand went off into other industries and eventually into retirement, and what they knew went with them.

The usual way to tell this part is as a story about budgets and politics. Congress lost interest. Vietnam. The public got bored of moonwalks and went back to watching television. All of that is true, and all of it is beside the point, because it lets you believe the ending was imposed from outside by people with no imagination.

It was not. The ending was in the sentence at the beginning.

On 25 May 1961, John F. Kennedy stood in front of a joint session of Congress and said that the United States should commit itself, before the decade was out, to landing a man on the moon and returning him safely to the earth. It is probably the most famous vision statement ever delivered, and it has everything the textbooks say a vision should have. A specific outcome. A hard deadline. Enormous emotional force. A scale that made grown engineers weep.

It also worked, which is the part that makes it dangerous. Eight years and two months later, Neil Armstrong stepped off a ladder onto the surface of another world, and roughly a fifth of every human being alive watched him do it.

But read that sentence again as what it actually was, which is an engineering specification. It does not ask for the ability to go to the moon. It asks for a man on the moon, and back, by a date. Everything downstream obeyed it perfectly. Cost, sustainability, the health of the people, the future of the organization — all of it was subordinated to one result on one calendar. The program was not badly built. It was built exactly to spec, and the spec had a finish line in it.

Six landings. Twelve men on the surface, between July 1969 and December 1972.

And then nobody, for over half a century.

### 2

I love this story and I do not want to be cheap with it, so let me be precise about what I am not saying. Apollo is one of the most extraordinary things human beings have ever done. The people who did it were magnificent, and the ones still alive have earned every gram of the pride they carry. Nothing here is an argument that it should not have been attempted.

The effort was also real in a way that is easy to say and hard to feel. Four hundred and ten thousand people gave the best years of their working lives to it. And on 27 January 1967, during a routine test on the ground, a fire tore through the sealed cabin of Apollo 1 and killed Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee in under half a minute. The program absorbed the deaths, rebuilt the capsule, and made the deadline.

The interesting question about a price like that is not whether it was heavy. It is what it bought.

So here is the whole inheritance, the entire physical yield of four hundred and ten thousand people and three hundred billion dollars and three men dead on a launch pad in Florida.

Three hundred and eighty-two kilograms.

That is the mass of rock, soil and dust the six landings brought home from six sites — around two thousand two hundred separate samples. Fifty years later, that material, plus a handful of robotic missions, is essentially the entire physical ground truth humanity possesses about another world. Everything we believe about the age of the solar system, about how planets form, about what happened to the early Earth, is being argued over on the basis of 382 kilograms collected during six visits by twelve men across three and a half years.

And it stopped, not because we had learned what there was to learn, but because the thing we had built was not capable of continuing.

The people who paid did not get anything durable back either. They were handed a moment they will never stop being proud of, and they are right to be proud of it. They were not handed an industry. There was no second act for four hundred thousand people, because there was nothing left running for them to work on.

Meanwhile, NASA's own Office of Inspector General puts the cost of the Artemis campaign — the program to go back — at around $93 billion between 2012 and 2025, with a projected cost of roughly $4.1 billion for every launch of the rocket that will carry the crew. We are spending tens of billions of dollars to reacquire a capability we already had and threw away, and the men who built the first one are being buried while we do it.

Now run the counterfactual, because it is the whole point of this chapter.

Suppose that in 1961 the sentence had been different. Suppose it had been: *build a machine that can put people on the moon and bring them home, four times a year, indefinitely.*

That is a far worse speech. It has no finish line. It cannot be photographed. It would not have beaten the Soviet Union to anything in particular, which means the political appetite that paid for Apollo might never have appeared and the whole thing might have died in a committee room. I am not pretending the choice was obvious or free.

But follow it anyway, because it forces a completely different set of questions in the first week. Not *how do we get there once*, but *what does it cost to move a kilogram, and how do we make that number fall every year.* Not *how do we survive three days on the surface*, but *how do people live there.* Not *how do we build a rocket*, but *how do we build a production line for rockets.* It would have been slower. The first landing might have come in 1975 instead of 1969. There would have been far less for anybody's ego to feed on, national or personal, and no single morning when a fifth of the planet stopped to watch.

And by 1990 there would have been a permanent presence on the moon. By now there would be a base, decades of continuous data, a generation of human beings who grew up with people living off Earth as an ordinary fact of life, and direct answers to most of the questions we are currently spending $93 billion to go and ask again.

We chose a moment. We got the moment, gloriously. We have been paying for it ever since.

Which brings me to the sentence this entire book is built on. I want to put it down before I have earned it, so you can hold it while I do.

> **The things that change the world do not happen once.**

Karl Benz built a working automobile in 1886, and for roughly thirty years it changed almost nothing for almost anybody. The car was a possession of people who were already rich. The life of an ordinary person was untouched by it. The invention existed, was demonstrated, was admired, and did not matter.

What mattered was the moving assembly line — the machine that made cars ordinary. It cut the time to assemble one from around twelve hours to roughly ninety minutes and pushed the price down until the man building the car could afford to own the car. Nobody built a single automobile, confirmed that it worked, and went to bed satisfied that humanity had been improved. The improvement was the repetition. It always is.

### 3

For a while, the most-watched videos in aerospace were of rockets falling over.

They were genuinely funny. A first stage would come screaming back out of the sky, aim itself at a barge in the Atlantic, touch down, wobble, and go over sideways in a fireball. Sometimes it missed the deck. Sometimes it arrived intact and then tipped like a felled tree. People in the industry enjoyed this a great deal, and you can see why, because the thing being attempted did not look like a victory. It looked like an accounting exercise with fireworks.

SpaceX had not set out to reach orbit. Reaching orbit was a solved problem by the 2000s; it had been done thousands of times and nobody was going to cry about it. What SpaceX set out to do was make reaching orbit repeatable and cheap, which in practice meant the deeply unromantic business of not throwing the rocket away.

You cannot make a Kennedy speech out of amortization. Nobody weeps at a reusable first stage.

In December 2015, a Falcon 9 first stage came back and stood up. Then it happened again. Then it became routine, then boring, then invisible, which is what success actually looks like when the thing you are building is a cause rather than a moment.

In 2020, SpaceX flew 25 orbital launches. In 2021, 31. In 2022, 61. In 2023, 96. In 2024, 134 — more than half of every orbital launch on the planet, by anybody, government or private. In 2025, 165. One booster has flown more than twenty times.

Now I have to say something I would rather not have to say.

I am not an admirer of Elon Musk. A good deal of how he operates, and a good deal of what he says, sits somewhere I am not willing to stand. But this book is not about character, and I will not pretend that a man whose values I do not share failed to build the more useful thing. What SpaceX built is more correct, and more genuinely in the service of humanity, than a single victory constructed to feed an ego — and that is true whether the ego belongs to one man or to a superpower. Apollo was, among other things, a demonstration aimed at the Soviet Union. It was magnificent and it was also that.

You are allowed to hold both of those judgements at once, and I would go further: you have to. Letting your opinion of a person decide what you are permitted to learn is a very expensive way to protect your feelings.

Ask yourself which of those two organizations is now more capable of putting a permanent base on the moon. It is not close, and it is not a compliment. It is arithmetic.

The difference is not talent, and it is not effort. Apollo had more of both, by an enormous margin, and it had better people in worse conditions doing harder things with slide rules. It is not even money. The difference is that Apollo owned a magnificent result and SpaceX owns a boring cause.

> **A result is what happened. A cause is what makes it happen again.**

### 4

I am opening a business book with a rocket because almost every company I have worked with in twenty years is built on the Apollo model, and almost none of them know it.

The pattern is exact. Set a result. Attach a date. Subordinate everything to it. Find the people who will not stop, and let them not stop. Absorb the damage — the exhaustion, the departures, the shortcuts, the maintenance nobody had time for. Cross the line. Get photographed.

And then discover, two quarters later, that you cannot do it again, because what you built was a campaign and not a capability.

The record quarter that nobody in the company can explain. The launch that worked because four people did not go home for eleven weeks. The client saved by the founder personally, at midnight, for the ninth time. The best year in the company's history, followed by a year that looks like a collapse and gets blamed on the market.

Every one of those is Apollo at a smaller scale, and every one of them gets celebrated on the way past. Founders are usually proud of precisely the wrong part. They will describe the crossing to you in extraordinary detail. They can tell you nothing about the boat.

And there is a second problem, which is the one that eventually does the killing. Apollo had the good sense to end. Your company is expected to do it again next quarter, and the quarter after that, forever — which means somebody inside it has to keep choosing the heroic version on purpose, year after year, and the reasons they choose it are far more interesting than they look.

Underneath all of it is a simple mechanical fact: results are visible and causes are not. Revenue is a number you can say out loud. Profit is a number the bank cares about. New customers, new locations, market share, the size of the contract signed on Thursday — all countable, all announceable, all available to be felt. So the company looks at the number and decides the number should be larger. A target is set, pressure is applied, a discount is introduced, more calls are demanded, people are hired quickly, hours get longer, decisions drift toward the founder, and whatever is lying around gets thrown at it.

And sometimes the number moves. That is the entire problem. If it never moved, nobody would do this twice.

But look at what moved underneath. A discount raises revenue and destroys margin. Contracts pulled forward make this quarter stronger and next quarter emptier. Three weeks of overtime protect the delivery and consume the energy that was going to produce next month's. Training postponed protects today's capacity by reducing tomorrow's. A founder who personally rescues the account saves it, and leaves the company slightly more dependent on the founder than it was on Monday.

Which means we have to be much more careful with the word *better*, because almost everybody uses it to mean *bigger, this quarter*, and that is not what it means.

> **Better is not a bigger result. It is owning the cause that produces it again.**

Read that against the summer of 1969. Measured on output, NASA had the greatest quarter any organization has ever had; nothing in recorded history competes. And on the morning Armstrong came down the ladder, NASA was not a better organization than it had been in 1968, because the capacity to do it again was not being built, and within three years it would be gone. The output was extraordinary. The underlying capability was already being spent to produce it.

> **A company can have its best quarter and become a worse business.**

That is not a paradox and it is not a warning against ambition. It is the ordinary condition of most growing companies, and it is invisible from the inside precisely because the one instrument everybody is watching moved the right way.

I want to refuse a version of this argument before it gets attached to me. It is the version where owning the cause is the sober, modest, grown-up choice, and the enormous numbers belong to the people willing to burn the house down. That is backwards, and Apollo and SpaceX are the proof. Apollo's greatest year was 1969 and its output for the following fifty was zero. SpaceX's unglamorous accounting exercise now flies more than half of everything that leaves this planet, and every year is larger than the year before it. Over any horizon longer than a single quarter, the machine is not the smaller number. It is the only route to the larger one, because it compounds, and because it is the only arrangement that lets your most intelligent people spend their time on what nobody has done yet instead of repairing what was supposed to work already.

So the question is not how to want less. It is what would have to exist inside this company for the result you are proudest of to become the result you produce in an ordinary quarter, without anybody being a hero about it — and what you would then do with all the intelligence you got back.

That question has an answer, and the answer has a shape. It is the same shape whether you build rockets, sell software, run four restaurants, or are trying to build a marriage that does not need rescuing every eighteen months. It is not a list of principles, and it is not a philosophy. It is a loop, with a rule at every point on it, and the rules matter more than the parts.

Here it is.
