### 1

On 25 May 1961, John F. Kennedy stood in front of a joint session of Congress and said that the United States should commit itself, before the decade was out, to landing a man on the moon and returning him safely to the earth.

It is probably the most famous vision statement ever delivered, and it has everything the textbooks say a vision should have. A specific outcome. A hard deadline. Enormous emotional force. A scale that made grown engineers cry.

It also worked. Eight years and two months later, Neil Armstrong stepped off a ladder onto the surface of another world, and roughly a fifth of everyone alive watched him do it.

Then look at what stood behind that ladder. At its peak, Apollo employed something like four hundred and ten thousand people across twenty thousand companies and universities. In 1966, three years before anyone landed, NASA was consuming 4.4 percent of the entire federal budget of the United States. The total bill came to around $25.8 billion in the money of the day, north of $300 billion in ours.

None of that was misspent, because Apollo did exactly what it was built to do. It was organized around a single result on a fixed date, and everything else — cost, sustainability, the people, the future of the organization itself — was subordinated to that result. That is not a criticism. It is a description, and it is the reason it succeeded. Twelve human beings walked on the moon between July 1969 and December 1972.

And then nobody did, for over half a century.

### 2

The usual way to tell that second half is as a story about budgets and politics. Congress lost interest. Vietnam. The public got bored.

All true, and all beside the point, because it lets you believe the ending was imposed from outside. It was not. The ending was in the design from the first day.

Apollo 18, 19 and 20 were cancelled. The Saturn V production line was shut down and the tooling was scrapped. The four hundred thousand people dispersed into other industries and eventually into retirement, and the knowledge went with them, because the knowledge had never been stored in an organization. It had been stored in a *programme*, and programmes end.

Then there is the detail that is almost unbearable.

In 2013, engineers at NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center wanted to understand the F-1, the first-stage engine, five of which lifted every Saturn V. So they took a gas generator out of an F-1 that had been sitting in storage, and another out of an F-1 in the Smithsonian, pulled them apart, and ran a structured-light 3D scan over the components to produce drawings.

That is how the most advanced engineering organization on earth got back to its own design. It went to a museum and measured it.

Read that as a story about NASA's filing system and you have missed it, because the bill was not sent to NASA. It was sent to everyone.

Six landings brought back 382 kilograms of rock, soil and dust from six sites, around 2,200 separate samples. Fifty years later, that material plus a handful of robotic missions is essentially the entire physical ground truth our species possesses about another world. Everything we believe about the age of the solar system, about how planets form, about what happened to the early Earth, rests on those six visits by twelve men in three and a half years. It stopped, not because we had learned what there was to learn, but because the thing we had built was not capable of continuing.

NASA's own Inspector General puts the cost of Artemis, the campaign to go back, at around $93 billion between 2012 and 2025, with roughly $4.1 billion for each launch of the rocket that will carry the crew. We are spending tens of billions to reacquire a capability we already had and threw away.

Now put the other side of the ledger next to it, because this is the part that gets sentimentalized and should not be.

Apollo ran on six-day weeks that became seven-day weeks, on people who gave it the best decade of their working lives, on a workforce that understood it was racing a deadline set by a dead president and a rival superpower. On 27 January 1967, during a routine ground test, a fire tore through the sealed cabin of Apollo 1 and killed Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee in under half a minute. The programme absorbed the deaths, rebuilt the capsule, and made the date.

The point of any of that is not that it was hard. Hard is the ordinary condition of serious work, and nobody involved was asking for sympathy. The point is the exchange. Four hundred thousand people, and three lives, and a decade, and $300 billion of today's money, bought one result six times over — and then the vehicle that result depended on had its tooling cut up for scrap, and its drawings had to be recovered from an exhibit. The species got a photograph. The people who made it possible got a programme that ended and a capability nobody inherited. That is what all of that effort was traded for, and the trade was decided in 1961, in a sentence, before a single bolt was machined.

This is a story I love, and I am not going to be cheap with it. Apollo is one of the most extraordinary things human beings have ever done, and the ones still alive have earned every bit of the pride they carry. That is precisely why the next question is worth asking seriously.

Suppose that in 1961 the sentence had been different. Suppose it had been: *build a machine that can put people on the moon and bring them home, four times a year, indefinitely.*

That is a far worse speech. It has no finish line. It cannot be photographed. It would not have beaten the Soviet Union to anything, which means the political appetite that paid for Apollo might never have appeared and the whole thing might have died in committee. The choice was neither obvious nor free.

But follow it anyway, because it forces different questions from the first week. Not how do we get there once, but what does transport cost per kilogram and how do we bring it down. Not how do we survive three days on the surface, but how do people live there. Not how do we build a rocket, but how do we build a production line for rockets. It would have been slower. The first landing might have been 1975 instead of 1969, and there would have been far less for anybody's ego to feed on, national or personal.

And by 1990 there would have been a permanent presence on the moon. By now, a base. Decades of continuous data. A generation that grew up with people living off Earth as an ordinary fact. Direct answers to the questions we are currently spending $93 billion to ask again, and a cost per kilogram to orbit that had been falling for fifty years instead of standing still for forty.

We chose a moment. We got the moment, gloriously. We have been paying for that choice ever since.

Which brings me to the sentence this entire book is built on. I want to state it before I have earned it, so you can hold it while I do.

> **The things that change the world do not happen once.**

Karl Benz built a working automobile in 1886, and for roughly thirty years it changed almost nothing for almost anybody. The car was a possession of people who already had everything. The life of an ordinary person was untouched by it.

What changed the world was the moving assembly line, which took the time to assemble a car from around twelve hours to roughly ninety minutes and drove the price down until the worker building the thing could afford to own one. Nobody built a single car, confirmed that it worked, and went to sleep satisfied that humanity had been improved. The improvement *was* the repetition. It always is.

### 3

I am opening a business book with a rocket because almost every company I have worked with in twenty years is built on the Apollo model, and almost none of them know it.

The pattern is exact. Set a result. Attach a date. Subordinate everything to it. Find the people who will not stop, and let them not stop. Absorb the damage: the exhaustion, the departures, the shortcuts, the maintenance nobody had time for. Cross the line. Get photographed. Then discover, six months later, that you cannot do it again, because what you built was a campaign and not a capability.

The record quarter that nobody can explain. The launch that worked because four people did not go home for eleven weeks. The client saved by the founder personally, at midnight, for the ninth time. The best year in the company's history, followed by a year that looks like a collapse and gets blamed on the market. Every one of those is Apollo at a smaller scale: a magnificent result, produced by heroic effort, whose cause the organization does not own and therefore cannot repeat without paying the same price again, if it can repeat it at all.

Founders are usually proud of exactly the wrong part. They will tell you about the crossing in enormous detail. They cannot tell you anything about the boat.

And the small version carries a problem the large one did not. Apollo had the good sense to end. Your company is expected to do it again next quarter, and the quarter after that, forever, with the same people and the same boat.

So look at what the other choice produces.

SpaceX did not set out to reach orbit. Reaching orbit had been done thousands of times; by the 2000s it was a solved problem. What SpaceX set out to do was make reaching orbit repeatable and cheap, which in practice meant not throwing the rocket away.

That goal is, emotionally, almost worthless. Nobody weeps at a reusable first stage. You cannot make a Kennedy speech out of amortization. And for years it looked ridiculous: boosters tipping over on barges, exploding on landing, missing the deck entirely. The failures were mocked, because the thing being attempted did not look like a victory. It looked like an accounting exercise with fireworks.

In December 2015, a Falcon 9 first stage landed upright. Then it happened again. Then it became routine, then boring, then invisible. In 2020, SpaceX flew 25 orbital launches. In 2021, 31. In 2022, 61. In 2023, 96. In 2024, 134, which was more than half of all the orbital launches on the planet by anybody. In 2025, 165. One booster has flown more than twenty times.

Now the thing I would rather not have to say.

I am not an admirer of Elon Musk. A good deal of how he operates, and a good deal of what he says, sits somewhere I am not willing to stand. But this book is not about character, and I am not going to pretend that a man whose values I do not share failed to build the more useful thing. What SpaceX built is more correct, and more genuinely in the service of humanity, than a single victory constructed to feed an ego — and that is true whether the ego belongs to one man or to a superpower. Apollo was, among other things, a demonstration aimed at the Soviet Union. It was magnificent and it was also that. You are allowed to hold both judgements at once, and I would go further: you have to, because letting your opinion of a person decide what you are permitted to learn is a very expensive way to protect your feelings.

Ask which of those two organizations is now more capable of putting a permanent base on the moon. It is not close, and it is not a compliment. It is arithmetic.

The difference is not talent, and it is not effort. Apollo had more of both, by a wide margin. It is not even money. It is that Apollo owned a magnificent *result* and SpaceX owns a boring *cause*.

A result is what happened. A cause is what makes it happen again.

Almost every company manages results, and it is nearly impossible not to, because results are visible and countable and emotionally available. Revenue is a number you can say out loud. Profit is a number the bank cares about. New customers, new locations, market share, the size of the contract signed on Thursday: all visible, all measurable, all available to be celebrated on the Friday. So the company looks at the result and decides it should be larger. A target is set, a deadline is attached, pressure is applied. A campaign is launched, a discount is introduced, more calls are demanded, people are hired quickly, hours get longer, decisions move toward the founder, and whatever is available is thrown at the number.

And sometimes the number moves. That is the whole problem. If it never moved, nobody would do it.

Look at what happened underneath while it moved. A discount can raise revenue and destroy margin. Contracts pulled forward from next quarter make this quarter stronger and next quarter weaker. Three weeks of overtime protect the delivery and consume the energy that would have produced next month's. Training postponed protects today's capacity by reducing tomorrow's. A founder who personally rescues the client saves the account and makes the company slightly more dependent on the founder than it was on Monday.

Which means the word *better* is doing damage, because almost everybody uses it to mean *bigger this quarter*, and that is not what it means.

> **Better means it repeats, because you own the cause.**

Read that against Apollo. In the summer of 1969, NASA had the greatest quarter any organization has ever had. Measured on output, nothing in human history compares. And it was not, on that morning, a better organization than it had been in 1968, because the capacity to do it again was not being built, and within three years it would be gone. The output was extraordinary. The underlying capability was already being spent.

Which gives us the sentence I need you to carry through the rest of this book.

> **A company can have its best quarter and become a worse business.**

That is not a paradox and it is not a warning against ambition. It is the ordinary condition of most growing companies, and it is invisible from the inside precisely because the one instrument everybody is watching moved the right way.

And it is not an argument for smaller numbers. It is the opposite, and I want to be exact about why. A campaign buys one large number and then charges you for it, in people who will not do that again, in maintenance deferred, in a founder who is now load-bearing. A machine produces a smaller number in its first cycle and a larger one in every cycle after, because what it produced last quarter is still standing next quarter, holding the floor up while you build on top of it. And it does something a campaign structurally cannot: it takes your most intelligent people off the work of repairing the present. In a company where the ordinary result happens by itself, they are free to spend their time on what nobody has solved yet, which is the only place very large numbers have ever come from.

Over any horizon longer than a single quarter, the machine is not the modest route to a big number. It is the fast one.

So the question is not how to get a bigger number this quarter. It is:

> **What would have to exist inside this company for the result we are proud of to become the result we produce normally?**

That question has an answer, and the answer has a shape. It is the same shape whether you are building rockets, selling software, running four restaurants, or trying to build a marriage that does not need rescuing every eighteen months. It runs continuously, it has a place for everything a company does, including the part where you do not yet know what works, and it will eventually explain why the person most likely to break it is the one the company admires most.

Here it is.
