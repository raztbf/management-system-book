# 6. The Crisis and the Puzzle

### A Plane Designed to Crash, Three Thousand Companies, and the Two States of a Hard Problem

---

### 1

In 1959, a British industrialist named Henry Kremer put up a prize of five thousand pounds for something aviation had been failing at since aviation began: a heavier-than-air machine, powered by nothing but the muscles of the person flying it, completing a figure of eight around two markers half a mile apart, crossing the start and the finish at least ten feet off the ground.

It sounds modest. It was, at the time, close to impossible — the aeronautical establishment knew perfectly well that a fit human being produces about a third of one horsepower, which is roughly nothing. Kremer, to give the impossible a fair chance, attached one condition: designer, pilot, place of construction and place of flight all had to be British.

Britain took the challenge seriously, and Britain sent its best.

In November 1961, a machine built over two years by students at Southampton University made the first officially authenticated take-off under human power in history. It could fly. It could not really turn. That same month a more formidable machine appeared: the Puffin, designed by John Wimpenny, an aerodynamicist at the de Havilland Aircraft Company, and built by a club of de Havilland's own engineers — people who built professional aircraft for a living, volunteering their evenings to build a perfect one. And the Puffin was perfect, in the way a competition glider is perfect: an eighty-five-foot wing, exquisitely crafted, gorgeously finished. In May 1962, Wimpenny himself pedalled it 910 metres down a runway — a world record.

That record stood for ten years.

Read that again, because it is the whole story of the first act. The finest aeronautical engineers of a nation that built world-class aeroplanes attacked a half-mile figure of eight — and the distance record moved not at all for a decade. When the Puffin was damaged, the club built Puffin II around its salvaged parts; that took until 1965. When Puffin II was damaged in its turn, the airframe was donated to a university, the way you donate the belongings of someone who has died. In 1972 a machine called Jupiter stretched the record to 1,239 metres. In 1976 a Japanese team reached two kilometres — in a dead straight line.

Nobody had flown the figure of eight. Nobody had come close. In 1973, Kremer multiplied the prize tenfold, to fifty thousand pounds, and opened it to the world. The money sat there.

And notice the shape of the trap, because it is not an engineering trap. Each of these aircraft took years to design and build, and each one was beautiful, because serious people had poured themselves into it. Which meant that each one was an answer — a finished, polished, once-in-a-programme answer — and every flight was a test of the answer, with years of work standing on the outcome. The one question that mattered — how does a machine this light and this slow *turn* — could only be explored by crashing, and a crash meant months. Sometimes it meant the end of the programme.

**Eighteen years, the best engineers alive, and a prize that grew tenfold — and the problem did not move. Then it was solved in about a year, by a man who was not trying to build a better aeroplane.**

---

### 2

In the summer of 1976, an American aeronautical engineer named Paul MacCready drove his family across the country on vacation, one hundred thousand dollars in debt.

The debt matters, so start there. Six years earlier he had guaranteed a bank loan for a relative's boat-building business. The business failed, and the guarantee landed on him. MacCready was a former world gliding champion with a doctorate from Caltech and no hundred thousand dollars. Somewhere on that drive, he did the currency conversion in his head and noticed that the unclaimed Kremer Prize was almost exactly the size of his debt.

So he was not a hobbyist wandering into the problem. He was a man in a genuine, personal, financial crisis — which is what makes what he did next remarkable, because the crisis is precisely what he refused to work inside.

On the same vacation, he found himself watching hawks and turkey vultures circling in thermals, and doing what an aerodynamicist does on holiday: timing their turns with his wristwatch, estimating their bank angles, working out speeds and turning radii on scrap paper. The arithmetic said something interesting. Make the wings enormous and the speed absurdly low, and the power required to stay airborne drops toward the third of a horsepower a cyclist can actually produce.

But his real insight was not aerodynamic, and he said as much for the rest of his life. Everyone knew big wings and low speed were the direction. His insight was about why eighteen years of superb engineers had failed anyway: **their aircraft could not afford to crash.** The learning the problem demanded — hundreds of small, stupid, informative failures — cost more than any of those beautiful machines could pay. The aircraft that would win the Kremer Prize would not be the best aeroplane. It would be the one that could be wrong the most times per week.

So the Gossamer Condor was, by every standard of the industry, a disgrace. Aluminium tubes. Mylar film — the stuff of party balloons. Piano wire, cardboard, balsa wood and tape. Ninety-six feet of wingspan and only seventy pounds of weight, assembled not like an aircraft but like a question.

And then they flew it, and crashed it, and flew it again. Minor crashes were constant; when a spar snapped mid-flight, the pilot simply put his feet down — the whole machine flew at the speed of a jog, ten feet up. Repairs were made with Scotch tape. At one point the tail surface was adjusted by taping a piece of card to it. A crash in the morning was flying again by the afternoon; a bad one cost a day. Whatever broke was not restored — it was rebuilt better, and the aircraft mutated week by week into something no committee would ever have drawn. Sixteen days before the end, the Condor suffered its biggest crash — and came back from it redesigned, six pounds lighter.

On the 23rd of August 1977, at a small airfield in Shafter, California, a young amateur cyclist and hang-glider pilot named Bryan Allen pedalled the Condor off the ground, banked it around one pylon, banked it back around the other, cleared the ten-foot bar, and ended eighteen years of failure in about seven minutes. Two years later the same team's next aircraft, the Gossamer Albatross, crossed the English Channel under Allen's legs and took the second, larger Kremer Prize. The debt was paid. The Condor hangs today in the Smithsonian, tape and all, among machines of exquisite craftsmanship that never flew the course.

Here is what I want you to take from Shafter, because it is the hinge of this entire chapter.

The British teams and the California team were solving the same problem, under the same physics, with the same prize on the table. The knowledge gap was not the difference — nothing in the Condor was beyond 1961 technology. The difference was what a crash *meant*. For the teams with the beautiful aircraft, a crash was a verdict on years of work, and so every flight was faced the way you face a sentencing. For the team with the tape, a crash was the afternoon's information, and so every flight was a question you were curious about.

**Same problem. Two meanings of failure. And only one of them ever produced anything.**

---

### 3

Let me name the mechanism, because once it is named you will see it everywhere, and then I will tell you where I finally saw it in my own life.

Put a required result over a problem — a number, a deadline, a name attached — and you have converted every attempt at that problem into a verdict. Something is now won or lost each time reality reports back. Run the same attempts as experiments — a question asked, a measurement taken — and each attempt is converted into information instead. Nothing is won or lost; something is *learned*, in either direction.

And those two framings do not produce two moods. They produce two different problems.

**A problem under a verdict is a crisis. A problem under a question is a puzzle.**

This chapter sits at a hinge in the book, and the mechanism is why. The fourth and fifth chapters built the machine: the validated, repeatable part of the business. The next chapter opens the two stations we have named since the first drawing and never entered — the **output**, where reality reports back, and the **audit**, the room where the company decides what to do about it. That chapter is the practical heart of this book: a rhythm, a place, four sheets of paper.

But there is something you will want to carry into that room, because everything you have ever been taught says it belongs there. When a company sees the distance between what it produces and what it wants, the reflex of our entire management culture is to convert that distance into an objective — a number, a deadline, a name — and push. Do that, and the audit I am going to describe will not work; you will get back, in every detail, the room I spent fifteen years running, a room that converted every hard problem it touched into a crisis. So before I hand you the room, I have to show you why the instrument everybody expects on the table is missing from it — why, in this system, objectives are the exception rather than the engine.

This is not a small adjustment. Objectives are the water our world swims in. We set them for our companies, our departments, our bodies, our marriages, our children. A method of planning that mostly declines to use them sounds, at first hearing, like planning that has given up on ambition. It is the opposite.

And I have to be honest about where I learned all of this, because it was not from aeroplanes, and it was not from data — not at first. It was from six months in which, for the first time in my adult life, I watched myself.

---

### 4

About seven years ago I stepped away from work for half a year, and I sank into my hobbies. No plan, no programme. Just the things I loved doing, done for as long as I felt like doing them.

Somewhere in those months, watching myself, I noticed something I had managed not to notice for forty years.

All my life, I had treated every problem in my work and my life as a **crisis.** But when a problem appeared inside one of my passions — a technique that would not come, a build that would not work, a thing I could not yet do — it was never a crisis. It was a **puzzle.**

Those are not two words for the same experience. They are two different physiological states.

**A crisis demands anxiety. A puzzle demands curiosity.**

And the two states behave completely differently over time. In curiosity, I can stay with a problem for as long as the problem takes — months, years — without boredom and without burning out. In anxiety, I am always looking for an exit. Any exit. Sometimes the exit is the solution. Just as often it is another problem, loud enough to take my attention away from this one.

That difference would not matter much if hard problems were quick. They are not. Some problems, however much time and money you throw at them, simply need time — and often a great deal of it. It may take you three or four years to solve the puzzle of holding a healthy weight. Four or five to solve the puzzle of a happy marriage. Seven to find the business model that can turn your vision into reality.

Now do the arithmetic on the other state. You can hold anxiety for three months. Six, perhaps. If you are masochistic enough, and have trained yourself to absorb a great deal of pain, maybe a year. And then the mind and the body quit.

And here is the part that took me longest to see, because the quitting does not look like quitting. The mind does not abandon the problem. **It simplifies the problem into one it can escape from.** The complex, slow puzzle of a marriage becomes: *I do not have the right partner.* Three to six months into a strict diet, the mind rules that the problem is genetic — heavy bones, a slow metabolism, a body that was always going to be this way. We arrive at these answers not from illumination but from a need to stop hurting. To get out of the crisis.

Now try the other perspective on the same problem, because I have, and the difference is not subtle. A marriage is a puzzle: there is a set of pieces that create a relationship that generates love, safety and calm, and the work is to discover them. Some pieces are already in place. Some are missing entirely. Some you hold wrong ideas about. Each piece you find and fit brings a small, real sense of progress — and the more of the picture appears, the more curious you become about the rest of it. You can live inside that work for years, happily. It is the same marriage. It is the same problem. Only the state has changed.

So here is the mechanism, fully assembled. Put a required result on a problem nobody knows how to solve — a number, a deadline, an audience — and you have installed a verdict over it, which makes it a crisis, which summons anxiety. But the timeline of anxiety is measured in months, and the timeline of discovery is measured in years. So somewhere around month two or three, the mind of the person holding that objective will do exactly what my mind did with my marriage and my weight: it will quietly trade the real problem for a simpler one it can survive. An explanation. A reclassification. A best guess, committed to publicly and then defended, because from the moment it was said out loud, their competence was attached to it.

If that mechanism is real, it should leave a fingerprint at scale — not in a laboratory, but in real companies, over real years. It does. I found it in my own client base, and I promise you I was not looking for it. To show it to you, I first have to show you what I spent fifteen years building.

---

### 5

The first fifteen years of my working life were spent inside other people's companies. More than three hundred of them, across twenty-three industries, on management and growth. Somewhere in the middle of that I started writing down what I was doing, because I kept doing the same things and got tired of reinventing them.

What came out of it was a management system.

It began with **vision, mission and values**, and then it split into two branches. The first was **operational management** — processes, indicators, standards, roles, the discipline of only letting proven things into the operation. If you have read the last two chapters you already know most of it. That branch is what I now call the machine.

The second branch was **growth management** — everything a company needed to do in order to get bigger, better, more profitable. And the engine of that second branch, the instrument that drove the entire growth side of the system, was **objectives.**

Objectives need a rhythm, so I built one. Three days, four times a year, away from the offices. Tables in a horseshoe. Flipcharts along one wall. And one rule I introduced at the very beginning and never softened: the telephones stayed upstairs, in the bedrooms. Not on the table. Not face down beside the notepad. Upstairs.

Day one was the evaluation. Each of the senior managers went to the front of the room, one at a time, and presented the objectives they had personally owned for the previous ninety days. Where they had succeeded. Where they had not. What had happened. The room asked questions. Then we planned: we decided what we wanted the next ninety days to produce, we turned those wants into objectives, each objective got an owner who accepted it in front of everybody, and we went back into the business with a strategy.

If you have ever sat in a quarterly planning meeting anywhere in this part of the world, there is a reasonable chance you were sitting inside a room I designed.

And it worked. I need to be emphatic about this, because everything in this chapter depends on it. The system is used today by more than three thousand companies. Fifty of them are leaders in their markets. I have watched businesses go from one or two million to over a hundred million running it. Separately — these are two different populations, not a conversion rate — more than thirty thousand companies have implemented parts of it, picked up from my courses and lectures.

I did not merely use objectives. I extended them. When I could feel the instrument straining, I did what a craftsman does with a tool he loves: I built better versions. I invented two variations of my own — Dynamic OKRs and Progressive OKRs — each designed to repair a specific failure I kept running into. I wrote well over a hundred articles about the power of goal setting. And this was no local eccentricity: Google ran on OKRs for years and it helped them; a large part of the technology industry still runs on some descendant of the same idea.

So when I tell you what I found, understand that I am not a critic who never picked up the tool.

**I did not stumble into the altar of objectives. I built it, I served at it, and I sold seats.**

---

### 6

A few years after the sabbatical, I decided to audit my own system.

The spirit in which I did it matters, and it was not heroic. I was not out to confirm what the six months had shown me — most of the time I was not even thinking about it. Thousands of companies were running something I had built, and I wanted to know which parts of it were carrying weight and which parts were decoration.

I ran it across **three thousand and twenty-three companies.** Not a sample, and not a selection of the good ones — essentially the whole population that was running the full system.

The first thing I measured was the simplest thing available. Of the objectives set in those quarterly meetings, how many were actually achieved?

**Fifty-six percent were achieved. Forty-four percent were not.**

I want to tell you exactly how I reacted to that number, because it is important. I did not react at all. Fifty-six percent, across three thousand real companies, is a perfectly respectable figure, and every consultant alive has an explanation ready for the other forty-four: stretch goals are supposed to be missed; seventy percent attainment is the healthy range. I had used those explanations. I had taught them. So I wrote the number down and moved on.

And then — I still do not know why — I went back to it. I did not want a better headline figure. I wanted to know something else: **what did the objectives that were achieved have in common with each other? And what did the ones that were missed have in common?**

I sorted the two piles by every variable I could think of — industry, company size, ambition, financial or operational. Most of those told me nothing, which is how you know you have found something when one of them finally does.

**Ninety-seven percent of the objectives that were achieved shared a single property: the company already possessed a validated method for producing that result.**

A company has three locations. All three work. The economics are known, the layout is known, the hiring is known, because they have walked the path three times. The objective for next year is to open two more. They open two more. The method existed. What the objective did was point at a capability already sitting in the building, and put a date on it.

Now the other pile.

**Eighty-eight percent of the objectives that were missed shared the opposite property: nobody in the company knew the method.**

*Grow sales by thirty percent.* How? Nobody knew. That was the entire content of the objective — a number, a deadline, an owner, and a hole where the cause should have been. *Enter two new markets.* How? *Cut churn in half.* How?

Where a method existed, it got applied, and the objective took the credit. Where no method existed, the objective sat in a document for ninety days, generated a great deal of anxiety, and then generated an explanation.

> **In three thousand companies, an objective never once produced a method.**

There it was — the fingerprint. The eighty-eight percent is the sabbatical mechanism, operating at scale, on schedule: hard problems placed under a verdict, held in anxiety for as long as anxiety can be held, and then exited through the nearest simplification. The explanations my managers brought to day one were not failures of character. They were the arrival, right on time, of the mind's escape from a crisis that had been scheduled ninety days earlier, in writing, by me.

And notice that nothing in this mechanism touches the other pile. A number set over a method you already possess never becomes a crisis, because there is nothing unresolvable in it to be anxious about — the path is known, the work is in your power, and the deadline is just a deadline. The crisis is manufactured only where the method is missing. Hold that asymmetry; at the end of this chapter it becomes the rule.

The first chapter of this book drew a line between a result and a cause: a result is what happened, a cause is what makes it happen again. It took me until that spreadsheet to see that the instrument I had built my professional life around sat on the wrong side of that line. An objective is a **result written in advance.** It belongs to the same family as the thing it is supposed to produce — which is precisely why it cannot produce it.

---

### 7

There is an obvious question sitting in the middle of that finding.

If eighty-eight percent of the missed objectives were things nobody knew how to do — **why did anybody accept them?**

These were not naive companies. These were capable leadership teams, in a structured meeting, with three days and no distractions and no telephones. Somebody proposes growing sales by thirty percent with no method attached. The correct response, from any of the eight other people in that room, is a single word.

*How?*

Nobody says it. Or somebody says it once, receives an answer made of adjectives, and lets it go.

I know why, because I built the room where it happens. The second chapter of this book was about it. People want to be heroes, and most organisational systems are built to reward them for it. An objective with no method is on the table; it is difficult, it is important, and everybody privately knows it is a wish. And into that silence steps somebody — there is always somebody — who says, in effect: *I'll do it.* Taking on the impossible number is how you get noticed. And if you fail, you will have failed at something hard, which is a better thing to have on your record than having declined something hard.

That chapter defined the organisational hero as the person whose exceptional intervention repeatedly compensates for a capability the system has not yet built. It said the hero is not the solution to the hole — the hero is the *shape* of the hole. And it called the objective the hero's favourite instrument.

What I had not understood, until I sorted that data, was **where the hole comes from.** It comes from a planning meeting. Four times a year. Conducted by serious people with the best intentions, using a methodology I wrote. An objective set on ground where nobody knows the method **is** the hole, and the person who volunteers for it is the shape of it.

The second chapter also contained a confession: that for years I manufactured my own crises — in my body, in my marriage, in my companies — so that I could rescue them. I told you the mechanism was quiet and that nobody decides to do it. What I can tell you now is that most companies do not even need a hero to do it for them. **They have a meeting that does it on schedule. I designed the meeting.**

### 8

I realise the two states may still sound like one man's temperament, discovered on a sabbatical. So let me show you the verdict working on ten million people at once.

Economists once collected the finishing times of nearly ten million marathon runners — every recorded finish in the world between 1970 and 2013 — and plotted them minute by minute. The curve is smooth, the shape of any large population doing something difficult, except at the round numbers, where it has cliffs: at the three-hour mark, **fifty percent more people finish in the minute before than in the minute after.** The distance does not know what time it is; nothing physiological happens at three hours. But the splits show runners on the edge of their chosen number finding effort in their legs that runners safely inside or hopelessly outside it do not — for a number picked months earlier, that nobody else would ever know about, about to be either satisfied or not. And a companion survey found that **only a quarter of runners hit the time goal they had set** — which means three out of every four people crossed that finish line, after months of training, in the territory of failure. Their satisfaction tracked the number in their head twice as strongly as it tracked how fast they had actually run.

One small experiment makes the mechanism impossible to unsee. Two women are described to a group of participants. Sally has set herself a goal of thirty-one sit-ups; Trish has set herself thirty-nine. Both stop at exactly thirty-five. Asked how each woman feels, **one hundred percent of participants said Sally feels good and Trish feels bad.** Same number of sit-ups, same effort, same body on the same floor — and the only difference was a number each woman had said out loud before she started.

> **A goal is not a description of the work. It is a line drawn across the work, in advance, that converts whatever happens into a verdict.**

The work is continuous. The world arrives in gradations. The goal takes all of that and reduces it to one bit: won, or lost.

A number does not have to be achievable, or meaningful, or even visible to anybody else, to change the state of the body carrying it. Now put the number inside a company. Attach a name to it, seat the colleagues around it, and give it ninety days.

### 9

You could watch the crisis state arrive, if you knew when to look. In every company I worked with, including my own, it arrived two weeks before the quarterly meeting.

The senior people stopped managing and started preparing. Not analysis — **accounts.** The sequence of true facts that will produce the most favourable reading of an unfavourable number. Everybody in that room understood the difference between an explanation and an analysis, and almost nobody was in a position to produce the second kind. Do the arithmetic on it: two weeks, times four quarters, times twenty senior people. Not one hour of it produced a single piece of information the company did not already have.

For years I diagnosed this as a character problem — insufficient ownership, insufficient courage, the sort of thing you address with a speech on the morning of day one. Then I found a study that measured it in blood.

In a year-long randomised field experiment inside a large Swedish public organisation, employees moved to a twelve-week evaluation cycle had significantly higher stress hormones than colleagues kept on a six-week one — cortisol up by a fifth at the six-month mark. And their self-reported fatigue did not differ at all. **They could not feel it.** Ask them and they would have told you they were fine, while their blood said otherwise. The researchers' explanation is a description of my fortnight, written by people who had never seen my meeting: the anticipated threat of being held accountable someday triggers self-justification, the rehearsal of defences — work that is cognitively expensive and interferes with the actual task.

Notice which way that result runs, because it is the opposite of what I expected. **It is not the evaluation that produces the fear. It is the interval.** An annual review manufactures eleven months of low-grade dread and one hour of release. The answer is not to evaluate less. It is to look often enough that nothing has time to accumulate — and to make sure that when you look, there is nothing to defend. That second half is the whole of the next chapter.

And if you want to see what the crisis state does to performance itself, there is one experiment I have never been able to forget. A hundred and seventy people ran a complex business simulation — a market, competitors, thirteen rounds of decisions, and halfway through, a deregulation shock that invalidated every strategy that had been working. One group was given a hard performance goal: reach twenty-one percent market share. One group was given a learning goal: discover and test strategies for running this business. One was told to do their best.

Of the fifty people with the learning goal, eight reached twenty-one percent. Of the fifty-nine told to do their best, six.

**Of the sixty-one people explicitly instructed to reach twenty-one percent — one.**

The people told to hit the number were, by a wide margin, the least able to hit the number. The mechanism is not mysterious once you have stood in it. Under a hard target, on unfamiliar ground, people grab the first strategy that looks like it might work and push. Being systematic looks like not making progress, and not making progress is the thing they have been told to avoid. That is crisis behaviour, reproduced in a laboratory: the mind under threat does not search. It grabs. The teams at Shafter and the teams in England were separated by exactly this — and so were my ninety-seven and my eighty-eight.

### 10

There is a version of this whole argument compressed into a single human life, because history occasionally runs both states through the same mind, on the same problem.

In 1986, a Princeton mathematician named Andrew Wiles learned that new results had connected Fermat's Last Theorem — the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics, unproven for three and a half centuries, and his obsession since the age of ten — to territory where it could finally be attacked. He went home, climbed to his attic study, and stayed there, in secret, for seven years. He told nobody but his wife. No deadlines, no progress reports, no audience, no number. Here is how he described what those years felt like:

> Perhaps I can best describe my experience of doing mathematics in terms of a journey through a dark unexplored mansion. You enter the first room, and it's completely dark. You stumble around bumping into the furniture. Gradually you learn where each piece of furniture is. Finally, after six months or so, you find the light switch, you turn it on, and suddenly it's all illuminated. Then you move into the next room and spend another six months in the dark.

Six months per light switch, for seven years, happily. That is the puzzle state, sustained longer than any company sustains anything.

In June 1993 he announced the proof in Cambridge, to standing ovations and front pages. And then a referee checking the manuscript found a gap: in one crucial step, the argument did not hold. Suddenly every condition of the attic was inverted — a watching world, a public countdown, a verdict pending on each day's work. The same problem, in the same mind, had become a crisis, and it nearly broke him; he later said that doing mathematics in that overexposed way was *certainly not my style, and I have no wish to repeat it.*

The end came in September 1994, and the way it came is the point. Close to giving up, Wiles sat down to take one last look at the failed method — not to force it to work, but to understand precisely *why* it failed. Curiosity, not rescue. And in that examination he saw that the method's failure was exactly the thing that made an approach he had abandoned three years earlier work. He called it the most important moment of his working life.

Seven years of puzzle produced the proof. Fourteen months of crisis nearly destroyed it, and produced nothing — until the morning he turned the crisis back into a puzzle.

### 11

At this point a fair objection arrives. Wiles loved his problem since childhood; MacCready's crew were playing with a flying machine. Your people are not in love with lead qualification. Surely the curiosity I keep describing is a luxury of vocation.

It is not, and the evidence is one of the most unsettling small experiments I know.

Seventy-two young writers — not amateurs; on average they had each published nearly four poems and were writing more than six hours a week — were asked to write a poem on the theme of *Snow.* Then each spent **five minutes** ranking a short list of reasons for writing, in order of personal importance. One group ranked intrinsic reasons: *you enjoy the opportunity for self-expression; you like to play with words.* The other ranked extrinsic ones: *you want your teachers to be favourably impressed; writing ability is a major criterion for graduate school.* A control group skipped the ranking. Then everyone wrote a second poem, on the theme of *Laughter*, and twelve poets, working blind, rated all of them.

The first poems were indistinguishable across the groups. In the second round, the control and intrinsic groups held steady — and the extrinsic group collapsed, falling far below both. Five minutes with a form was enough to measurably damage the creative output of people who loved writing and had loved it for years.

And the detail that almost nobody quotes: **the intrinsic group did not rise.** All of the effect is in the fall.

> **You can break it easily. You cannot build it easily.**

That asymmetry is, I think, the most important thing in this chapter that is not a number from my own companies. There is no management technique that will make your people love the work. Mostly the love is already there — people do not generally spend their lives doing things they hate. What management can do, reliably, cheaply, in five minutes, without anybody intending it, is take it away: place a verdict in front of the work, and the work reorganises itself around the verdict. Every objective you set is, among other things, five minutes with that form.

Curiosity is not a luxury of vocation. It is the default state of a competent person in front of a problem they have a real chance of solving — right up until somebody converts the problem into a crisis.

### 12

My first instinct, holding all of this, was that I had made some enormous private discovery. That instinct did not survive a trip to the library.

**The problem was not undiscovered. It had been published, in good journals, by serious people, for decades. What nobody had built was a replacement.**

In 1987, the *Journal of Applied Psychology* published a meta-analysis of more than a hundred studies asking one question: does the effect of a goal change with the complexity of the task? It does. Goal-setting worked most strongly on the simplest tasks, and the effect fell to roughly half on the most complex — and look at what counted as complex: business simulations, scientific and engineering work. The authors' explanation of why is this chapter's thesis in academic language: on complex tasks, the effect of a goal depends on whether the person can discover an appropriate strategy. The strategy is the method. On complex work, a goal mostly reveals whether the person already had one.

I should be honest about that number: the effect shrinks, it does not vanish, and anybody who tells you the research shows goals stop working on hard problems is selling you something. But now look at the third author on the paper: **Edwin A. Locke** — the man who, with Gary Latham, founded goal-setting theory. The architect of the entire field, publishing the boundary of his own instrument, in a top-tier journal, where it has sat in plain sight ever since.

Eighteen years later, Latham himself — the other founder — went further, in a paper Locke read and is thanked in. A performance goal, he wrote, *often distracts attention from the discovery of task-relevant strategies*; a performance goal *should not be set until an employee has the knowledge to attain it.* When the way to the result is already deciphered, set performance goals. When it requires innovation that has yet to emerge, set learning goals. And then, in a footnote, having just summarised a theory built on five hundred experiments: *but the theory yields no clues as to when to set performance versus learning goals.*

> **The founders published the limit. What travelled was the part that fit on a slide.**

The distinction was in print for twenty years. It named the exact boundary I found in three thousand companies — known method, unknown method — while I was busy inventing better versions of the instrument on the wrong side of it. I had not discovered a problem nobody knew about. I had discovered that the problem was documented by the people who invented the tool, and that in twenty years nobody had built the thing you use instead.

That, in the end, is the work. Not the critique. The replacement.

---

### 13

Which brings me to the thing I got most wrong, and it was not a technique. It was what the room was for.

I have thought for a long time about how to describe my old quarterly meeting honestly, and the most accurate description I have is this: **it was an awards ceremony for heroes.** Who was the hero this quarter, and who was not. That was the actual content of day one, underneath the slides and the market context. And the strangest part is that everybody knew. I knew. We did it four times a year, for fifteen years, because we were serious people who took the business seriously and this was what serious people did.

Put the two possible questions side by side.

*Let us find out who did best.*

*Let us find out the most we can.*

Those are not two moods. They are two machines, and they build two different companies. The first builds a company that lives from verdict to verdict — a permanent, low-grade, scheduled crisis. The second builds a company that is assembling a picture. **We do not actually need heroes. What we need is to know which methods work.** A hero is what you reach for when you do not have that, and a room organised around identifying the hero will never go looking for the method, because the method is not what is being rewarded in it.

If you want to see where that road ends when nobody stops it, look at Atlanta.

Beverly Hall ran the city's public school system from 1999 to 2010, and in 2009 she was named National Superintendent of the Year, for what the citation described as a decade of significant gains. In 2011, a state investigation found cheating on the standardised tests in **forty-four of the fifty-six schools it examined.** A hundred and seventy-eight educators were named. Eighty-two confessed. Thirty-eight were principals. The investigators' report has a section headed, simply, *Targets*, and its finding was that the pressure to meet them was the primary motivation for the cheating. Hall's own formulation was: *No exceptions. No excuses.* Principals who missed targets were replaced — she replaced ninety percent of them — and, in her words, *I will find someone who will meet targets.*

And then there is the detail I cannot get out of my head, because it is my quarterly meeting with the volume turned all the way up. Every year the district held a celebration at the Georgia Dome. **The faculty of schools that hit their targets sat on the floor of the stadium. The faculty of schools that did not were seated in the uppermost sections.**

An awards ceremony. Made out of architecture.

The investigators were careful, and their care is worth borrowing: *we do not express any opinion as to the merits of targets*, they wrote — but targets were implemented in such a way that teachers believed they had to choose between cheating to meet them and losing their jobs for missing them. Eighty-two people confessed. That is not a criminal class. That is a large number of ordinary people placed in a room where the only two available identities were winner and loser, who found the third door.

It does not require a scandal, either. The fifth chapter showed you the quiet version: Deming's red beads — praise the best worker, coach the worst, post the chart, announce the stretch goal, fire the bottom three. Every one of those interventions is a reasonable management action, and not one of them changes anything, because there are eight hundred red beads in the box.

**My quarterly meeting was the red bead experiment, run four times a year, for fifteen years, with real people. And every single thing I did in it was reasonable.**

I want to say clearly what was *not* wrong, because I still believe it and the next chapter is built on it. The rhythm was right. The three days were right. The house, the horseshoe, the telephones upstairs — I would defend every piece of that container tomorrow, and I will. What was wrong was the question I put inside it.

### 14

So where do objectives belong? Not nowhere — and a chapter that ended with *therefore never set a target* would be both wrong and useless.

The best answer I have found was written in 1950, by a man running an industrial laboratory. Mervin Kelly spent his career at Bell Telephone Laboratories, most of it as director of research and then president. The transistor came out of his building; so did information theory, the solar cell, the laser, and a substantial fraction of the twentieth century. When he described how he organised the work, he divided it into zones. Of the research, he wrote: *this is our non-scheduled area of work.* Of the development and design of specific systems: *the work is most carefully programmed.*

Read those two phrases together. *Non-scheduled.* *Most carefully programmed.* **Kelly did not abolish planning. He drew a border around it.** You cannot schedule the discovery of something nobody knows yet; you can and must schedule the building of something you know how to build. That is the known and the unknown from the fourth chapter, drawn as an organisational chart, seventy-five years ago.

And he did not leave it as philosophy. On the NIKE missile programme, the preliminary study found that everything required already existed — except one area of radar technology. They did not put a schedule on that area. They sent research at it, and *the project was not undertaken until this deficiency was eliminated by new knowledge from research.* Then Kelly, who had no management book to sell, added the sentence I would have paid for twenty years ago: he knew of large programmes where research and development were intermingled, *probably with the intent of gaining time. Actually, time has been lost.*

**Do not set the target until you know the method. And if you set it anyway in order to gain time, you will lose time.** An engineer wrote that in 1950, and he had a missile to prove it.

One honest counterweight, because without it the story is propaganda. Kelly could refuse to schedule research because Bell Labs was funded by a regulated monopoly — the cost of the unscheduled zone was quietly recovered from every telephone subscriber in America. Wiles could sit in his attic for seven years because a university salary arrived every month regardless. Almost nobody reading this has either. So take the architecture and leave the economics: the border between scheduled and unscheduled work is available to any company of any size, but the thing that funds your unscheduled side is your machine — which is precisely why the next chapter is so insistent on protecting it. **The machine pays for the laboratory.**

So here is the rule, in the language this book has been building since the fourth chapter.

**Objectives belong in the known.** Where the method exists, has been validated, and simply needs to be pointed somewhere or run at higher volume, an objective is an excellent instrument — it converts a general intention into a specific number that a specific person will be asked about on a specific date, and that conversion is most of what management was invented for. And notice what the same instrument does to the people holding it there. There is no dread in an objective whose method you possess. There is concentration. The number stops being a verdict hanging over the work and becomes a lens that gathers the work — everybody knows what to do, and the objective simply says how much of it, by when. People hit those objectives, because the work is genuinely within their power. That is what my ninety-seven percent was.

**Objectives do not belong in the unknown.** Where the method does not exist, the same number, the same deadline and the same name on the same document produce the opposite state. The objective is not a plan there. It is a wish with a deadline attached — a crisis, manufactured and scheduled. That is what my eighty-eight percent was.

One instrument. Two grounds. Two completely different physiological states.

> **An objective with a validated method behind it creates focus. An objective without one creates a crisis.**

Which means the question to ask before accepting any objective is never *is it ambitious enough?* It is: *do we have the method?* If yes, set it, and it will do what it has always done well. If no —

> **When you do not know how, the goal is not the result. The goal is learning.**

### 15

What does the unknown get instead? It gets an experiment — and the next chapter will put the actual sheet of paper in your hands. Here I only want you to see what changes psychologically, because that is the part no template can carry.

An objective records one bit: won or lost. An experiment turns the same ninety days into a piece of the puzzle. A hypothesis that proves false has erased a road from the map, permanently; a hypothesis that proves true has placed a piece. Either way the picture is more complete than it was, and — this is the part I did not expect when we began working this way — **the room becomes more curious with every piece, not more tired.** The more of the picture you can see, the more you want to see the rest. That is what curiosity does over time, and it is the exact opposite of what anxiety does over time.

I owe you one honest caveat, because I used to say *you cannot fail at an experiment* and the claim, said that baldly, is not true. The gain from a failed experiment is real, but it is not automatic. Researchers who set out to confirm the obvious — that people learn from failure — found the opposite: people learn less from their own failures than from their own successes, because failure threatens the ego and the mind tunes out. But the same people learned from *other people's* failures perfectly well. The information is real and available; what blocks it is ownership of the verdict. Which tells you exactly what the machinery has to do: a written hypothesis and measures agreed in advance are not bureaucracy. **They are the mechanism that moves failure off the person and onto the idea** — the difference between a room hearing *the hypothesis did not hold* and a room hearing *I did not deliver.* That is why the experiment sheet in the next chapter has the fields it has.

And this, finally, is why the fulfilment question — which sounds soft — is actually the hard economics of the whole system. The problems that decide a company's future are the ones that take years: the business model, the market position, the method nobody in the industry has found yet. A company that meets those problems in the crisis state will abandon them, quarter after quarter, at precisely the pace its people's anxiety runs out — and will produce explanations on schedule while the real problem waits. A company that meets them in the puzzle state can stay with them for as long as they take. **The companies that solve the long problems are not the ones that push harder. They are the ones whose people can stand to keep looking.**

Winners and losers have the same goals. That line is James Clear's, and I read it years before I understood it. What winners and losers do not have in common is a method for finding out what is actually true.

I built a system that produced winners and losers, four times a year, for fifteen years, and I taught it to thirty thousand companies. I built it because it was rigorous, because it worked more than half the time — and because I was extremely good at winning inside it. That is the part I would have denied for most of my career and cannot deny now: the second chapter told you I wanted to be the hero, and a room that crowns a hero every quarter is a room a hero builds. There is a study of companies that abandoned their budgets and reinstated them within a year, and the researchers found it was not because the budgets had worked; it was because without them, the senior managers no longer felt in control. I recognise those managers completely. The number on the wall was never doing what I thought it was doing. It was not producing the result. **It was telling me that I was managing.**

Go back to Shafter one last time. The Gossamer Condor was, by every standard of its industry, a bad aeroplane — tubes, film and tape, flown by a cyclist. It hangs in the Smithsonian, and the exquisite machines that spent eighteen years failing do not, because the prize was never going to be won by the team that built the best answer. It was won by the team that could afford the most questions. The men were not braver, and the physics was not different. **A crash simply meant something different in California — and that one difference in meaning was worth eighteen years.**

A quarter of your company's life can be a crisis, or it can be a puzzle. That is not a property of your market, and it is not a property of the problem. It is a property of the room — of what a bad number *means* when it lands on the table. And the room, unlike the market, is entirely in your power.

The next chapter builds it.
