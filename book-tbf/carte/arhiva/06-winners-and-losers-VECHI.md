# 6. Winners and Losers

### Ten Million Runners, Three Thousand Companies, and the Line That Turns Work Into a Verdict

---

### 1

If you collect the finishing times of nearly ten million marathon runners — every recorded finish in the world between 1970 and 2013 — and plot them minute by minute, you get exactly the curve you would expect. A slow rise, a long thick middle, a slow decline. The shape of any large human population doing something difficult.

Except in four places, where the graph has a cliff.

There is a cliff at three hours. There is another at three hours thirty. Another at four hours. Another at four hours thirty. In the minute immediately before each of those marks, the number of finishers spikes. In the minute immediately after, it collapses. At the three-hour mark, **fifty percent more people finish in the minute before than in the minute after.**

Now, a marathon is 42.195 kilometres. It is not 42.195 kilometres for people who want to run three hours and something else for everybody else. The distance does not know what time it is. There is no medal at 2:59:59 that is withheld at 3:00:01. Nothing physiological happens to the human body at the three-hour mark. Nothing at all happens there, except that it is a round number in a system of timekeeping the Babylonians invented for reasons that had nothing to do with running.

And yet ten million people, over forty-three years, on every continent, bent their bodies around it.

The economists who found this went looking for an explanation that did not require the runners to be doing anything strange. Maybe the spikes were an artefact of how races were timed. Maybe it was pacing groups. Maybe it was some feature of how courses are certified.

It was none of those. It was effort.

They could see it in the splits. A runner who arrives at the final stretch on pace for 3:45 — comfortably inside the next round number, no threat — slows down over the last two kilometres by six to eight percent, which is what you would expect from a tired human being at the end of a marathon. A runner who arrives at the same point on pace for 4:05, hopelessly outside the four-hour mark with no way to get there, slows by the same six to eight percent.

But a runner who arrives on pace for 3:58, or 4:01 — right on the edge — slows by only four to six percent.

They found something in their legs. Not because the finish line had moved, and not because anybody was paying them, and not because anybody else would ever know. Because a number they had chosen months earlier, for no reason except that it was round, was about to be either satisfied or not satisfied.

---

### 2

A companion study surveyed eighteen hundred runners before a marathon and again afterwards.

**Eighty-six percent of them had a time goal.** Seventy percent had chosen a goal divisible by ten minutes. Before the race, on average, they put their chances of hitting it at seventy-two percent.

**A quarter of them hit it.**

Which means that three out of every four people who ran that race — people who had trained for months, who had run further than most human beings ever run, who had almost all finished — crossed the line having failed.

And the satisfaction data is the part that should stop you. How happy a runner was with their race correlated only weakly with how fast they actually ran. It correlated strongly, more than twice as strongly, with how their time compared to the number in their head. And the relationship was not a smooth slope. It was a step. **Just clearing your goal, by any margin at all, more than doubled the odds that you would report being satisfied with the same body, the same course, the same day.**

There is a small experiment that makes the mechanism visible in a way the marathon data cannot.

Two women are described to a group of participants. Sally has set herself a goal of thirty-one sit-ups. Trish has set herself a goal of thirty-nine. Both of them stop at exactly thirty-five.

The participants were asked how each woman feels.

**One hundred percent of them said Sally feels good and Trish feels bad.**

Not most. All of them. Same number of sit-ups, same effort, same body on the same floor — and everybody who looked at it agreed that one of these women had succeeded and the other had failed, and the only thing that made the difference was a number each of them had said out loud before she started.

The researchers drew the conclusion that this chapter is built on:

> **If goals are reference points, then people who set goals will sometimes feel worse about their performance even when they perform better.**

I want to state it as plainly as I can, because everything else in this chapter follows from it.

**A goal is not a description of the work. It is a line drawn across the work, in advance, that converts whatever happens into a verdict.**

The work is continuous. Thirty-five sit-ups is more than thirty-four and less than thirty-six. A marathon time is a number with decimal places. Revenue is a number with decimal places. The world arrives in gradations.

The goal takes all of that and reduces it to one bit. Won, or lost.

And that is what we do to people in our companies. Four times a year. In a room, with their colleagues watching.

I know, because I built the room. I built it carefully, over fifteen years, and I taught it to thirty thousand companies, and it worked.

This chapter is about what it cost, and about the thing I could not see until I went looking for something else entirely.

Before we can talk about how to audit a machine — which is the next chapter, and the practical heart of this book — we have to deal with objectives. Because if you take the audit I am going to describe and put objectives inside it, you will get back exactly the room I spent fifteen years running, and none of the rest of it will work.

---

### 3

The first fifteen years of my working life were spent inside other people's companies. More than three hundred of them, across twenty-three industries, on management and growth. Somewhere in the middle of that I started writing down what I was doing, because I kept doing the same things and got tired of reinventing them.

What came out of it was a management system.

The architecture was simple enough to draw on a napkin, and I drew it on a great many napkins. It began with **vision, mission and values.** Then it split into two branches.

The first branch was **operational management** — processes, indicators, standards, roles, the discipline of only letting proven things into the operation. If you have read the fourth and fifth chapters of this book you already know most of it. It has been rebuilt in places since, but the bones are the same. That branch is what I now call the machine.

The second branch was **growth management.** Everything a company needed to do in order to get bigger, better, more profitable. Everything that was not simply running the thing you already had.

And the engine of that second branch — the instrument that drove the entire growth side of the system — was **objectives.**

Objectives need a rhythm, so I built one. Three days, four times a year, away from the offices. Tables in a horseshoe. Flipcharts along one wall. And one rule I introduced at the very beginning and never softened: the telephones stayed upstairs, in the bedrooms. Not on the table. Not face down beside the notepad. Upstairs.

Day one was the evaluation. Each of the senior managers went to the front of the room, one at a time, and presented the objectives they had personally owned for the previous ninety days. Where they had succeeded. Where they had not. What had happened. The room asked questions.

Then we planned. We decided what we wanted the next ninety days to produce, we turned those wants into objectives, each objective got an owner who accepted it in front of everybody, and we went back into the business with a strategy.

If you have ever sat in a quarterly planning meeting anywhere in this part of the world, there is a reasonable chance you were sitting inside a room I designed.

---

### 4

And it worked.

I need to be emphatic about this, because everything in this chapter depends on it. The version of this argument in which the tool never worked is both false and useless. If objectives did not produce results, nobody would use them, this chapter would not be necessary, and you would have thrown them out years ago on your own.

The system is used today by more than three thousand companies. Fifty of them are leaders in their markets. I have watched businesses go from one or two million to over a hundred million running it.

Separately from that — and these are two different populations, not a conversion rate — more than thirty thousand companies have implemented parts of it, picked up from my courses and lectures.

I did not merely use objectives. I extended them. When I could feel the instrument straining in particular places, I did what a craftsman does with a tool he loves: I built better versions. I invented two variations of my own, which I called Dynamic OKRs and Progressive OKRs, each designed to repair a specific failure I kept running into. I wrote well over a hundred articles about the power of goal setting.

And this was not some local eccentricity. Google ran on OKRs for years and it helped them. A large part of the technology industry runs on some descendant of the same idea. Every serious manager I knew was using some version of it, and the ones who were not were, as far as I could tell, worse off.

So when I tell you what I found, understand that I am not a critic who never picked up the tool.

I did not stumble into the altar of objectives. I built it, I served at it, and I sold seats.

---

### 5

A few years ago, for reasons that had more to do with my personal life than with my work, I decided to audit my own system.

The spirit in which I did it matters, and it was not heroic. I was not looking for a flaw. I had no thesis. Thousands of companies were running something I had built, and I wanted to know which parts of it were carrying weight and which parts were decoration. It was the kind of housekeeping you do when you have been doing something long enough to suspect that some of it has become habit.

I ran it across **three thousand and twenty-three companies.** Not a sample, and not a selection of the good ones — essentially the whole population that was running the full system.

The first thing I measured was the simplest thing available. Of the objectives set in those quarterly meetings, how many were actually achieved?

**Fifty-six percent were achieved. Forty-four percent were not.**

I want to tell you exactly how I reacted to that number, because it is important.

I did not react at all.

There was no moment of recognition. Nothing jumped. Fifty-six percent, across three thousand real companies with real markets and real staff turnover, is a perfectly respectable figure. Every consultant alive has an explanation ready for the other forty-four: stretch goals are supposed to be missed, seventy percent attainment is the healthy range, if you hit all of them you did not aim high enough.

I had used those explanations. I had taught them, in rooms exactly like the one I described above.

So I wrote the number down and moved on to the next item on the list.

---

### 6

And then — I still do not know why, and I have thought about it since — I went back to it.

I did not want a better headline figure. I wanted to know something else. **What did the objectives that were achieved have in common with each other? And what did the ones that were missed have in common?**

So I went back into the data and sorted the two piles by every variable I could think of. Industry. Company size. Seniority of the owner. Whether the objective was financial or operational. How ambitious it was. How long the company had been running the system.

Most of those told me nothing, which is how you know you have found something when one of them finally does.

**Ninety-seven percent of the objectives that were achieved shared a single property: the company already possessed a validated method for producing that result.**

Let me make that concrete, because in the abstract it sounds like a technicality and it is not.

A company has three locations. All three work. The economics are known, the layout is known, the hiring is known, the path to break-even is known, because they have walked it three times. The objective for next year is to open two more.

They open two more.

The method existed. The experience existed. The processes existed. What the objective did was point at a capability that was already sitting in the building, and put a date on it.

Now the other pile.

**Eighty-eight percent of the objectives that were missed shared the opposite property: nobody in the company knew the method.**

*Grow sales by thirty percent.* How? Nobody knew. That was the entire content of the objective — a number, a deadline, an owner, and a hole where the cause should have been. *Enter two new markets.* How? *Cut churn in half.* How?

Read the two figures next to each other and the conclusion arrives without any help from me.

Where a method existed, it got applied, and the objective took the credit. Where no method existed, the objective sat in a document for ninety days, generated a great deal of anxiety, and then generated an explanation.

> **In three thousand companies, an objective never once produced a method.**

The first chapter of this book drew a line between a result and a cause: a result is what happened, a cause is what makes it happen again. It took me until that spreadsheet to see that the instrument I had built my professional life around sat on the wrong side of that line.

An objective is a **result written in advance.** It is a result with a date on it and somebody's name beside it. It belongs to the same family as the thing it is supposed to produce, which is precisely why it cannot produce it.

---

### 7

There is an obvious question sitting in the middle of that finding, and it is the question that turned a statistic into a chapter.

If eighty-eight percent of the missed objectives were things nobody knew how to do — **why did anybody accept them?**

These were not naive companies. These were organisations with capable leadership teams, sitting in a room, in a structured meeting, with three days and no distractions and no telephones. Somebody proposes growing sales by thirty percent with no method attached. The correct response, from any of the eight other people in that room, is a single word.

*How?*

Nobody says it. Or somebody says it once, receives an answer made of adjectives, and lets it go.

I know why, because I built the room where it happens.

**People want to be heroes. And most organisational systems are built to reward them for it.**

Look at what actually happens in that moment. An objective with no method is on the table. It is difficult, it is important, and everybody privately knows it is a wish. And into that silence steps somebody — there is always somebody — who says, in effect: *I'll do it.*

That person is not being reckless. They are responding correctly to the incentives in front of them. Taking on the impossible number is how you get noticed. It is how you get the promotion, the budget, the reputation, the sense that you matter here. And if you fail, you will have failed at something hard, which is a far better thing to have on your record than having declined something hard.

The second chapter of this book defined an organisational hero as the person whose exceptional intervention repeatedly compensates for a capability the system has not yet built. It said that every hero in your company is standing in front of a hole — that the hero is not the solution to the hole, the hero is the *shape* of the hole, and that as long as they keep standing there nobody can see how big it is.

What I had not understood, until I sorted that data, was **where the hole comes from.**

It comes from a planning meeting. Four times a year. Conducted by serious people with the best intentions, using a methodology I wrote.

An objective set on ground where nobody knows the method **is** the hole. And the person who volunteers for it is the shape of it.

That is not a story about ambition being dangerous. It is a story about a room that produces a specific kind of silence at a specific moment, and about who steps into it.

---

### 8

My first instinct was that I had made a mistake somewhere. That is a reasonable instinct and I recommend it, but it did not survive the arithmetic. Three thousand and twenty-three companies is not a sample that produces an accident of that size.

So I went looking for somebody who had seen it before me.

What I found was not what I expected, and it changed how I understand my own contribution.

**The problem was not undiscovered. It had been published, in good journals, by serious people, for decades. What nobody had built was a replacement.**

In 1987, the *Journal of Applied Psychology* published a meta-analysis covering a hundred and twenty-five studies from the previous two decades. It asked one question: does the effect of a goal change depending on how complex the task is?

It does. Goal-setting effects were strongest on the simplest tasks — reaction time, brainstorming — at around d = 0.76. On the most complex tasks, the effect fell to about **0.42.** And look at what the paper counted as complex: business game simulations, scientific and engineering work, the research productivity of university faculty.

I want to be honest about that number before I use it, because the temptation to overstate it is enormous. **0.42 is still a positive, medium effect.** The benefit of a goal on complex work shrinks by roughly forty-five percent. It does not vanish. Anybody who tells you the research shows goals stop working on hard problems is selling you something.

But the authors' own explanation of *why* it shrinks is the thesis of this entire chapter, written in academic language thirty-odd years before I stumbled into it with a spreadsheet:

> On complex tasks, the effect of a goal depends on whether the person can discover an appropriate strategy — and people vary enormously in that ability.

The strategy is the method. On complex work, a goal does nothing except reveal whether the person already had a way to do it.

And then I looked at the third author on the paper.

**Edwin A. Locke.** The man who, with Gary Latham, founded goal-setting theory. The architect of the entire field. The person whose work every corporate target-setting seminar on earth is downstream of.

This is not a critic of objectives assembling evidence against them. This is the founder publishing the boundary condition of his own theory, himself, in a top-tier journal, where it has sat in plain sight ever since.

> **The founder published the limit. What travelled was the part that fit on a slide.**

And it goes further than that, which I did not know until much later.

In 2005, Gary Latham — the other founder, the co-author of the theory itself — published a paper with Gerard Seijts asking directly when you should use a performance goal and when you should not. The acknowledgements thank Ed Locke, who read it.

Here is what they wrote.

> **A performance outcome goal often distracts attention from the discovery of task-relevant strategies.**

> That setting specific challenging performance goals can sometimes lead to worse performance than urging individuals to do their best is at first glance **astonishing** in that this conclusion is contrary to the weight of over a quarter of a century of accumulated findings in the behavioral sciences.

> **Our research shows that a performance goal should not be set until an employee has the knowledge to attain it.**

And then, on page 130, the sentence I would have paid a great deal of money to have found fifteen years earlier:

> **When the strategy for an organization is already known, and the ways to attain it have been deciphered, setting performance goals for an individual or team is appropriate. When an effective strategy requires innovation that has yet to emerge, specific high learning goals should be set.**

In a footnote, having just summarised a theory built on more than five hundred laboratory and field experiments, they add:

> But the theory yields no clues as to when to set performance versus learning goals.

I want to be precise about what Latham is doing there, because it would be easy and dishonest to overstate it. He is not abandoning goal-setting theory. He is conceding that **performance** goals backfire when the method is missing. That is a narrower claim than the one I am making, and he would probably not sign the rest of this chapter.

But sit with what it means for the rest of us.

The distinction was in print. It was published by the people who invented the instrument. It named the exact boundary I found in three thousand companies twenty years later, by accident, while looking for something else.

**I had not discovered a problem nobody knew about. I had discovered that the problem was documented, that the founders had documented it themselves, and that in twenty years nobody had built the thing you use instead.**

That, in the end, is the work. Not the critique. The replacement.

---

### 9

So let me lay out what an objective actually costs, now that I have stopped being defensive about it.

There are five costs. Only one of them is the one everybody talks about, and it is not the most expensive.

**The first is that you stop learning.**

An objective tells you what to deliver. It never tells you to find out how.

Watch what that does to a room. There is a sentence that almost never survives in a meeting organised around objectives, and it is this one: *I want to run experiments until I find the best method.*

Say it out loud in that room and listen to how it lands. It sounds like hedging. It sounds like somebody managing their own exposure. It has no date in it, no number, nothing anybody can celebrate in ninety days.

And it is almost always the correct answer.

There is an experiment I have not been able to stop thinking about since I found it. A hundred and seventy people ran a business simulation — a market, competitors, thirteen rounds of decisions. Halfway through, at round eight, the researchers detonated a deregulation shock that invalidated the strategies that had been working. It is as close to a real market as a laboratory gets.

The participants were split three ways. One group was given a specific performance goal: **reach twenty-one percent market share.** One group was given a learning goal: identify and implement six or more strategies for running this business. One group was simply told to do their best.

Out of a hundred and seventy people, **fifteen reached twenty-one percent market share.**

Eight of the fifty in the learning condition. Six of the fifty-nine told to do their best.

**And one out of sixty-one in the group that had been told to reach twenty-one percent.**

Read that again, because it is not a subtle result. The people who were explicitly instructed to hit the number were, by a very wide margin, the least likely to hit the number. Being told the destination made them worse at arriving.

The mechanism is not mysterious once you have seen it. Under a hard target, in unfamiliar territory, people grab the first strategy that looks like it might work and push. They do not have time to be systematic. Being systematic looks like not making progress, and not making progress is what they have been told to avoid.

There is a study from *Organization Science* that maps the same chain at organisational scale: people working under challenging goals interpret weak early feedback as failure faster, abandon promising alternatives sooner, exploit more and explore less — and then adapt slowly when the environment shifts. Not because they are slow. Because they have nothing prepared. They spent the whole period pushing on the one thing.

---

### 10

**The second cost is that you choose what you already know how to do.**

For this one I do not need a study. I ran the mechanism myself, four times a year, for fifteen years, and I never once saw what I was doing.

Go back to the three days.

Day one is the evaluation. Everybody stands at the front, one at a time, and accounts for the objectives they owned. The room watches. Somebody has a difficult quarter and explains it. The room watches that too.

Day three is when we set the next quarter's objectives, and each person proposes their own.

Which means that at the moment a person decides what to commit to for the next ninety days, they have just spent two full days watching what happens to somebody who commits to something and misses.

Nobody says this out loud. Nobody has to. Everybody in that room is quietly, mostly unconsciously, calculating how ambitious they can afford to be.

**And then we spent day three congratulating ourselves on the ambition.**

I built a meeting that systematically selected for small ambition while I stood at the front of it talking about ambition. It took me fifteen years and a spreadsheet to notice.

The behaviour has a name, and the name is older than I am. In 1977, researchers described what extrinsically motivated workers actually do as a **minimax** strategy: *they attempt to perform the bare minimum of work sufficient for the achievement of maximal rewards.*

Nineteen seventy-seven. Thirty years before anybody in my world had heard of an OKR, somebody had already demonstrated in a laboratory that if you attach the reward to the number, people will find the cheapest number that collects the reward.

That is not cynicism about human nature. It is the correct response to the system. If the system judges you on whether you cleared the line, the rational move is to put the line somewhere you can clear.

---

### 11

**The third cost is that fear replaces curiosity.**

Two weeks before every quarterly meeting, in every company I worked with including my own, something happened that I treated for years as an unfortunate but minor overhead.

The senior people stopped managing and started preparing.

Not analysis. **Accounts.** The sequence of true facts that will produce the most favourable reading of an unfavourable number. Everybody in that room understood the difference between an explanation and an analysis, and almost nobody was in a position to produce the second kind.

Do the arithmetic on that. Two weeks, times four quarters, times twenty senior people. And not one hour of it produced a single piece of information the company did not already have.

Worse: assembling an account is skilled work. Some people are exceptionally good at it. And in a culture built on objectives, those are the people who get promoted — which is how the condition becomes hereditary.

For years I diagnosed this as a character problem. Insufficient ownership. Insufficient courage. The sort of thing you address with a speech about accountability on the morning of day one.

Then I found a study that reframed it completely, and it did not measure what people said. It measured their blood.

Researchers ran a randomised field experiment for a year inside a large Swedish public insurance organisation. Real employees, real evaluations, real managers. They randomised **within** teams, so every manager ran both conditions. And they took intravenous blood samples at the start, at six months and at twelve.

One group was moved to a performance evaluation cycle of **twelve weeks.** The other was kept on a cycle of **six.**

The employees on the twelve-week cycle had significantly higher cortisol and thyrotropin than the ones on the six-week cycle. Cortisol was up **twenty-one and a half percent** at the six-month mark.

And here is the part that matters most: **their self-reported mental fatigue did not differ at all.** They could not feel it. Ask them and they would have told you they were fine.

The researchers' explanation is a description of my fortnight, written by people who had never been in my meeting:

> The anticipatory threat of being held accountable someday in the future functions as a stressor… such a threat triggers self-critical thoughts and self-justifications in evaluatees, and induces active reflection on what are justifiable actions, what superiors' expectations are, what justification strategies are available… **These behaviors act as stressors because they are cognitively demanding but also because they are likely to interfere negatively with task execution itself.**

Now notice which way that result runs, because it is the opposite of what I expected and it is more useful than what I expected.

**It is not the evaluation that produces the fear. It is the interval.**

The researchers say so explicitly. They contest the argument that frequent reviews cause stress and conclude the reverse: frequent reviews may *reduce* the build-up of anticipatory threat.

Which means the answer is not to evaluate people less. An annual review manufactures eleven months of low-grade dread and one hour of release. The answer is to look often enough that nothing has time to accumulate — and to make sure that when you look, there is nothing to defend.

That second half is the whole of the next chapter.

While we are here, one more number about feedback in general, because it is not what most managers assume. The largest meta-analysis ever conducted on the subject — six hundred and seven effect sizes, twenty-three thousand observations — found that feedback improved performance on average, at d = 0.41.

**And that in over a third of cases, it made performance worse.**

The authors called feedback interventions *double-edged swords.* The variance in that distribution was ten times larger than sampling noise could produce. It is not statistical fog. Feedback is a genuinely dangerous instrument, and the studies where it was most threatening to the recipient's sense of themselves produced an average effect of 0.08 — which is to say, the same as doing nothing at all.

---

### 12

**The fourth cost is the one I find hardest to talk about in a business book, and it is probably the largest. The work stops being enjoyable.**

There is a study I keep returning to because of who the subjects were.

Seventy-two young writers. Not amateurs — on average they had published nearly four poems each and were writing more than six hours a week. They were recruited with an advertisement that said, in part: *We are studying people's reasons for writing.*

Each of them wrote a poem on the theme of **Snow.**

Then they read a short story. Then they spent **five minutes** ranking seven reasons for writing, in order of personal importance.

One group got a list of intrinsic reasons: *you enjoy the opportunity for self-expression* · *you like to play with words* · *you feel relaxed when writing.*

The other group got a list of extrinsic reasons: *you want your writing teachers to be favorably impressed* · *you enjoy public recognition of your work* · *you know that writing ability is one of the major criteria for acceptance into graduate school.*

A control group did not do the ranking at all.

Then all of them wrote a second poem, on the theme of **Laughter.** Twelve poets, working blind, rated every poem.

The first poems were indistinguishable. Control 18.18, intrinsic 18.76, extrinsic 18.19. No difference whatsoever.

The second poems: control **18.78**, intrinsic **19.88**, extrinsic **15.74**.

The researcher's own comment:

> It is nonetheless startling that **spending barely five minutes reading and ranking extrinsic reasons for writing** could have a significant impact on the creativity of creative writers.

Five minutes with a form.

And now the detail that almost nobody quotes, and that changes what the study means. **The intrinsic group did not rise significantly.** All of the effect is in the fall.

> **You can break it easily. You cannot build it easily.**

That asymmetry is, I think, the most important thing in this chapter that is not a number about my own companies. There is no management technique that will make your people love the work. The love is either there or it is not, and mostly it is there, because people generally do not spend their lives doing things they hate. What management can do — reliably, cheaply, in five minutes, without anybody intending it — is take it away.

The same shape shows up in a study of preschool children who liked to draw. Some were promised a certificate for drawing; some got one unexpectedly; some got nothing. The famous result is that the promised group later chose to draw less in free play.

But the researchers measured something else that almost never gets repeated. **The promised group did not produce fewer drawings during the session itself. They produced worse ones.** Judges who did not know the conditions rated their work significantly lower in quality.

The reward did not reduce the amount of work. It reduced the quality of the work, on the spot, while the child was doing it for the reward.

And if you think this is a children-and-poets phenomenon that stops at the office door, there is a study built on a database of over twelve thousand employee assessments, validated inside a single company with more than thirty thousand people, comparing a hundred and forty-one matched pairs of projects — highly creative against uncreative — inside the same firm.

The strongest discriminators between a creative project and an uncreative one were **challenging work** and **freedom.**

And the weakest, by a wide margin, was **workload pressure.**

It is not how much work you give people that destroys the good part. It is the conditions you make them do it in.

One honest caveat, and I take it from the researcher herself, because it is the objection any competent manager will raise and it deserves to be raised properly. The freedom that matters is freedom in deciding **how** to carry out a project. It is not freedom in deciding **which** project gets done. Nobody in this chapter is arguing that people should work on whatever they feel like. The vision decides what gets worked on. That was the third chapter.

---

### 13

**The fifth cost is that it is binary — and here I have to correct something I have said carelessly for years.**

An objective has exactly two outcomes. Achieved, or missed. Won, or lost. Whatever actually happened in those ninety days, however much the company learned, however much closer you got, the instrument records one bit.

I used to say, in workshops, that an experiment is always a win. That you cannot fail at an experiment; you can only measure it badly; that knowledge accumulates in both directions and therefore every properly designed experiment is a gain for the organisation.

I still believe the spirit of that. But the claim, stated that way, is not true, and I would rather say so than have somebody else say it for me.

When researchers examined nearly five thousand effects from more than two and a half thousand real experiments run inside real companies, they found that roughly **one in five interventions that reach statistical significance turn out to be ineffective when actually deployed.** A false positive is not a gain. It is a wrong belief that has been given a certificate and let into the machine.

And there is a finding that cuts even closer. Across five studies with nearly seventeen hundred participants, researchers set out to confirm the obvious — that people learn from failure — and found the opposite. **Participants learned less from failure feedback than from success feedback.** Even when the two conveyed exactly the same information. Even when learning was paid for.

The explanation:

> Failure is ego threatening, which causes people to tune out.

But in the same studies, those same people **learned just as much from other people's failures as from other people's successes.**

There it is. The information in a failed experiment is real, and it is available, and human beings will absorb it perfectly well — as long as it is not their own.

Which tells you exactly what the machinery has to do.

> **The gain from a failed experiment is real, but it is not automatic. It requires a structure that decouples the result from the person. Experiments do not beat opinions because they produce information. They beat opinions because "the hypothesis failed" removes the threat to the self that "you failed" installs.**

So here is the honest version, and it is the one I will defend:

An objective records one bit. An experiment turns the same ninety days into information — but only under four conditions. There has to be enough signal to beat the noise. There has to be genuine uncertainty beforehand, or you have merely spent money confirming what you knew. It has to be cheap and fast enough that being wrong costs a fortnight instead of a year. And it has to be framed so that the hypothesis fails, not the person.

Remove any one of those and you have paid the cost of an experiment without buying the gain.

That last condition is not sentiment, and it is not culture in the poster sense. It is a design requirement, and it is the reason the experiment sheet in the next chapter has the fields it has. A hypothesis and a set of measures agreed in advance are not bureaucracy. **They are the mechanism that moves failure off the person and onto the idea.**

### 14

Which brings me to the thing I got most wrong, and it was not a technique. It was what the room was for.

I have thought for a long time about how to describe my old quarterly meeting honestly, and the most accurate description I have is this.

**It was an awards ceremony for heroes.**

Who was the hero this quarter, and who was not. That was the actual content of day one, underneath the slides and the market context and the discussion of what had got in the way.

And the strangest part is that everybody knew. I knew. We did it four times a year, for fifteen years, in a house with the telephones upstairs, because we were serious people who took the business seriously and this was what serious people did.

Put the two possible questions side by side.

*Let us find out who did best.*

*Let us find out the most we can.*

Those are not two moods. They are two machines, and they build two different companies.

**We do not actually need heroes. What we need is to know which methods work for what we are trying to do.** A hero is what you reach for when you do not have that. And a room organised around identifying the hero will never go looking for the method, because the method is not what is being rewarded in it.

If you want to see where that road ends when nobody stops it, look at what happened to the Atlanta public school system.

Beverly Hall ran it from 1999 to 2010. In 2009 she was named National Superintendent of the Year by the American Association of School Administrators, for what the citation described as significant gains in student achievement over the previous decade.

In 2011, a special investigation ordered by the governor of Georgia found cheating on the state competency tests in **forty-four of the fifty-six schools it examined.** It identified a hundred and seventy-eight educators as involved. **Eighty-two of them confessed. Thirty-eight were principals.**

The investigators' report contains a section headed, simply, *Targets.* Their finding was that the pressure to meet them was the primary motivation for the cheating. Hall's own formulation, quoted in the report, was: *No exceptions. No excuses.* If principals did not meet targets within three years they would be replaced, and *I will find someone who will meet targets.* She replaced ninety percent of the principals during her tenure.

And then there is the detail I cannot get out of my head, because it is my quarterly meeting with the volume turned all the way up.

Every year the district held a system-wide celebration at the Georgia Dome. **The faculty of schools that hit their targets sat on the floor of the stadium. The faculty of schools that did not were seated in the uppermost sections.**

An awards ceremony. Made out of architecture. Once a year, where everybody could see it.

I want to be careful here, and so were the investigators, and their care is worth borrowing:

> **We do not express any opinion as to the merits of targets.** However, targets were implemented by APS in such a way that teachers and administrators believed that they had to choose between cheating to meet targets or failing to meet targets and losing their jobs.

Eighty-two people confessed. That is not a criminal class. That is a large number of ordinary people who were placed in a room where the only two available identities were winner and loser, and who found the third door.

It does not require a scandal, either. The fifth chapter of this book already showed you the quiet version. W. Edwards Deming used to run a factory in front of a room full of executives — a box of four thousand beads, eight hundred of them red, a paddle with fifty holes. The standard was no more than three red beads. Then he would praise the best worker, coach the worst, put a chart on the wall, introduce merit pay, announce a stretch goal, and eventually lay off the three worst performers.

Every one of those interventions is a reasonable management action. I have taken all of them. And not one of them changes anything, because there are eight hundred red beads in the box.

**My quarterly meeting was the red bead experiment, run four times a year, for fifteen years, with real people. And every single thing I did in it was reasonable.**

### 15

So where do objectives belong?

Not nowhere. I want to be exact about this, because a chapter that ends with *therefore never set a target* would be both wrong and useless, and you would be right to ignore it.

The best answer I have found was written in 1950, by a man running an industrial laboratory.

Mervin Kelly spent his career at Bell Telephone Laboratories, most of it as director of research and then as president. The transistor came out of his building. So did information theory, the solar cell, the laser, and a substantial fraction of the twentieth century.

And when he described how he organised the work, he divided it into three zones.

> The first includes all of the research and fundamental development. **This is our non-scheduled area of work.** It provides the reservoir of completely new knowledge, principles, materials, methods, and art…

> The second we call systems engineering. Its major responsibility is the determination of the new specific systems and facilities development projects…

> The third encompasses all specific development and design of new systems and facilities. **The work is most carefully programmed** in conformity with the plan established by the systems engineering studies.

Read those two phrases together. *Non-scheduled area of work.* *Most carefully programmed.*

**Kelly did not abolish planning. He drew a border around it.**

The research is unscheduled — not because researchers are delicate, but because you cannot schedule the discovery of something nobody knows yet. The development is programmed to within an inch of its life, because by then you know what you are building.

That is the distinction this book has been making since the fourth chapter, drawn as an organisational chart, seventy-five years ago.

And he did not leave it as philosophy. He described what it looked like in practice on a missile programme:

> The programming study on the NIKE missile system established that basic knowledge and art were available for the development of a system that would meet the service requirements **except for a particular area of radar technology.** This area was at once subjected to a research and exploratory development attack. **The project was not undertaken until this deficiency was eliminated by new knowledge from research.**

They found the one place where the method did not exist. They did not put a schedule on it. They sent research at it, and they refused to start the programme until it came back.

And then Kelly, who was not writing for a management audience and had no book to sell, added this:

> I am familiar with large military systems developments where this approach is absent, where research and exploration are intermingled with specific development, **probably with the intent of gaining time. Actually, time has been lost.**

**Do not set the target until you know the method. And if you set it anyway in order to gain time, you will lose time.**

An engineer wrote that in 1950, and he had a missile to prove it.

Now the counterweight, and it belongs on this same page rather than in a footnote, because without it the story is propaganda.

Kelly could refuse to schedule research because Bell Labs was funded by a regulated monopoly. AT&T's rate of return was set by law, which meant research went into the rate base, which meant the cost was recovered from every telephone subscriber in America. One historian of science called it a built-in research tax on telephone service.

That is not a management lesson. **It is a funding condition, and almost nobody reading this has it.**

And you can watch what happened when it went away. AT&T was broken up in 1984. By 2008, in the laboratory that produced the transistor, **four scientists remained in fundamental physics research.**

So take the architecture and leave the economics. The border between scheduled and unscheduled work is available to any company of any size. The ability to fund the unscheduled side out of a legal monopoly is not — which is precisely why the next chapter is so insistent that the machine has to keep working. **The machine pays for the laboratory.** Kelly had a monopoly. You have an operation, and if you damage it in the name of exploring, you have destroyed the thing that funds the exploration.

### 16

I said that nobody had built the replacement. That is true of the management literature I came from, but it is not quite true of the world, and there is one institution I wish I had known about twenty years earlier.

In 1970, the largest commercial bank in Sweden was in serious trouble. Profitability was poor, it was in conflict with the authorities, the chief executive had left and so had several of the senior people. The board went looking for somebody to run it, and they picked a man named Jan Wallander, who was running a provincial bank in the north of the country.

Here is how he described himself, later, in print:

> I did not have any practical business experience and knew nothing about banking.

The first thing he did was abolish the budget.

Not reform it. Not simplify it. Abolish it, along with centrally set sales targets, on the argument that a budget is either an extrapolation of what just happened — in which case it tells you nothing you did not know — or a forecast of a future that is genuinely different, in which case it is wrong. He thought budgets did not help organisations adapt. He thought they slowed adaptation down, because people treat deviations from the budget curve as noise rather than as news.

That was fifty-five years ago.

Here is what Handelsbanken's annual report said in 2018, and again, in almost identical words, in 2025:

> Our employees who meet customers are paid no variable remuneration – neither in the form of bonuses nor commissions – and therefore have no financial incentive to convince the customer that a particular service or product suits them best. **Nor does the Bank have requirements regarding volumes, budgets or centrally determined sales targets.** Instead, the Bank measures its success in terms of customer satisfaction, cost-effectiveness and profitability.

Every branch is an independent profit centre with its own full profit and loss account. And the corporate goal — the one thing the whole bank is measured against — is not a number at all. It is **a higher return on equity than the average of comparable banks in its home markets.**

Look at what that is. It is not a promise negotiated in a meeting. It is a default, measured against what the rest of the market manages to produce. If the industry has a bad year and you have a slightly less bad year, you have met your goal. If the industry has a spectacular year and you merely have a good one, you have not.

**The number cannot be gamed by choosing it, because nobody in the bank chooses it.**

The bank met that goal in **forty-six of the following forty-seven years.**

I want to be careful with that figure, because the version that circulates is wrong and the correct version is better. The 2018 annual report claimed a forty-seventh consecutive year. Five months later, in a quarterly filing, the board reversed the provision to the employee profit-sharing scheme for 2018 — and that provision is only made when the corporate goal has been met. The claim was quietly withdrawn, and the bank has not published a running count since.

Forty-six out of forty-seven, with one year they went back and corrected, is a far more convincing number than forty-seven out of forty-seven.

And I have to tell you the rest, because a story that ends in 2018 would be dishonest.

Since 2019 the model has been contracting. The bank left the Baltic states and Poland — before the pandemic, not because of it. In 2020 it announced it would cut its branch network from around three hundred and eighty to roughly two hundred, with about a thousand redundancies in Sweden. In 2021 it left Denmark and Finland, explaining that the two markets accounted for thirteen percent of costs and eight percent of operating profit. It has gone from nearly eight hundred branches worldwide to just over four hundred.

The branch — the independent profit centre, the sacred unit of Wallander's entire design — has been halved.

And yet the bank has not collapsed. Its cost-to-income ratio in 2025 was around forty-one percent against roughly fifty for its competitors. Return on equity was thirteen percent.

**What changed is not the profitability. It is the model.** Which is its own kind of lesson, and an honest one: a system can be right about the thing it is right about and still be overtaken by an industry that has moved.

### 17

So here is the rule, in the language this book has been building since the fourth chapter.

**Objectives belong in the known.** Where the method exists, has been validated, and simply needs to be pointed somewhere or run at higher volume, an objective is an excellent instrument. It converts a general intention into a specific number that a specific person will be asked about on a specific date, and that conversion is most of what management was invented for.

And notice what happens to attainment in that territory. People hit their objectives — because the method is validated, the process exists, and the work is genuinely within their power.

Which is exactly what my audit found. Ninety-seven percent.

**Objectives do not belong in the unknown.** Where the method does not exist, an objective is not a plan. It is a wish with a deadline attached and somebody's name on it.

> **When you do not know how, the goal is not the result. The goal is learning.**

And the mechanism is not philosophical, it is arithmetical. Attach a required result to ground where no method exists and you have built a room with exactly two exits. Either a person carries ninety days of stress for a number they have no way to produce — or they take their best current guess, commit to it publicly, and then defend it for the rest of the quarter, because from the moment they said it out loud their competence has been attached to it.

The first chapter of this book called that converting uncertainty into premature commitment. I wrote that sentence before I could prove it.

Eighty-eight percent.

I would like to end this chapter by telling you that once you see this, the change is easy. It is not, and there is a piece of research that shows exactly how not-easy it is.

Some years ago a Scandinavian bank abandoned its budget. Not a small experiment — a genuine, organisation-wide removal, of the kind Wallander did. And within about a year, it put the budget back.

Researchers went in to find out why, expecting to hear that the alternative had not worked operationally. That is not what they found. Their conclusion:

> **Undergoing a period of budget abandonment causes senior managers to realize the importance of budgets for maintaining a sense of control.**

They did not go back because the budget produced better results. They went back because without it, they did not feel like they were in charge of anything.

That is not an observation about instruments. It is an observation about people, and specifically about the people in charge, and I recognise it completely, because I was one of them for fifteen years.

The number on the wall was never really doing what I thought it was doing. It was not producing the result. It was telling me that I was managing.

### 18

Winners and losers have the same goals. That line is James Clear's, and I read it years before I understood it. What winners and losers do not have in common is a method for finding out what is actually true.

I built a system that produced winners and losers, for fifteen years, and I taught it to thirty thousand companies.

I built it because it was rigorous, because it worked more than half the time, and because I was extremely good at winning inside it. That is the part I would have denied for most of my career and cannot deny now. I did not design a hero's system by accident. I designed it because I wanted to be the hero, and it took auditing my own life's work to see that.

Go back to the runners one last time.

Ten million people. The same distance, the same bodies, the same finish line. And a number each of them chose in advance — a round number, chosen for no reason except that it was round — deciding whether they crossed that line in triumph or in failure.

The number did not make them run better. There is no evidence anywhere in that data that goals made those ten million people faster.

**What the number did was make the same thing feel completely different.**

That is what an objective does. In a marathon, where the work is known and the method is your own legs, it is mostly harmless and sometimes useful. In a company, on ground where nobody knows how yet, it takes ninety days of genuine, difficult, uncertain work and stamps a verdict on it — and then it asks the person who did that work to stand at the front of a room and account for the verdict, in front of everybody they work with, four times a year.

There is a better way to run those three days.

It does not produce winners, and it does not produce losers. It produces two lists and a decision, and the person who owns the worst number in the room is very often the most useful person in it.
