## 1. The book

**Title (working):** The Business Framework
**Author:** Răzvan Căzănescu — Romanian management consultant, 20+ years, directly involved in growing 300+ companies across 23 industries. Writing in English for an international audience of founders.

**The book's argument:** most companies own *results*; the founder's job is to own the *cause* that produces them repeatedly. There is a specific, coherent system for doing that.

**The system** (a diagram the reader has already seen in Chapter 1):

```
VISION → THE MACHINE → OUTPUT → QUARTERLY AUDIT → { DISCOVERY | MULTIPLICATION } → back into VISION ↺
```

- **Vision** = a quantified description of the *default output* the machine should produce in an ordinary cycle, without extraordinary intervention. Called the **Ordinary Great Quarter**.
- **The Machine** = the validated, repeatable part of the business. Only proven things are allowed in.
- **Output** = reality reporting back. Evidence, not a target to be pushed.
- **Quarterly Audit** = compare output against the vision. Ask what is blocking the machine.
- **Two branches:** *Discovery* (find a better or new method) and *Multiplication* (more of what already works). Both are built beside the machine and validated before anything crosses back in.
- The loop closes at the **vision**, not the machine: validated *methods* go into the machine, validated *knowledge* rewrites the vision.
- **Governing rule: protect the machine.** Build beside it, validate, then merge. The machine pays for the laboratory.

## 4. Style contract — this is a Malcolm Gladwell pastiche

The model is *The Tipping Point*, and the previous five chapters follow it strictly. Match the cadence exactly.

1. **Cold open on a scene.** Named people where possible, a date, a place, physical detail. No framing, no "in this chapter."
2. **The scene contains a puzzle.** The reader must want the answer before the theory arrives.
3. **Theory flows out of story, never before it.** Never state a concept then illustrate it. Always show the reality, then name it.
4. **Numbered sections** (`### 1`, `### 2` …) separated by `---`. Each is one step of the argument.
5. **Return to earlier material later, with new meaning.** Both within the chapter and back to Chapters 1–5.
6. **The turn is short.** After 600 words of narrative, the sentence that changes everything is eight words long.
7. **First person is rare and heavy.** The author's "I" appears where it costs him something.
8. **Bold pull-quotes** on their own line for load-bearing sentences — roughly one every 700–900 words, no more.

**Voice:** direct, unhedged, occasionally blunt. American spelling. Contractions sparingly. Never corporate-motivational.

**Avoid:** listicles in the prose, headers inside sections, "Firstly/Secondly", clusters of rhetorical questions, the words "journey", "unlock", "leverage" as a verb, "game-changer".

**Chapter opening/closing format.** Begin with `# 6. ## 5. VERIFIED FACT SHEET — use only these

### The author's own quarterly meeting, as he ran it for 15+ years

Three days, in a hotel, deliberately outside the office. **The rule was that telephones stayed upstairs in the bedrooms** — not on the table, not face down. A horseshoe of tables, flipcharts.

- **Day one:** evaluate last quarter's objectives. Each manager presents theirs. Green, amber, red.
- **Day two:** reconnect with the vision — which in that model was itself a very large objective, describing what the company would achieve in ten, twenty or forty years.
- **Day three:** set the next quarter's objectives, using what was learned in the first two days.

His own verdict on it: it was an awards ceremony and an execution running back to back — *who was the hero this quarter, and who was the failure.* Managers arrived already ashamed. They spent the preceding fortnight assembling explanations rather than analysis. It was not a place where anybody could be creative, because it was not a place where anybody could be honest. **He still believes the intention was right — making evaluation inevitable every ninety days is a good structural decision. What he put inside it was wrong.**

Directly involved in growing 300+ companies across 23 industries, using objectives for growth in every one. **Invented two variations on OKRs** — Dynamic OKRs and Progressive OKRs — specifically to make the instrument work better, because he could see it failing in places and assumed the fix was a better version of the same thing. ### The 300-company analysis — his own data, the spine of the chapter

He took the highest-performing 300 organizations using his management system and measured what distinguished achieved objectives from missed ones.

- **56% of the objectives set were achieved. 44% were not.**
- **97% of the objectives that WERE achieved were ones where the method was already known and validated** — a Level 2 or Level 3 process being implemented somewhere new, or multiplied, or turned up.
- **88% of the objectives that were NOT achieved were ones where nobody knew the method** — e.g. "grow sales 50%", with no idea how.

This is not a published study. It is his own analysis of his own client base, and presenting it honestly as that makes it stronger, not weaker.

### The five studies — citations verified, use exactly as written

**Williamson et al. (2022)**, meta-analysis, *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*. Screened 17,841 studies, analysed the 27 that qualified. **Outcome goals** ("win the race" — depends on others): **d = 0.09**. **Performance goals** ("run under 10 seconds" — your own number, still a result): **d = 0.44**. **Process goals** ("hold the breathing pattern, the cadence, the body position" — entirely in your control): **d = 1.36**. Process goals also raised **self-efficacy: d = 1.11**. Studies built on self-regulation — the person monitoring their own process, adjusting, running the loop again — produced the largest effects of all: **d = 1.53**. Context: in social science d ≈ 0.8 is considered large.

**Wood, Mento & Locke (1987)**, *"Task Complexity as a Moderator of Goal Effects: A Meta-Analysis"*, **Journal of Applied Psychology**, 72, 416–425. 125 studies covering 1966–1985. Goal effects **shrink as tasks get more complex**: goal-difficulty effect sizes of about **d = 0.67 for the least complex tasks versus about d = 0.48 for the most complex**; for specific difficult goals versus "do your best", about **d = 0.41 on the most complex tasks**. The paper's own explanation: on complex tasks the effect of a goal depends on whether the person can discover an appropriate strategy, and people vary enormously in that ability.
⚠️ **Use approximate language** ("around", "roughly") for these figures. **The rhetorical point is the third author: Edwin A. Locke — the founder of goal-setting theory, with Gary Latham. This is not a critic of objectives showing they work badly on complex work. It is the man who built the field, publishing it himself in 1987.**

**Raveendran, Srikanth, Ungureanu & Zheng (2023)**, *"How Do Performance Goals Influence Exploration-Exploitation Choices?"*, **Organization Science**, 34(6), 2464–2486. People under challenging goals interpret weak early feedback as failure faster, abandon promising alternatives sooner, exploit more, explore less, and adapt slowly when the environment shifts because they have no alternatives prepared. Conclusion: that focus on exploitation is **beneficial in stable environments and harmful in unstable ones.**

**Welsh, Bush, Thiel & Bonner (2019)**, *"Reconceptualizing goal setting's dark side: The ethical consequences of learning versus outcome goals"*, **Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes**, 150, 14–27. Outcome goals trigger a **prevention focus** — a mental state organized around not failing rather than around succeeding — which increases unethical conduct aimed at preventing goal failure. **The effect gets worse as the goal gets harder.** Learning goals ("find out how to do this") reduce prevention focus and reduce unethical behaviour **without reducing performance**, even when difficult. Supported across field and laboratory studies.

**Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky & Bazerman (2009)**, *"Goals Gone Wild: The Systematic Side Effects of Overprescribing Goal Setting"*, **Academy of Management Perspectives**. Argues goals should be treated as a prescription drug, not an over-the-counter one. Six systematic harms: narrow focus (you neglect everything not in the goal); distorted risk preferences; increased unethical behaviour; **inhibited learning** (under pressure to hit a result there is no space to explore or learn how); corroded organizational culture; reduced intrinsic motivation.

### Sears Auto Centers, 1992

On **11 June 1992**, Edward Brennan, chief executive of Sears, Roebuck and Company, learned that the California Department of Consumer Affairs was moving to shut down **all 72 Sears Auto Centers in the state**, after a year-long undercover investigation found repeated instances of customers being sold repairs and services their cars did not need. New Jersey announced similar charges within days; Florida, Illinois and New York opened investigations. **Auto centre revenues fell 15%.** A former centre manager in Sacramento said the quotas **"were not based on realistic activity and were constantly escalating."**

### NASA Aviation Safety Reporting System

Established **1976**, a NASA–FAA partnership, to collect near-misses and errors from the people who commit them. **Confidential** — NASA strips every identifying detail before analysis. **Non-punitive.** Filing grants **limited immunity** from FAA enforcement, provided the report was filed promptly and the violation was not deliberate. Has collected and analysed **over 1.7 million reports** and issued **more than 5,500 safety alerts**. In nearly fifty years it has **never breached the identity of a reporter**.

### 30 Days MBA — the author's own experiment

About twelve years into his career, having been involved in hundreds of businesses, he founded TBF to give what he had learned to as many Romanian entrepreneurs as possible. Problem: he had no brand with them. Plenty of success stories, a strong reputation inside companies he had worked with directly, but nothing in education. For two years he did everything he knew worked — press, webinars, newsletters. It moved slowly.

Then one autumn: **thirty days, live, every morning, ninety minutes, 10:00 to 11:30**, teaching the actual systems. Not a taster — the whole thing.

**Almost nobody in the company believed it could work, including him.** They assumed that after three or four days it would be him and five viewers. **And they would almost certainly never have done it if it had needed to hit a sales target** — under an objective, an idea with those odds does not survive the meeting. So he proposed it as an experiment.

First run: close to **2,000 companies registered; 1,100 still attending the last session**. And although he had taught the complete systems across the thirty days, **over 600 then bought the course explaining the same material** — which made no rational sense.

He did not scale it. He said: **this is a Level 1 process. It worked once.** So they ran it again the following year, deliberately, to find out whether the first result was the method or the weather.

Second run: **8,000 companies registered, more than 5,000 watching every day.** **At that point it was Level 2, and it went into the machine.** It has now run five times; **more than 20,000 companies** have been through it.

### Other material available (use if it earns its place)

- **"You cannot fail at an experiment."** The only way to fail is to measure it badly. A properly measured experiment always succeeds, because its output is knowledge, and knowledge accumulates whether the hypothesis was right or wrong.
- **"Therapy by objectives."** When we do not like reality we do not examine the machine that produced it — we set an objective announcing that from next quarter things will be different because we decided so. *This quarter I lose the weight. This quarter I fix the marriage. This quarter we double the client base.* It changes nothing except how you feel, immediately. It is an emotional technique wearing management clothes.
- **An objective carries less responsibility than a process.** You can set a goal to lose thirty kilograms in three months while knowing nothing about how weight is lost, and feel proud of the setting of it. A process is entirely within your power, which is exactly why we avoid it — there is nowhere for the explanation to go.
- **"I know what to do but I can't stick to it"** means you do **not** know what to do. You have a system you cannot apply, which equals not knowing how to make it work. You have not run enough experiments to find something that works for *you*. **You cannot claim to have found the solution to something you cannot implement.**
- **Forecast, not dreams.** Instead of setting a goal, forecast your own system: where does this land if nothing changes? That is the honest starting point. Then find the causes in the process blocking the future you want. If you know how to fix them, change the process. If not, experiment.
- **Two mirror pathologies.** The **large organization** has a machine and is afraid to touch it — getting it wrong can cost billions — so it sets objectives instead, people reach for what is already in the machine, nobody explores, and **learning dies**. Its experimentation migrates outside into corporate venture arms and acquired startups, which is the right instinct and an expensive way to buy what it could have built. The **startup** has no machine but takes its assumptions as validated and declares processes Level 2 without ever running them at Level 1, because the culture says move fast. **The resolution: move fast in experimentation and validation. Do not move fast in putting things into the machine — that is where the damage is.**
- **Who is in the audit room.** Top management, yes — but also **whoever can answer the question with data, regardless of title.** If the bottleneck is in lead generation, is the person who actually looks at the lead data in the room? Otherwise experiments and multiplications get chosen from emotion.
- **The second source of experiments.** Alongside those that come from bottlenecks, always reserve a few from a different question: **what would be genuinely interesting to know? What idea would we bet against, but would really like to find out whether there is something in it?** 30 Days MBA came from exactly that question.

## 6. The audit method — the logic that must come through clearly

The previous draft got this right in substance and wrong in delivery: it was a wall of prose and readers wanted to skip. The worksheets in §7 exist to fix that.

1. **Compare the current output with the vision** — not with last quarter, not with a target. With the Ordinary Great Quarter. That comparison produces **gaps**.
2. **For each gap, ask what is blocking the machine.** The answers come from the indicators of the Level 2 and Level 3 processes inside the machine. **This is why it matters that only validated processes are in there** — otherwise the meeting is a war of opinions and egos, won by whoever argues best. Output: a list of **bottlenecks**.
3. **Route each bottleneck.** Can it be removed by **multiplying** something that already works, or does it need **discovery** of something not yet known? Look at both the machine's processes and the data from experiments already completed.
4. **If multiplication — check the level first.** You may only multiply Level 2 and above. **If the process is only Level 1, next quarter's job is not multiplication. It is moving that process from Level 1 to Level 2, in the discovery branch.** Protect the machine.
5. **If nothing in the machine solves it** — you do not know how, and no amount of commitment changes that. So the quarter's work is to formulate hypotheses (Level 0), build proper experiments, and find out. The output of that quarter is knowledge.

Subtitle: *"For each GAP: name the bottleneck, the processes behind it, and mark the solution path."*

This is the heart of the chapter. ### Worksheet 3 — EXPERIMENT

Header: **Experiment name · Bottleneck it solves**

And the **Source** field quietly records where the idea came from, which is how a company learns whether its instincts are any good.
