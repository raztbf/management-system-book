## 12. Six Times, and Then Never Again

**Unde intră:** "Oamenii au avut ca țintă să ajungă pe lună... au reușit o singură dată." · "A one-time victory may prove that something is possible. A desired default output requires the company to own the repeatable cause."
**Status:** REALĂ

On 25 May 1961, John F. Kennedy stood in front of Congress and said that the United States should commit itself, before the decade was out, to landing a man on the moon and returning him safely to the earth.

It is probably the most famous vision statement ever delivered. It had everything the textbooks tell you a vision should have: a specific outcome, a hard deadline, emotional force, and a scale that made grown engineers cry. It also worked. Eight years and two months later, Neil Armstrong stepped off a ladder, and something like six hundred million people watched him do it.

Between 1969 and 1972, twelve human beings walked on the surface of the moon.

Then nobody did, for over half a century.

Think about what that actually means. The United States built, from nothing, in under a decade, the most complex machine in human history. It solved problems that had no precedent — navigation, life support, rendezvous in lunar orbit, re-entry, the largest rocket ever flown. And then it stopped, and it could not do it again. Apollo 18, 19 and 20 were cancelled. The Saturn V production line was shut down. The tooling was scrapped. The engineers dispersed. By the 1990s NASA could not have built another Saturn V if you had handed it the money, because the knowledge had not been stored in an organization — it had been stored in a program, and the program ended.

The vision had been a moment. And the moment was achieved.

Now hold that against what happened to a different organization with a different definition of victory.

SpaceX did not set out to reach orbit. Reaching orbit had been done thousands of times; it was, by the 2000s, an entirely solved problem. What SpaceX set out to do was to make reaching orbit *repeatable and cheap*, which meant not throwing the rocket away.

That goal is, emotionally, almost worthless. Nobody weeps at the idea of a reusable first stage. You cannot make a Kennedy speech out of amortization. And for years it looked ridiculous — boosters tipping over on barges, exploding on landing, missing the deck. Those failures were mocked, endlessly, because the thing being attempted did not look like a victory. It looked like an accounting exercise with fireworks.

In December 2015, a Falcon 9 first stage landed upright for the first time. Then it happened again. Then it became routine, then boring, then invisible. In 2020, SpaceX flew 25 orbital launches. In 2021, 31. In 2022, 61. In 2023, 96. In 2024, 134 — more than half of all orbital launches on the planet, by anyone. In 2025, 165. A single booster has flown more than twenty times.

Apollo produced a moment that a species will remember forever and could not repeat. SpaceX produced a capability so routine that most people no longer notice a rocket launch.

Ask yourself which of those two organizations would be more capable of putting a permanent base on the moon.

If, in 1961, the goal had been *build a machine that can put people on the moon four times a year*, the timeline would have been longer, the speech would have been worse, and the political appetite might have collapsed before the first landing. It is also entirely possible that we would have had a lunar base for the last forty years.

**Cotitura:** A vision built from a moment is complete the day the moment happens — and everything that produced it becomes overhead. A vision built from a default output is never complete, and everything that produced it becomes an asset.

## 13. Magnificent Desolation

**Unde intră:** "Both targets and victories move satisfaction into the future." · "Amânăm fericirea constant mințindu-ne că viața va fi bună atunci când se va întâmpla X."
**Status:** REALĂ

Buzz Aldrin was thirty-nine years old when he walked on the moon.

He had a doctorate from MIT in orbital rendezvous — his thesis is a reason Apollo worked at all. He had flown sixty-six combat missions in Korea. He was, by any reasonable measure, one of the most capable human beings alive, and on 20 July 1969 he became the second person in the history of the species to stand on another world.

Standing there, looking at the lunar surface, he said two words that have been quoted ever since as poetry: *magnificent desolation.*

What is less often quoted is what he wrote later about what those words came to mean.

He came home to the largest welcome any human being has ever received. Parades. Presidents. A world tour. And everywhere he went, for months, people asked him the same question, which is the only question anyone could think to ask: *what was it like on the moon?*

He had no idea how to answer it. And underneath the question was a second one that nobody asked out loud but that he could hear anyway: *what are you going to do now?*

He was thirty-nine. The single most extraordinary thing that would ever happen to him had already happened. Every remaining decade of his life was going to be lived on the far side of it.

In his 1973 memoir *Return to Earth*, Aldrin did something almost unthinkable for an American national hero in that era: he described the depression and the drinking honestly. Days when he did not get out of bed. A marriage he damaged. A career at NASA that no longer had a shape, because the thing it had been shaped around was finished. He checked into psychiatric care. He stopped drinking in 1978, nine years after the moon, and stayed sober for the rest of a very long life.

He was clear, later, that the moon did not cause his depression — there was family history, there was alcoholism in his line. But he was equally clear about what the structure of his life had done to him. He had spent his entire adult existence pointed at a single event. The event happened. And the machinery of his life had been built to arrive somewhere, not to live anywhere.

That phrase, *magnificent desolation*, turned out to be a description of a state of mind and not just a landscape.

Now here is the part that should make you uncomfortable, because it is not really a story about astronauts.

Almost every ambitious person you know has organized their life exactly the way Aldrin organized his, at a smaller scale. The exit. The first million. The house. The book. The weight. The moment when the thing is finally true and you get to stop.

And the structural problem is identical, whether the summit is the moon or a number in a bank account: you spend 99.99% of your life on the way to it, and a vanishingly small amount of time standing on it, and the standing does not feel the way you imagined, and then it becomes normal, and then you need another one.

Twelve men walked on the moon. Read what happened to them afterwards. One became a painter. One found God and started a healing ministry. One became a used-car salesman for a while. Several had their marriages end. Almost all of them describe some version of the same problem: what do you do on Tuesday, when the summit is behind you?

**Cotitura:** If your vision is a moment, then the best possible outcome of your life is that you achieve it — and are then required to live for decades in the ruins of your own ambition.

## 14. Winning Changes Nothing

**Unde intră:** "Dissatisfaction-ul apare natural chiar dacă am realizat obiectivul."
**Status:** REALĂ

In July 1992, Andre Agassi won Wimbledon.

It is impossible to overstate what that meant at that point in his life. He was twenty-two. He had lost three Grand Slam finals. He had been dismissed by the entire tennis establishment as an image with a forehand — the hair, the denim shorts, the Canon commercial where he said *image is everything*, which followed him like a conviction. Wimbledon was the tournament that was supposed to be least suited to him, on the surface that was supposed to expose him, in the sport's most conservative cathedral.

He beat Goran Ivanišević in five sets. He fell to his knees on the grass and wept.

Years later, in his memoir *Open*, he wrote about what happened in the hours after. He described it as being let in on a dirty little secret: winning changes nothing.

The sentence people usually quote from that passage is the one that follows, and it is the most quietly devastating thing an athlete has ever written about achievement. *A win doesn't feel as good as a loss feels bad, and the good feeling doesn't last as long as the bad. Not even close.*

He is not describing a bad day. He is describing an asymmetry that is structural, and that anybody who has ever chased a target will recognize the moment they stop lying to themselves about it. The victory gives you a few hours of relief and then dissolves into the new baseline. The defeat gives you months.

Later, describing the same win, he said that at that point in his career Wimbledon felt more like relief than like celebration — and he was careful to say that this was the unfortunate part, not the noble part.

*Open* famously begins with Agassi saying he hates tennis, has hated it his whole life, with a dark and secret passion. The book is not about a man who fell out of love with a sport. It is about a man whose entire life was organized by his father around a series of victories, who won almost all of them, and who discovered that the structure itself was the thing hurting him.

Here is what makes Agassi's story genuinely useful rather than just sad.

He did not quit. What changed, late in his career, was what he was pointed at. He got married, built a life he actually wanted to live inside, and founded a school in Las Vegas for children from underserved families — a school that has now been operating for over two decades and has produced graduating class after graduating class. It is not a victory. It is a machine that produces educated children, every year, on a cadence, whether or not Andre Agassi is having a good week.

And by every account, including his own, the second structure did something for him that eight Grand Slam titles could not.

**Cotitura:** Every founder chasing the exit is running Agassi's experiment. He already ran it, at the highest level available to a human being, and published the result: the win does not do what you think it will do.

**Surse:** Andre Agassi, *Open: An Autobiography* (2009) · [Agassi on his 1992 Wimbledon win — ATP Tour](https://www.atptour.com/en/news/agassi-1992-wimbledon-atp-heritage)

## 15. Twenty-Two Lottery Winners

**Unde intră:** "După câteva luni de la realizare se transformă în normal și nu mai au același efect asupra vieții noastre."
**Status:** REALĂ

In 1978, three psychologists — Philip Brickman, Dan Coates, and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman — published a study with a title that reads like a provocation: *Lottery Winners and Accident Victims: Is Happiness Relative?*

They found twenty-two people who had won major prizes in the Illinois State Lottery, ranging from fifty thousand dollars to a million — serious money in 1978. They found twenty-nine people who had been paralyzed in accidents within roughly the last year, most of them permanently. And they found a control group of ordinary people who had neither won nor lost anything remarkable.

Then they asked all three groups the same questions. How happy are you now? How happy were you before? How happy do you expect to be in a couple of years? And, crucially, how much pleasure do you take in a list of ordinary daily things — talking with a friend, watching television, eating breakfast, hearing a funny joke, getting a compliment?

The headline finding is the one everybody remembers: the lottery winners were not significantly happier than the control group in their general ratings of present happiness. Something that every human being on earth believes will transform their life had, within a year, largely failed to.

But the second finding is the one that belongs in this chapter, and it is stranger.

The lottery winners rated the ordinary daily pleasures as *less* enjoyable than the control group did. Not the same. Less. Talking to a friend, reading a magazine, having breakfast — all of it had lost some of its flavour.

The researchers offered an explanation that has held up reasonably well since: contrast. Once you have experienced an enormous positive event, everything ordinary is now being measured against it. The peak does not just fail to raise the baseline. It can lower the value of everything below the peak.

And there is a third finding that gets left out of most retellings, which matters for honesty. The accident victims were not as unhappy as anyone predicted. They rated their present happiness lower than the controls, yes — but far, far higher than people imagine when asked to guess. They also rated their *past* as happier, in a way that suggests they were doing something the lottery winners were not: they were reconstructing a life, rather than waiting inside one.

The study has been argued about for forty-five years — the sample is small, the design is cross-sectional, later work has complicated the picture. What has survived all of it is the core observation, now boringly well established under the name hedonic adaptation: human beings absorb changes in their circumstances and return, mostly, to something near where they were.

Which means the entire architecture of the goal-based life is fighting a documented property of the human nervous system.

You are not lazy for failing to stay happy after the victory. You are a normally functioning animal. The problem is not you. The problem is that you have built your life around events, and events wash out.

**Cotitura:** The peak adapts. The pattern does not. This is the whole argument for defining vision as a default rather than a moment — not because moments are bad, but because your nervous system will delete them.

**Surse:** Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman, "Lottery winners and accident victims: Is happiness relative?", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 36(8), 1978

## 16. The Chef Who Could Not Survive a Rumour

**Unde intră:** "Sindromul eroului" · "Vision as identity" · costul emoțional al victoriei ca sursă de valoare personală
**Status:** REALĂ

Bernard Loiseau was one of the great chefs of France, and by 2003 he had achieved essentially everything a French chef can achieve.

He had three Michelin stars at his restaurant in Saulieu, in Burgundy, which he had built from a provincial roadside inn into a destination. He had held a peak score of 19.5 out of 20 from the rival guide GaultMillau — a score almost nobody gets. He had listed his company on the stock exchange, the first French chef ever to do so. He had a television presence. He had, in the most literal sense, won.

He had also spent his entire adult life understanding himself as a man who had won, which is a different and far more fragile thing.

In early 2003, two blows landed in quick succession. GaultMillau dropped his score from 19 to 17. And François Simon, the critic at *Le Figaro*, wrote a review suggesting his cooking had slipped. Neither of these was Michelin. His three stars were intact. But a rumour began circulating in the French food world that Michelin had privately warned him he was in danger of losing the third.

There is no evidence Michelin ever did. When the 2003 Guide Rouge was published, Loiseau still had his three stars.

It was published on the day of his funeral.

On 24 February 2003, Bernard Loiseau went home in the afternoon and shot himself. He left no explanation. He was fifty-two.

Paul Bocuse, the most famous chef in France and Loiseau's friend, publicly blamed GaultMillau for his death. Another three-star chef, Jacques Lameloise, said that Loiseau had told him not long before that he would not hesitate to kill himself if he lost a star.

I want to be careful here, because a suicide has causes that no outsider gets to summarize. Loiseau had a documented history of depression. His widow, Dominique, has spoken about it since. It would be obscene to reduce a man's death to a management lesson.

But the reason chefs across France recognized something in that story is that they were all living inside the same structure, and they knew it.

Here is the structure. A Michelin star is not a description of what your kitchen can reliably produce. It is a verdict, delivered annually, by anonymous strangers, on whether you are still who you were last year. It cannot be earned permanently. It can only be defended. And once your identity is fused to it, every service of every day for the rest of your career carries the possibility of losing the thing that makes you a person.

Loiseau had built one of the great restaurants in Europe. On the day he died, the machine was working. The food was excellent. The guests were coming. The stars were, in fact, still there.

None of that was load-bearing, because his sense of his own value had never been attached to the machine. It had been attached to the verdict.

**Cotitura:** When your significance comes from the victory rather than from what the machine produces, you do not lose your significance when the machine fails. You lose it when somebody says you might.

## 17. The Chef Who Gave the Stars Back

**Unde intră:** "O viziune nu trebuie să fie worldchanging." · "Wisdom is wanting what's worth getting in the first place." · "Can the business default be produced without destroying the life default?"
**Status:** REALĂ

Fourteen years after Loiseau's death, another French chef looked at exactly the same structure and did something nobody in the history of French cooking had done.

Sébastien Bras runs Le Suquet, in Laguiole, high in the Aubrac in southern France. It is not a restaurant you pass by. You drive up into the hills to get there. His father, Michel Bras, built it, and it had held three Michelin stars for eighteen years — a run that puts it among the most consistently excellent restaurants on earth.

In September 2017, Bras made a video and asked Michelin to remove Le Suquet from the guide.

He was not in trouble. He had not lost anything. He was fifty, at the height of his powers, running one of the great restaurants in France. He simply did not want to keep paying the price.

What he described was not vanity or exhaustion in the ordinary sense. It was something more precise. Michelin inspectors arrive unannounced, two or three times a year. Which means that every plate that leaves the kitchen might be the plate being judged. Five hundred meals go out. Any one of them could be the one that costs you the thing your family has held for eighteen years.

*You're inspected two or three times a year, you never know when,* he said. *Every meal that goes out could be inspected. That means that every day one of the five hundred meals that leaves the kitchen could be judged.*

And then the line that matters: *I will be able to feel free.*

Michelin, to its credit, eventually agreed. Le Suquet did not appear in the 2018 guide. It was the first time in the guide's history that a restaurant had been allowed to withdraw.

There is a postscript that is almost too perfect. In 2019, Michelin put Le Suquet back in — two stars, unrequested. Bras had not asked to return. The guide simply resumed judging him. Which tells you something about how much of this game you actually get to opt out of.

But here is what Bras had understood, and it is the exact question this chapter is asking.

He had a business default and a life default, and the two were no longer compatible. The business default — hold three stars indefinitely — required a level of permanent tension that was consuming the life default he actually wanted, which was to cook in the mountains where he grew up, for people who drove up to find him, without a knot in his stomach every service.

Most people in that position change the life. Bras changed the business.

He did not close. He did not lower his standards — by every account the food did not get worse. He removed a constraint that was not his, that had been imposed by an external verdict, and that had nothing to do with what his machine was actually built to produce.

Almost everyone reading this has, somewhere in their business, a Michelin star: a metric they never chose, imposed by an audience they never selected, that they are now organizing their life around defending.

**Cotitura:** A vision becomes useful when it lets you reject something attractive. Bras rejected three Michelin stars. What have you rejected lately?

## 18. Five Thousand to One

**Unde intră:** "Nu este despre a realiza o victorie one-shot. Este despre a construi mașinăria care generează acea victorie repetabil și predictibil."
**Status:** REALĂ

At the start of the 2015–16 season, the bookmakers offered odds of 5000-1 on Leicester City winning the English Premier League.

To understand how absurd that number is: the same bookmakers offered shorter odds on Elvis Presley being found alive. Leicester had finished the previous season fourteenth, and had spent most of it bottom of the table. They had been in the third tier of English football seven years earlier. Their squad had cost a fraction of what their rivals spent on a single player.

They won it.

It is, by consensus, the greatest single achievement in the history of English club football, and probably in the history of any major league sport. Grown men who had supported Leicester for fifty years wept in the street. Their striker, Jamie Vardy, had been playing non-league football and working in a factory making splints five years earlier. Their manager, Claudio Ranieri, had never won a league title in his life.

Now here is what happened next.

Ranieri was sacked nine months later. The core of the team was sold. Leicester spent the following seasons oscillating — a very good FA Cup win in 2021, some strong league finishes, and mounting losses. Then in 2023 they were relegated from the Premier League, seven years after winning it. They came straight back up, and were relegated again in 2025. In 2026 they went down again, to League One — the third tier of English football, where they had been before the fairytale began.

Ten years from champions of England to the third division.

The temptation is to read this as a tragedy or as bad luck, and there was certainly bad luck: the pandemic destroyed the revenue of King Power, the owners' duty-free business, and the money stopped. But that explanation is itself the point.

Leicester's title was a victory. It was not a capability.

What Leicester had, in 2015-16, was an extraordinary alignment of temporary conditions: a squad of undervalued players all hitting career-best form simultaneously, an unusually low injury count, a season in which every large club underperformed at once, and a manager whose approach happened to suit that exact group. Every single one of those things was true once.

Contrast that with what a machine looks like in the same industry.

Brighton and Hove Albion, and before them Brentford, were built by owners who came out of professional gambling — Tony Bloom and Matthew Benham — and who therefore had a professional's contempt for the one-off. They did not build a squad. They built a system for identifying undervalued players, developing them, selling them at a large profit, and replacing them with the next one from the same pipeline. Brighton has repeatedly sold its best players for enormous fees and finished higher the following season. That is not luck. That is a machine, and it produces its output whether or not any individual player stays.

Neither Brighton nor Brentford has ever won the Premier League. Both will still be in it, in some form, long after the Leicester fairytale has become a documentary.

**Cotitura:** A victory proves something was possible once. A machine proves you own the cause. Leicester owned a season. Brighton owns a method.

## 19. Fourteen Percent

**Unde intră:** "Constraint-urile sunt expresia operațională a valorilor." · "Toată lumea vorbește despre importanța valorilor, dar extrem de puțini își bat capul să transforme acele valori în unelte manageriale."
**Status:** REALĂ

Every company in the world says it puts customers first. It is on the wall. It is in the induction deck. It is the second bullet on the values slide, right after integrity.

Costco does something different, and the difference is not a slogan. It is a number.

Costco caps its markup on branded goods at fourteen percent. On its own Kirkland Signature products, fifteen. Not as a target. As a ceiling. If a buyer negotiates a spectacular deal on a product, the saving is not converted into margin — it is converted into a lower shelf price, because the ceiling does not move.

Ordinary retail markups run from twenty-five to fifty percent and higher. Costco's actual average across the store, by its own reporting, sits around eleven.

Now, the rule itself is not in any SEC filing. It is internal. Which makes it more interesting, not less, because it means nobody outside forces them to keep it. It has been kept, without wavering, since Jim Sinegal ran the company.

There is a story that circulates about Sinegal and a supplier who brought him a deal that would have let Costco keep an extra few points of margin without raising the shelf price — free money. He refused. His stated reasoning was that if you let yourself do it once, you will do it again, and the number will creep, and in ten years you will be an ordinary retailer with a nice story about your founder.

He described the discipline in terms of addiction. Raise the price three cents because you can, and you will need the next three cents.

Watch what this single constraint actually does to the architecture of the business.

Because Costco cannot make money on markup, it has to make money somewhere else — so the profit engine becomes the membership fee, which means the entire company is now structurally required to make members feel that renewing is obviously correct. The renewal rate runs above ninety percent.

Because margin per item is capped, the only way to grow gross profit is volume — so the company must carry a small number of items in enormous quantities. Costco carries something like four thousand SKUs where a supermarket carries fifty thousand.

Because it needs volume on a few items, it must be relentless with suppliers, and suppliers accept it because the order sizes are absurd.

Because shelf price is protected, the cost pressure lands on operations — concrete floors, pallets, no bags, no advertising budget.

One constraint, and it has shaped the product range, the profit model, the supplier relationship, the store design, and the marketing strategy of a company with a quarter of a million employees.

That is what a value looks like when it has been converted into a management instrument.

Compare it with the alternative. A company writes *we deliver exceptional value to our customers* on a poster. Nothing follows. No decision is ever harder because of it. No attractive opportunity is ever rejected because of it. Nobody in the company can tell you what would have to be true for the sentence to be false.

**Cotitura:** A value you cannot audit is a slogan. A value becomes real on the day it costs you money you were allowed to keep.

## 20. Fourteen Hundred Years, and Then Fifteen

**Unde intră:** "Probabil cel mai valoros și în același timp cel mai puțin utilizat cuvânt în antreprenoriatul mondial este: suficient." · "Asta face cancerul... creștere până la sinucidere."
**Status:** REALĂ

In the year 578, a Korean carpenter named Shigemitsu Kongō was invited to Japan to help build the country's first Buddhist temple, Shitennō-ji in what is now Osaka.

The company he founded to do that work, Kongō Gumi, was still operating in 2005.

Fourteen hundred and twenty-eight years. Forty generations of the same family. It survived the rise and fall of the samurai class, the Mongol invasions, the closing of Japan to the outside world, the reopening of Japan to the outside world, the Meiji Restoration, two world wars, the firebombing of Osaka, the American occupation, and the near-total destruction of Japanese Buddhism as a state institution in the nineteenth century.

The way it survived is a case study in itself. Kongō Gumi did one thing: it built and repaired Buddhist temples, using traditional joinery, at extremely high quality. When temple construction collapsed during the war, they made coffins. When the family had no capable son, they adopted one, or the leadership passed to a son-in-law who took the family name — a Japanese practice that let the business select for competence while keeping the family structure. Their internal guidance to successive generations reads, in translation, almost like a constraints list: stay in your trade, do not drink to excess, treat people fairly, do not take on more work than you can do well, and never guarantee a debt for someone else.

For fourteen centuries, that machine produced its default output. Not spectacular growth. Temples, well built, repeatedly, at a cadence, in a market that renewed itself every few decades because wooden temples need rebuilding.

Then, in the 1980s, Japan had a property bubble.

Kongō Gumi borrowed heavily and moved into real estate. Everybody was doing it. The returns available in Osaka land in 1988 made careful temple carpentry look like a hobby for the unambitious. It was, by the standards of the moment, obviously the right decision — a fourteen-hundred-year-old company finally scaling.

The bubble burst in 1992. The assets securing the debt collapsed in value. The debt did not. By 2006 the company owed something on the order of three hundred and forty-three million dollars, could not service it, and was liquidated. Its assets were bought by Takamatsu Construction, and Kongō Gumi survives today only as a subsidiary brand.

Fourteen hundred years of enough, ended by roughly fifteen years of more.

The seduction here is worth naming precisely, because it is the same seduction operating in every business that has found something that works. The temple business was not failing. It was producing exactly what it had always produced. The problem was that in 1988 the temple business looked *insufficient* — not against its own history, and not against the needs of the people it served, but against what other people were making that year.

Nobody at Kongō Gumi asked whether the machine was working. They asked whether the machine was growing.

**Cotitura:** The question is not whether you could be bigger. The question is whether the output your machine produces is enough for the life you actually want — and if it is, growth is a decision, not an obligation.

## 21. The Company That Mailed DVDs

**Unde intră:** răspuns la obiecția "dar asta nu te va împinge să mergi în zig zag și să nu ai o direcție consistentă?" · "Poți să mergi din aproape în aproape."
**Status:** REALĂ

The objection to defining vision as a default output rather than a distant summit is always the same, and it is a fair objection. If you only describe what the machine should produce at its next level, aren't you condemning yourself to wandering? Don't the great companies have a north star?

Look at the two most cited north stars in modern business.

In 1997, Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph launched a company whose product was this: you go to a website, choose a DVD, and it arrives in your letterbox in a red envelope. You mail it back. There was no subscription at first — it was rental by the disc, with due dates.

There was no vision about transforming global entertainment. There was a vision about whether a DVD would survive the American postal system, which Randolph and Hastings tested by mailing a CD to themselves in a greetings card before they built anything.

The subscription model came later. The removal of due dates came later. The recommendation algorithm came later. Streaming — the thing the company is now — did not launch until 2007, ten years in, and even then it was a free add-on to the DVD subscription because nobody believed people would give up the discs. Original production came later still. Netflix's first original series aired in 2012, fifteen years after founding.

Fifteen years of the machine producing its default output — deliver a physical disc, reliably, cheaply, faster than a video store — funding the discovery of what the machine should become next.

Now the second one. In 2007, two designers in San Francisco, Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia, could not make rent. There was a large industrial design conference in town and every hotel in the city was full. They had three air mattresses.

They built a one-page website called airbedandbreakfast.com, aimed at exactly one market: attendees of large conferences in cities with insufficient hotel rooms. That was the vision. Not *belong anywhere.* Conference overflow accommodation.

Three people stayed. They made about a thousand dollars. Then they went to the 2008 Democratic National Convention in Denver, where eighty thousand people were arriving into a city with far fewer hotel beds, because conferences were still the model. They funded that year by selling novelty breakfast cereal boxes — Obama O's and Cap'n McCain's — which is not a detail you will find on a vision slide.

It took years to work out that the actual machine was not about conferences at all.

Both of these companies are now used as examples of visionary founders who saw the future. What they actually did was define, at each stage, what their machine should be able to produce by default at that stage, build it until it produced that reliably, and then let contact with reality tell them what the next default should be.

The direction was consistent. What changed was the description of the next normal.

That is not zig-zagging. Zig-zagging is what happens when you have no defined default at all, so every attractive opportunity looks equally valid and nothing ever becomes reliable enough to fund the next question.

**Cotitura:** You do not need to see the summit. You need to see the next level of default output clearly enough to know when you have reached it — because that is the only moment at which you have earned the right to redefine it.
