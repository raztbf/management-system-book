## 33. Eight Is Great

**Unde intră:** The Output · "People optimize what the system rewards. If the metric is a poor proxy for the real purpose, they can improve the number while damaging the business."
**Status:** REALĂ

Dick Kovacevich, who ran Wells Fargo for most of a decade, had a theory about banking that was, on its own terms, entirely correct.

A bank makes money not from acquiring customers but from deepening them. A customer with one checking account is barely profitable. A customer with a checking account, a savings account, a credit card, a mortgage, an auto loan, an overdraft line, and an investment account is enormously profitable, and — this is the key part — almost impossible to poach, because moving all of that to a competitor is unbearable.

He gave this a name. He called the products "solutions," he called branches "stores," and he set a number. Eight products per household.

He even gave it a slogan, which tells you how proud the organization was of it: *Eight is great.*

For years, Wells Fargo's cross-sell ratio was the most closely watched number in American retail banking. It appeared in the annual report. Analysts asked about it on earnings calls. It went up, year after year, and Wells Fargo was regarded as the best-run large bank in the United States. It came through the 2008 financial crisis in better shape than any of its peers.

The number was real. It was measured accurately. It was reported honestly.

It was also, by the end, substantially fictional in the only sense that mattered, and the mechanism by which it became fictional is the whole lesson.

The number was pushed down. Regional targets, branch targets, daily targets, hourly targets. Employees were reported to have had to phone in their numbers several times a day. Managers who missed were replaced. Bonuses, promotion, and continued employment ran through it.

And a bank employee who is short by two products at four in the afternoon has a choice. They can find two more customers who genuinely need two more products, in ninety minutes, or they can open an account.

So they opened accounts. They opened savings accounts for customers who had not asked and moved a few dollars in from checking. They issued credit cards nobody applied for. They created email addresses to enrol customers in online banking. Some accounts generated fees, and customers who had not asked for the product paid the fee.

Forensic review after the fact identified up to around 3.5 million accounts that may have been unauthorized. Wells Fargo fired approximately 5,300 employees over the practice. In September 2016 it was fined $185 million, its chief executive resigned, clawbacks followed, the stock fell, and the reputation the bank had spent a century building was gone in about six weeks.

In January 2017, Wells Fargo announced that it would stop reporting the cross-sell number entirely.

Now, the comfortable interpretation is that 5,300 people were dishonest. Wells Fargo initially reached for that interpretation itself.

But 5,300 people do not independently invent the same fraud. What happened is what happens whenever a proxy is promoted to a purpose. The purpose was *customers who value more of our services*. The proxy was *number of open accounts per household*. Those two things overlap heavily in ordinary conditions — which is precisely why the proxy looked so good for so long — and they come apart completely under pressure.

And the organization could not see it, because the instrument it was using to see was the same instrument that was being gamed.

**Cotitura:** Every metric is a proxy for something you actually want. Ask, for each one: what is the cheapest way for a person under pressure to move this number without producing the thing it stands for? Then assume someone will find it, because someone always does.

## 34. The Rat Farms of Hanoi

**Unde intră:** The Output · măsurarea greșită a rezultatului · "They can improve the number while damaging the business."
**Status:** REALĂ

In 1902, the French colonial administration in Hanoi had a problem with its sewers, and the problem was that the sewers were excellent.

Hanoi was the showpiece of French Indochina, and the European quarter had just been fitted with a modern underground sewer system — nine miles of it, the pride of the colonial engineers, physical proof of the *mission civilisatrice*. Clean, dark, damp, temperature-stable tunnels running under the entire white district.

Which is to say: the finest rat habitat ever constructed in Southeast Asia, with direct access to every French kitchen and bathroom in the city.

Rats came up through the toilets. Bubonic plague was moving through Asia. The administration hired Vietnamese sewer workers to go down and kill rats, paying a wage, and the men — doing genuinely appalling work — went on strike.

So the administration switched to the mechanism that every management textbook would recommend. Stop paying for time. Start paying for results. A bounty, per rat killed.

The numbers were spectacular. Rats came in by the thousands. On busy days, seven to fourteen thousand. On the record day, over twenty thousand. The colonial government was, on paper, winning the war.

The logistics of collecting and disposing of twenty thousand rat carcasses a day at a municipal office, in the heat, were themselves horrific. So the administration made a sensible efficiency improvement: don't bring us the rat. Bring us the tail. The tail is proof of a dead rat, and it fits in a sack.

You already know what happened.

Within weeks, French officials began noticing live rats in Hanoi with no tails, running around the city perfectly healthy.

An inspection of the outskirts found rat farms. Enterprising Vietnamese were breeding rats specifically to harvest tails. Others were catching rats, docking them, and releasing them to breed more taxable tails. A smuggling network developed, importing rodents into Hanoi from across Tonkin — that is, people were bringing *more rats into the plague city* because rats had become a cash crop.

The bounty was cancelled. In 1906, plague arrived in Hanoi anyway.

The historian Michael Vann found the whole affair in a folder in the French overseas archives labelled *Destruction of Hazardous Animals: Rats*, and published it in 2003.

Here is why this is not just a funny story about colonial incompetence.

Every step the administration took was defensible and, in isolation, smart. Paying for output rather than time is correct. Requiring physical proof of the result is correct. Reducing the administrative burden of collecting proof is correct.

What they never did was ask what the *purpose* was. The purpose was fewer rats in Hanoi. The measure was tails delivered. And the fastest way to deliver more tails is not to kill more rats — it is to produce more tails, which requires *more rats.* The measure was not merely imperfect. It was, at scale, inversely related to the goal.

**Cotitura:** When you pay for a measure, you get the measure. If you are lucky, the measure and the purpose stay pointed roughly the same way. If you are not, your incentive quietly starts funding the thing you are trying to eliminate.

**Surse:** [Michael G. Vann, "Of Rats, Rice, and Race: The Great Hanoi Rat Massacre," *French Colonial History*, 2003](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/42110/summary) · [The Great Hanoi Rat Hunt: A Conversation with Michael G. Vann — *Made in China Journal*](https://madeinchinajournal.com/2020/08/20/the-great-hanoi-rat-hunt/)

## 35. The Chalk Circle

**Unde intră:** The Audit · "During the audit, the current output is compared with the desired default described by the vision."
**Status:** REALĂ

Taiichi Ohno spent most of his career at Toyota building what became the Toyota Production System, and he had a training method that most Western managers find close to abusive.

He would take a young engineer or a new manager out onto the factory floor, pick a spot with a clear view of a work area, and draw a circle on the concrete in chalk.

Then he would tell them to stand in it and watch.

Not to do anything. Not to talk to anyone. Not to take a laptop, because there were no laptops, and had there been he would have taken it away. Just stand in the circle and observe the process in front of them.

Then he would leave.

He would come back hours later — the stories vary between four and eight hours, and Ohno himself was vague about it, which is very much in character — and he would ask a single question. *What did you see?*

And the first answer was always wrong. The engineer would report what they had been trained to report: the line is running, the operators are working, output is roughly on target, there's a small issue with the third station. Ohno would say *look again*, and walk away.

The point of the circle was not endurance. The point was that the first hour of observation shows you what you already believe. You watch the process and you see the process as it exists in your head, because your head has a model and the model is what your eyes are looking for.

Somewhere in the third or fourth hour, if you are honest and you have nothing to do but look, the model breaks. You notice that the operator walks four steps to get a part that could be at his elbow, and does it eleven hundred times a shift. You notice that there is a small stack of components at the second station that is always there, which means it is never zero, which means something upstream is producing faster than something downstream can consume, which is a bottleneck that exists in the physical world and appears nowhere in any report. You notice the operator's little private workaround — the jig he made himself out of tape — which is either an improvement nobody has captured or a symptom nobody has fixed.

None of that is in the data. All of it is in the room.

Ohno's principle, which Toyota still teaches, is *genchi genbutsu* — go and see for yourself, at the actual place, with the actual thing. Not the report about the place. The place.

Now think about what a quarterly audit usually is.

It is a meeting. In the meeting are numbers, and the numbers were produced by people who knew they would be in the meeting. Everyone in the room discusses a representation of the business, and the quality of the discussion is bounded by the quality of the representation, and nobody in the room has stood anywhere for four hours.

An audit that only reads reports can tell you *that* the output has drifted from the desired default. It very rarely tells you *why*, because the why is almost always a physical fact about how the work actually happens — and physical facts do not survive the trip into a spreadsheet.

**Cotitura:** Your dashboard tells you the machine's output has moved. Only the floor tells you what the machine is actually doing. An audit without a chalk circle in it is a conversation about a picture.

**Surse:** Taiichi Ohno, *Toyota Production System: Beyond Large-Scale Production* (1978) · Jeffrey Liker, *The Toyota Way* · [Lean Enterprise Institute — genchi genbutsu / the Ohno Circle](https://www.lean.org/)

## 36. A Minor Invention Every Ten Days

**Unde intră:** Discovery · "Un sistem care folosește capacitatea eroică în descoperire și explorare." · Managing the Known VS the Unknown
**Status:** REALĂ

In March 1876, Thomas Edison moved into a new white two-storey building in an empty stretch of New Jersey farmland called Menlo Park, and made a promise that nobody in the history of invention had ever made.

He said he would produce a minor invention every ten days, and a big thing every six months or so.

Read that again, because it is one of the strangest sentences ever uttered by a creative person. Edison was not promising to invent something remarkable. He was promising a *cadence*.

Everything about that sentence should be impossible. Discovery is famously unpredictable. You cannot schedule an insight. The whole romance of invention is that it arrives, unbidden, in a bath or a dream or a flash. And here is a man in a field in New Jersey announcing a delivery schedule.

What Edison had understood — and what makes him a genuine architect rather than a genius — is that while you cannot make any *particular* discovery predictable, you can make the *rate* of discovery predictable, if you build a machine for producing attempts.

So look at what he actually built, because it was the real invention.

The ground floor was a fully equipped precision machine shop, so that a sketch made in the morning could be a physical object by the afternoon, without waiting on an outside supplier. The upper floor was a chemical and scientific laboratory with a library, and — famously — an organ, so that men working through the night had something to do at two in the morning. He stocked the place with a stated ambition of holding a sample of essentially every material available, so that no experiment ever had to stop and order something.

He staffed it not with assistants but with specialists: a mathematician, a glassblower, machinists, chemists, a British engineer. He kept them there, together, at all hours, and worked alongside them.

And he ran it on documentation. Every experiment was written up, in bound notebooks, with dates and results — including, especially, the failures. The Edison Papers project has been working through several million pages of it for decades. Failures were not embarrassments to be buried; they were inventory. When you have tried sixteen hundred materials for a filament and written down what each one did, you have an asset that no competitor can buy.

The famous outputs are the phonograph and a commercially viable incandescent lighting system. But Edison also filed patents at a rate that at times approached four hundred applications a year.

Now here is the connection to everything in this book.

Edison was not applying industrial discipline to make his discovery process safe. He was applying it to make the discovery process *productive*, and he kept the two things architecturally separate. Menlo Park was the laboratory. The Edison Electric Light Company, the manufacturing operations, the utilities — those were machines, and they were run like machines, and he did not conduct experiments in the middle of them.

The heroic capacity was deployed at full strength, in a building designed for it, with a cadence and a measurement system around it.

**Cotitura:** You cannot schedule the discovery. You can absolutely schedule the attempts — and once you can, the discovery starts arriving on something disturbingly close to a timetable.

**Surse:** [The Invention Factory — Thomas A. Edison Papers, Rutgers](https://edison.rutgers.edu/life-of-edison/biographical-essays/the-invention-factory) · [Building the Lab — Edison Papers, Rutgers](https://edison.rutgers.edu/life-of-edison/biographical-essays/factory/building-the-lab)

## 37. Nobody Has to Ask

**Unde intră:** Discovery · Hypothesis → Experiment → Level 1 · "A disciplined business keeps asking: is this already known well enough to belong in the machine? If not, what experiment will generate the evidence we need?"
**Status:** REALĂ

At Booking.com, if you want to change something about the website, you do not need permission.

Not from your manager. Not from a committee. Not from the founder. You do not need to build a business case, get design sign-off, or defend the idea in a meeting. If you are a developer, a designer, a copywriter, a marketer, or a customer service agent with a hunch, you can wrap your idea in an experiment and put it live to real customers.

At any given moment, the company is running something like a thousand experiments in parallel, and more than twenty-five thousand a year.

To an ordinary executive this sounds like anarchy, and the immediate objection is obvious: what if someone ships something that damages the business?

The answer is the interesting part, and it is what makes this architecture rather than chaos.

The thing that is centralized at Booking.com is not the decision. It is the *infrastructure for being wrong safely*. There is a dedicated internal team whose entire job is to build and maintain the experimentation platform — the traffic splitting, the statistical engine, the guardrail metrics, the automatic stopping rules. An experiment that starts hurting a core metric gets pulled. Nobody has to notice; the platform notices.

Because the cost of a bad idea has been driven to near zero, the permission to have one can be given to everybody. The system is not tolerant of risk because it is reckless. It is tolerant of risk because it has been engineered so that risk cannot get out of the sandbox.

And then there is the number that everyone at Booking.com knows and almost nobody outside the experimentation world believes.

Roughly nine out of ten ideas do not work.

The people generating those ideas are not amateurs. They are experienced product people, designers, and analysts, working on a website they know intimately, in a market they understand, with vastly more data than any competitor. And nine times out of ten, the thing they were confident about does nothing or makes things slightly worse.

Sit with that, because it destroys the standard model of how companies make decisions.

If nine out of ten expert intuitions are wrong, then a company that decides by seniority — where the most experienced person in the room chooses, and the choice is implemented — is implementing wrong answers most of the time, at full scale, and calling the result strategy. And it will never find out, because once you have rolled something out everywhere, you have no comparison group and therefore no way to know what would have happened otherwise.

Booking.com's founders and executives are wrong nine times out of ten too. The difference is that the organization is designed to find out cheaply, in a week, on two percent of traffic, instead of finding out expensively, in two years, on the whole business.

**Cotitura:** The question is not whether your instincts are good. Everyone's instincts are wrong most of the time. The question is how much it costs you to discover which ones — and whether that cost is low enough to let ordinary people have ideas.

**Surse:** [Building a Culture of Experimentation — *Harvard Business Review*, March 2020](https://hbr.org/2020/03/building-a-culture-of-experimentation) · [The role of experimentation at Booking.com](https://partner.booking.com/en-us/click-magazine/industry-perspectives/role-experimentation-bookingcom) · [Peek Inside Booking.com's Experimentation Culture — VWO](https://vwo.com/blog/cro-best-practices-booking/)

## 38. Five Thousand One Hundred and Twenty-Seven

**Unde intră:** Discovery · Level 0 → Level 1 · "Pentru cele unde nu am putut să le trecem de la level 0 la level 1, continuăm cu alte experimente."
**Status:** REALĂ

In 1979, James Dyson was renovating a house and got annoyed with his vacuum cleaner.

The annoyance was specific and, if you have ever owned a bagged vacuum, familiar: it lost suction almost immediately. He opened it up and worked out why. The bag is not just a container; it is the filter. And the moment a thin layer of dust coats the inside of the bag, the air can no longer pass through it, and the machine is finished — not full, just choked. He bought a new bag. Same thing.

Around the same time he had seen a sawmill using a cyclone tower to separate sawdust from air by spinning it. No filter. Nothing to clog.

So the hypothesis was formed in an afternoon: put a cyclone in a vacuum cleaner.

The hypothesis was correct. It took him about five thousand one hundred and twenty-six failures to make it true.

The number is famous because Dyson repeats it, and it is worth understanding what it actually consisted of, because "5,127 prototypes" makes it sound like he built five thousand vacuum cleaners. He did not. Almost all of them were cardboard and gaffer tape, and almost all of them differed from the previous one by a single variable — the angle of the cone, the diameter of the inlet, the height of the cylinder. He would build one, run it, measure, change one thing, build the next.

This is the least glamorous possible form of work. It is also the only form of work that produces knowledge, because if you change three things and it improves, you have learned nothing.

He got a working prototype in the early 1980s. That was the end of the easy part.

He took it to the vacuum cleaner industry — Hoover, Electrolux, Miele — and every one of them declined. The reasons given were about market readiness and manufacturing cost. The real reason, which became clear years later in litigation and in the industry's own internal history, is that the vacuum industry's profits came substantially from selling replacement bags. Dyson was not offering them a better product. He was offering them the destruction of their annuity.

He could not get manufactured in Britain. So he licensed it in Japan, where a machine called the G-Force went on sale in 1986, in pink, for a price that would be well over a thousand pounds today, and became a design object.

That licensing income funded the thing he actually wanted, which was his own factory. The DC01 launched in Britain in 1993 — fourteen years after the annoyance in the house — and within about two years it was the best-selling vacuum cleaner in the country.

Now, the popular version of this story is about persistence, and persistence is the least interesting thing about it.

What matters is the structure of what he did. Dyson had a Level 0 hypothesis: *cyclonic separation can replace a bag.* Most founders, at that point, would have raised money, built a factory, and hired a sales team — converting a hypothesis directly into an operation, the Webvan move.

Dyson spent years, alone, in a shed, refusing to promote the idea to Level 1 until it actually worked in his hands. Then, having proven it worked once, he still could not get it to Level 2 in Britain — so he ran it at small scale in a different market until the evidence and the capital were unarguable.

**Cotitura:** The romantic reading is that he never gave up. The useful reading is that he never once pretended he had proof before he had it.

## 39. Hot Doughnuts Now

**Unde intră:** Multiplication · "Creștere până la sinucidere." · multiplicarea unei mașinării înainte să fie validată la Level 3
**Status:** REALĂ

For a few years around the turn of the millennium, Krispy Kreme was the most exciting retail brand in America, and it was exciting for a genuinely good reason.

The product was extraordinary, and the delivery of it was theatre. A Krispy Kreme store was built around a visible production line — you could watch the doughnuts float down a river of hot oil and pass under a curtain of glaze. There was a neon sign in the window that lit up when a batch came out: HOT DOUGHNUTS NOW. People drove across town for that sign. New store openings drew crowds that camped overnight, and were covered on local news.

The company went public in April 2000. It was one of the best-performing IPOs of its era, the stock rising more than five hundred percent in a few years, from under ten dollars to near fifty. In 2000 there were about 140 stores. By 2004 there were around 360, and the company was opening more than two hundred additional locations in the following stretch.

Then the whole thing came apart, and the way it came apart is a textbook on multiplication.

The first problem was that Krispy Kreme mistook a scarcity effect for a product effect. The magic of Krispy Kreme in 1997 was partly the doughnut, and largely the fact that there was one store within a hundred miles and getting there was an occasion. When there are four stores in a metropolitan area, plus doughnuts on the shelf at every supermarket and petrol station in the state — which is what the wholesale channel did — the occasion is gone. What is left is a doughnut, which is a commodity, and a good one, but not one people drive across town for.

The second problem was that the growth was substantially manufactured out of the franchise system rather than out of customer demand. Franchisees complained that the company was packing new stores into territories that were already served, which produced a very satisfying revenue line at head office — franchise fees and equipment sales — while individual store sales cannibalized each other. Head office was, in effect, selling growth to its own franchisees and booking it as performance.

The third problem was that the accounting was aggressive enough to attract the Securities and Exchange Commission, which opened an investigation into how the company had recorded franchise buybacks.

In 2004 Krispy Kreme reported its first quarterly loss. Then the losses came in a run, same-store sales fell about twenty percent, roughly a quarter of the stores closed, the stock collapsed back to around its IPO price and below, and something on the order of two and a half billion dollars of shareholder value disappeared.

Krispy Kreme survived. It was taken private, rebuilt over a decade, and eventually returned to the market as a smaller, saner business.

But the diagnosis is precise, and it is not "they grew too fast." It is this: the thing that Krispy Kreme multiplied was not the thing that had produced its result.

The result had been produced by a specific combination — remarkable product, theatrical store, deliberate scarcity, and a location worth travelling to. When the company multiplied, it copied the stores and the doughnut and dropped the scarcity and the occasion, because those two were the parts that limited growth. It kept the visible parts of the machine and discarded the invisible ones.

**Cotitura:** Before you multiply, you have to know which parts of the machine actually caused the output. Multiplying the parts you can see, while quietly removing the constraints that made them work, is the most common way successful companies destroy themselves.

**Surse:** [10 famed American franchises that faced financial ruin — CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2014/10/31/10-famed-american-franchises-that-faced-financial-ruin.html) · [The Rise & Fall and Rise Again of Krispy Kreme — Wolf of Franchises](https://wolfoffranchises.com/newsletter/the-rise-fall-and-rise-again-of-krispy-kreme/)

## 40. Closed for Three Hours

**Unde intră:** Standardization · Multiplication · "The customer experiences your machine at the points that matter."
**Status:** REALĂ

On the afternoon of 26 February 2008, every Starbucks in the United States — 7,100 of them — put a sign in the window and locked the door.

The sign said, roughly: we're taking time to perfect our espresso. Great espresso requires practice. That's why we're dedicating ourselves to our craft.

For three and a half hours, in the middle of a business day, in the middle of a recession, one of the largest retailers in America shut down. Estimates put the lost revenue at around six million dollars. Dunkin' Donuts ran a promotion during the window.

Inside the stores, 135,000 baristas were being retaught how to pull a shot of espresso.

Howard Schultz had returned as chief executive seven weeks earlier. He had left the operating role in 2000, watched the company grow enormously, and come back to find something he described in a now-famous internal memo written the previous year — a memo that leaked, and that is one of the most honest documents ever written by a founder about his own company.

In it, Schultz went through the decisions of the growth years one by one, and noted that each of them had been individually correct and collectively fatal.

They had installed automatic espresso machines, because the manual La Marzocco machines were slow and inconsistent, and automation improved speed and reduced errors. It also removed the theatre — the barista's body, the movement, the sound — and it was tall enough to block the customer's view of the person making their drink.

They had moved to flavour-locked packaging, which kept the coffee fresher and made logistics vastly easier. It also removed the smell of coffee from the store, and the smell was, in Schultz's words, perhaps the most powerful signal the brand had.

They had standardized store design for efficient rollout, which is exactly what you do when you are opening thousands of locations. The stores lost their soul and started to look, in his phrase, like a chain of stores.

Every one of those decisions was a rational optimization of a part. Together they had removed the thing the whole machine existed to produce.

That is what the three and a half hours were actually about. Not espresso technique. The closure was a message with two audiences. To 135,000 employees: the standard is real, and we will burn six million dollars to prove it is real. To the customer: something here is being taken seriously.

Schultz also did the unglamorous half. Starbucks closed around six hundred underperforming US stores that year and slowed new openings dramatically. It replaced the espresso machines with shorter ones so you could see the barista again. It went back to grinding beans in store, accepting a slower, messier, more fragrant process.

**Cotitura:** Multiplication does not usually fail loudly. It fails as a series of individually sensible optimizations, each of which shaves a little off the thing customers were actually buying — until one day the machine is running perfectly and producing something nobody wants.

**Surse:** [Starbucks closing all 7,100 US stores for training — Press Democrat, 26 Feb 2008](https://www.pressdemocrat.com/2008/02/26/starbucks-closing-relax-its-just-for-3-hours-all-7100-us-stores-shutting-to-train-baristas-to-make-coffee-worth-4-a-cup/) · [Starbucks to close all U.S. stores for training — NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna23351151) · Howard Schultz, "The Commoditization of the Starbucks Experience," internal memo, 14 February 2007 · Howard Schultz, *Onward* (2011)

## 41. The Box

**Unde intră:** Standardization · "Standardizăm pentru a putea să trecem procesul la nivel 2." · Level 3 — transferability
**Status:** REALĂ

On 26 April 1956, a converted oil tanker called the *Ideal-X* left Port Newark, New Jersey, carrying fifty-eight metal boxes bolted to a reinforced deck. Five days later it arrived in Houston, where fifty-eight trucks were waiting to take the boxes away.

Almost nobody noticed. It is now generally regarded as the beginning of globalization.

To understand why, you have to understand what loading a ship meant before that day. Cargo arrived at a port as barrels, sacks, crates, bales, and drums, in every size that human beings had ever invented. Gangs of longshoremen — dozens of men per hold — physically carried, stacked, and wedged each item, fitting a three-dimensional puzzle by hand and by judgement. A ship could sit in port for a week or more. Goods were stolen at a rate that everyone simply accepted. Men were regularly injured and killed.

The cost of all that hand-work was so large that it dwarfed the cost of the ocean voyage itself. Loading a medium-sized ship conventionally in 1956 ran around $5.83 a ton. Loading it with containers came to under $0.16 a ton.

That is not an improvement. That is a different universe. When moving something across an ocean becomes essentially free relative to the value of the thing, you can put the factory anywhere on earth.

Malcom McLean, who made it happen, was not a shipping man. He was a North Carolina trucker who had spent a day in 1937 sitting in his truck at a New Jersey dock waiting for men to unload his cotton bales one at a time, thinking that it would be so much simpler to lift the whole trailer body onto the ship.

But the boxes on the *Ideal-X* were not the invention. Containers had existed in various forms for decades. Railways had tried them. The invention was what came next, and it took twelve more years and was excruciating.

McLean's boxes were thirty-five feet long, because that was the maximum trailer length on the highways he knew. Matson, on the Pacific, used twenty-four feet, because their cargo was dense Hawaiian pineapple. European operators used other sizes. Every operator had its own corner fittings, its own lifting gear, its own chassis, its own cranes. A container from one line could not be handled by another line's port.

Which meant that the entire economic miracle was locked inside individual companies and could not become an industry.

The fight over standardization took most of the 1960s and was conducted through committees, trade associations, and the International Organization for Standardization. It was bitter, because every operator had already invested enormous capital in its own dimensions and each was arguing for the standard that would not bankrupt it. McLean's own company ultimately released its patents royalty-free so a common standard could exist.

By 1968–70 the world had agreed: standard lengths of twenty and forty feet, a fixed width and height, and — the crucial detail — a standardized corner casting, so that any crane in any port on earth could pick up any container made by anyone.

That last piece is what turned a company's clever process into infrastructure that the whole world could use. And the effect was total: within two decades, most of the world's great port cities had moved their docks, the longshoreman unions of New York and London had largely ceased to exist, and it became rational to make a shirt in Bangladesh for a shop in Bristol.

**Cotitura:** A process that works only in your hands is Level 2. A process specified precisely enough that a stranger can plug into it without talking to you is Level 3 — and the difference between those two is the difference between a good company and an industry.

## 42. The Waffle House Index

**Unde intră:** The Output · "Customer-facing predictability creates trust." · "Innovation may attract attention. Predictability is often what earns loyalty."
**Status:** REALĂ

When a hurricane makes landfall on the American Gulf Coast, one of the things the Federal Emergency Management Agency looks at is whether the local Waffle House is serving pancakes.

This is not a joke, or rather it started as one and stopped being one. The former FEMA administrator Craig Fugate coined it after Hurricane Charley in 2004, and it has been used inside emergency management ever since. It is called the Waffle House Index and it has three settings.

Green: the restaurant is open and serving the full menu. Power is on. The supply chain is intact. The area is basically fine.

Yellow: the restaurant is open but on a limited menu. That means no power, or no gas, or no water, or a broken supply line — they are cooking what they can on what they have. The area is damaged.

Red: the Waffle House is closed. In Fugate's words: if you get there and the Waffle House is closed, that's really bad. That's where you get to work.

Now, why does this work? Why can a federal agency use a diner chain as a sensor?

Because Waffle House has, over decades, built one of the most obsessively predictable operating machines in American retail. Over two thousand locations, most of them in exactly the parts of the country that get hit by hurricanes, tornadoes and floods. Open twenty-four hours, every day of the year, including Christmas. No locks on some of the doors, historically, because there was never a reason to close.

And critically, the company has an actual playbook for disasters — a documented set of limited menus for different failure states, portable generators, pre-positioned equipment, a jump team that deploys into damaged regions, and a protocol for reopening. They have rehearsed operating without power. They know which menu items survive which failures.

That is why the signal is meaningful. A restaurant that closes when things get difficult tells you nothing when it closes, because it might have closed for any reason. A restaurant that has engineered itself to stay open through almost anything is, when it closes, transmitting real information about the state of the world.

Sit with what that means as a business outcome.

Waffle House did not set out to become a federal disaster indicator. It set out to be open. And the byproduct of extreme, boring, documented, rehearsed predictability is that an institution as sceptical as the US government came to treat its behaviour as data.

You cannot buy that. There is no marketing campaign that produces it. It is the compound interest of a machine that does the same thing, in the same way, for fifty years, including on the days when doing so is very inconvenient.

Every founder wants a brand that people trust. Almost all of them try to get it by being memorable. Waffle House got it by being boring on a scale that became remarkable.

**Cotitura:** Innovation gets you noticed. Predictability gets you trusted — and trust is the only asset in a business that compounds without you touching it.

## 43. The Monkey

**Unde intră:** Managing the Known VS the Unknown · "The machine converts uncertainty from a fog into monitored variation." · eroismul scos în afara mașinăriei
**Status:** REALĂ

Somewhere inside Netflix's production infrastructure, during normal working hours, on a normal Tuesday, a program wakes up and destroys a running server at random.

Not in a test environment. In production. While customers are watching films.

It is called Chaos Monkey, and it is one of the most counterintuitive pieces of architecture in modern engineering.

The origin was a migration. In 2008 Netflix suffered a major database corruption that stopped it shipping DVDs for three days, and over the following years it moved its infrastructure onto Amazon's cloud. Cloud infrastructure has a property that is easy to say and very hard to internalize: individual machines fail constantly, without warning, and there is nothing you can do about it. It is not a bug. It is the operating condition.

So Netflix's engineers faced a question that every company faces in some form. You know that failures will happen. You believe your system will survive them. How do you find out whether that belief is true?

The traditional answer is that you find out at three in the morning during a Super Bowl, and you find out by watching a very talented engineer save the company while everyone watches. That is the heroic model of reliability: build the thing, hope, and keep excellent people on call.

Netflix's answer was to stop waiting.

Chaos Monkey kills random production instances continuously, deliberately, during business hours — specifically during business hours, so that when it breaks something, the engineers who built it are awake, caffeinated, at their desks, and can fix it properly instead of applying a bandage at four in the morning.

The logic is precise. If a random server dying at eleven on a Tuesday causes a customer-visible outage, then your system was already broken; you simply had not been informed yet. The monkey does not create the fragility. It converts an unknown fragility into a known one, at a time you have chosen, at a cost you can afford.

Netflix later built a whole family of these — the Simian Army — including one that simulates the failure of an entire Amazon availability zone, and eventually one that took out a whole geographic region.

Now, notice what this is, in the language of this book.

Netflix did not eliminate uncertainty. Servers still fail unpredictably; that will never change. What it did was move the uncertainty from the fog into monitored variation. Before Chaos Monkey, "our system probably survives a node failure" was a belief. After Chaos Monkey, it is a fact that is re-verified thousands of times a week, automatically, and any regression shows up immediately.

And notice the second thing, which matters more. The engineers who used to be heroes — the ones who saved the company at three in the morning, whose reputations were built on those saves — did not become less valuable. Their capacity got redeployed. Instead of spending their excellence compensating for fragility that nobody had measured, they spend it building systems that do not need to be saved.

**Cotitura:** You do not remove uncertainty from a business. You choose whether to meet it at a moment of your own choosing, in daylight, with everyone rested — or at the worst possible moment, with a hero.
