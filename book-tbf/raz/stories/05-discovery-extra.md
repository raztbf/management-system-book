## 44. The Houseboat and the Bicycle Shop

**Unde intră:** Discovery · "Hypothesis → Experiment → Level 1" · "Building the operation is not the fast route to validating the model"
**Status:** REALĂ

On 8 December 1903, a houseboat sat in the middle of the Potomac River with the future of aviation bolted to its roof.

The machine on top was called the Great Aerodrome, and it belonged to Samuel Pierpont Langley, the Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution — which is to say, the most institutionally powerful scientist in the United States. He had received fifty thousand dollars from the War Department, and further funds from the Smithsonian on top of that. He had professional machinists. He had a purpose-built catapult. He had a pilot, Charles Manly, sitting in the frame. And he had the national press, who had been invited, standing on the riverbank in December, waiting.

The catapult fired. The Great Aerodrome left the houseboat, folded in on itself, and dropped into the river. Manly was pulled out of the water.

It was the second time this had happened in nine weeks. Langley never tried again. The newspapers were merciless. He died three years later, and his obituaries mentioned the Potomac.

Nine days after that second crash, on a beach in North Carolina, two brothers who repaired bicycles for a living flew.

The part everybody knows is the flying. The part almost nobody knows is the arithmetic. Between 1900 and 1903, Wilbur and Orville Wright spent — on everything, including materials, the engine, and every trip to Kitty Hawk and back — a little under one thousand dollars, paid out of the profits of a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio.

Langley had perhaps a hundred times the money and lost.

That is a satisfying story if you like underdogs, and told that way it is almost useless. The Wrights did not win by being plucky. They won because of what they had been doing for two years in the back of the bicycle shop, which nobody photographed and no newspaper thought was news.

In October 1901 they built a wind tunnel. Six feet long. The balances inside it — the instruments that measured lift against drag — were made from bicycle spokes and scrap metal. Over the following weeks they tested up to two hundred model wing shapes and recorded formal data on roughly fifty of them.

They were not building an aircraft. They were building a machine that produces knowledge about aircraft.

And then they found the thing that should stop you cold.

Every serious person working on flight was using Otto Lilienthal's lift tables, and inside those tables sat Smeaton's coefficient — a value of 0.005, in continuous use since the 1700s. The Wrights' 1901 glider produced about a third of the lift those tables predicted.

The obvious conclusion was that they had built the glider wrong. Instead, they checked the number. They calculated the coefficient themselves from their own measurements and got 0.0033.

Modern aerodynamicists confirm they were right. An entire field had been building machines on top of a figure nobody had verified for a hundred and fifty years.

On 17 December 1903 the Wright Flyer left the ground. The first flight lasted twelve seconds and covered a hundred and twenty feet.

And then they did it three more times that day. The fourth flight lasted fifty-nine seconds and covered eight hundred and fifty-two feet. By 1905, the Flyer III could stay up for thirty-nine minutes and circle a field.

That is the difference, and it is not about money or courage. Langley was trying to achieve a flight. The Wrights were building something that produces flights.

**Cotitura:** The expensive machine on the houseboat was a bet. The cheap machine in the bicycle shop was an instrument. One of them could only be right once.

## 45. From Suck to Not-Suck

**Unde intră:** Discovery · "9 din 10 idei de expert sunt greșite" · răspunsul la obiecția "munca mea e creativă, nu se poate sistematiza"
**Status:** REALĂ

The most famous sentence ever written about the film business is four words long, and it was written by a man who had won two Academy Awards for writing films.

In *Adventures in the Screen Trade*, published in 1983, William Goldman set out to explain how Hollywood decides what to make, and concluded that it does not, and cannot:

> *Nobody knows anything.*

He meant it precisely. Not one person among the handful of people able to greenlight a film knows, or can know, whether it will work. Every studio has released a certain flop that became a phenomenon and a certain hit that vanished. The industry's own considered position on its own core competence is that the cause is unknowable.

An entire industry, organized around the belief that you cannot own the cause.

Now consider what happened at a computer graphics company in Emeryville, California, between 1995 and 2010.

*Toy Story.* *A Bug's Life.* *Toy Story 2.* *Monsters, Inc.* *Finding Nemo.* *The Incredibles.* *Cars.* *Ratatouille.* *WALL·E.* *Up.* *Toy Story 3.*

Eleven feature films. Every one profitable. Almost every one critically acclaimed. The run did not break until 2011.

Nothing like that had happened before, in any studio, in the history of the medium. And the interesting question is not how Pixar found so many good ideas. It is what Ed Catmull, who ran the place, says about the ideas themselves.

> *In truth, all of our movies suck at first. I don't mean this in the sense that I'm being self-effacing or modest. I mean this in the sense that they suck.*

Read that again, because it inverts everything the industry believes. Pixar's advantage was not that its people had better ideas. Its advantage was that it had built something reliable for converting a bad film into a good one.

The centrepiece is the Braintrust — a group of directors and writers who watch each other's work in progress and say exactly what they think. And the design detail that makes it function is a subtraction: **the Braintrust has no authority.** It cannot mandate a single change. It exists to give the director the most honest possible picture of what is wrong, and then the director decides.

That one rule does something specific. It removes the incentive to defend. If the feedback cannot be imposed, hearing it costs you nothing, so you can afford to actually listen to it.

The second mechanism is dailies — showing incomplete, ugly, unfinished work to your colleagues every single day. Not when it is ready. Every day. Which sounds like a productivity ritual and is actually a psychological one: if everyone sees everyone's bad work constantly, the shame of being seen mid-failure evaporates, and people stop hiding problems until they are too expensive to fix.

Neither of those is a creative technique. Both are process design. And together they turn something the industry considers a lottery into something Pixar did eleven times in a row.

**Cotitura:** Hollywood says nobody knows anything. What it actually means is that nobody in Hollywood had built a machine for finding out.

**Surse:** William Goldman, *Adventures in the Screen Trade* (1983) · Ed Catmull, *Creativity, Inc.* (2014) · ["Inside the Pixar Braintrust" — Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3027135/inside-the-pixar-braintrust)

## 46. The Fifty-Second Game

**Unde intră:** Multiplication · "Un rezultat e ce s-a întâmplat. O cauză e ce face să se întâmple din nou." · cazul cel mai apropiat de cititorul tău obișnuit
**Status:** REALĂ

In December 2009, a small Finnish company released a mobile game about birds being fired at pigs, and it became one of the largest cultural phenomena in the short history of the telephone.

You already know Angry Birds. Your mother knows Angry Birds. There were plush toys, an animated series, a feature film, theme parks, and for a period of about three years a reasonable argument that it was the most recognised entertainment property on earth.

Here is the part that is not in the legend. Angry Birds was, by the company's own account, Rovio's fifty-second game.

Not their first. Not their lucky break. Their fifty-second. Six years of work and fifty-one attempts that went nowhere.

So whatever else you want to say about Rovio, you cannot say they did not experiment. They experimented more than almost anyone. They did the thing every business book tells you to do — try, repeatedly, cheaply, until something works.

And then something worked, and they could never do it again.

Not for want of trying. Over the following decade Rovio launched title after title, and remained heavily dependent on the one franchise. In 2015 they laid off a third of their staff. In October 2017 they went public at a valuation of around a billion dollars, and the shares spent the following years well below the offer price. In April 2023, fourteen years after the birds, the company was sold to Sega for seven hundred and six million euros — less than it had been worth on the day it listed.

I want to be careful here, because this is not a story about failure and it becomes much less useful if you read it as one. Seven hundred and seventy-six million dollars is real money. Angry Birds is still earning. Nobody defrauded anybody, nobody scaled greedily, nobody destroyed anything, and the people involved were talented and worked extremely hard.

Rovio simply never became a machine. And the market, in the end, priced it accurately: as one hit, rather than as a company that makes hits.

That is the ordinary condition of most businesses. Most companies do not collapse. They get stuck owning a result.

**Cotitura:** Experimenting is not the same as owning the cause. You can run fifty-one experiments, find the answer on the fifty-second, and still be unable to explain it — which means you cannot do it again.

## 47. The Least Powerful CEO in the World

**Unde intră:** oriunde e nevoie de o singură companie care demonstrează toate piesele buclei simultan · Protect the machine · Discovery
**Status:** REALĂ

Supercell was founded in Helsinki in 2010, in the same city as Rovio, in the same market, drawing on the same small pool of Finnish game developers.

Its first three games failed.

Then came the thing Supercell is actually known for inside the industry, which sounds like a joke until you look at the numbers: it kills almost everything it builds. Dozens of games, some already in regional soft launch and performing acceptably, stopped.

And the decision is not made by an executive committee. There isn't one. The team that built the game decides for itself whether to continue or to kill it. When they kill it, they open champagne.

Ilkka Paananen, who runs the company, is insistent that the popular version of this is wrong: *"Some say we celebrate failure, but we don't. We celebrate the learnings from the failure, which are so valuable that they're worth celebrating."*

He describes his own role this way: **"My goal is to be the world's least powerful CEO."**

What survives all that killing is a handful of games. And look at what they do.

Clash of Clans launched in 2012. In 2025 — thirteen years later — it generated around $254 million and had close to a hundred million monthly players. Lifetime revenue is above ten billion dollars.

That is not a hit. That is a **normality**. It is precisely what an Ordinary Great Year describes: an output the machine produces routinely, year after year, without extraordinary intervention.

In 2016, Tencent bought 84.3% of the company at a valuation of $10.2 billion. In 2018, with 283 employees, revenue per employee was close to $5.7 million.

But the reason this company matters is not the money. It is that Supercell is the only organization I have found that demonstrates every station of the loop at the same time, and shows how they sit next to each other without contaminating one another.

The **machine** is live operations on a few validated games — Clash of Clans, thirteen years, a quarter of a billion a year. Boring. Predictable. It pays for everything.

**Discovery** is the independent cells building new games, most of which die.

**Multiplication** is a validated game rolling out globally and running for a decade, and new cells built from the same blueprint.

And the rule that holds it together: the experiments happen **in separate cells, never inside the live games**. A game producing two hundred and fifty million dollars a year is not where you test ideas.

That separation is not a cultural preference at Supercell. It is architecture.

**Cotitura:** The machine pays for the laboratory, and the laboratory is not allowed into the machine. Everything else in this book is a detail of that sentence.
