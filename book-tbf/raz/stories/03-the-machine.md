## 22. The Tennis Court

**Unde intră:** "A process is not a list of tasks. It is a repeatable path from an input to an output." · "How to build a machine?"
**Status:** REALĂ

In 1948, in San Bernardino, California, two brothers closed a successful restaurant in order to fix it.

Richard and Maurice McDonald ran a drive-in barbecue joint. It made money. It had carhops, a twenty-five-item menu, china plates, and metal cutlery. It also had all the problems that every restaurant has: orders got wrong, the food took time, teenagers loitered, plates broke, cutlery was stolen, and the labour cost of doing all of it well was crushing.

Most restaurateurs improve a restaurant by finding better staff. The McDonalds did something else. They looked at their receipts and noticed that eighty percent of their revenue came from hamburgers.

So they shut down, cut the menu from twenty-five items to nine, replaced china with paper, eliminated the carhops, eliminated cutlery, and then did the thing that actually matters for this book.

They went to a tennis court behind Richard's house, and with a piece of chalk they drew the kitchen on the ground. Full size. Every station. The grill, the fry station, the drinks, the dressing table, the window.

Then they brought their staff out onto the tennis court and made them cook an imaginary lunch rush. Nobody had food. Nobody had equipment. They simply walked through it: pick up the imaginary patty, turn, place it here, two steps to the left, dress it here, hand it there.

And they watched where people collided.

Wherever two employees crossed paths, that was a defect in the layout. Wherever someone had to turn around, that was wasted seconds. Wherever someone had to wait, there was a bottleneck. They rubbed out the chalk and redrew the kitchen. Then they did it again. And again. Reportedly they were out there for hours, and then came back the next day, and kept going until every motion in the kitchen had been eliminated, shortened, or sequenced.

Richard McDonald later described a moment when a rain shower washed the whole thing away and they had to start over.

What they built was called the Speedee Service System, and when it opened, customers hated it. There were no carhops. You had to get out of your car and walk to a window. There were no plates. The menu had almost nothing on it. Sales dropped, and for months the brothers held their nerve.

Then it worked, and it worked in a way nothing in the restaurant industry had ever worked. Fifteen-cent hamburgers, served in seconds, at consistent quality, produced by teenagers with no cooking skill whatsoever, because the skill had been moved out of the people and into the layout.

That last sentence is the whole discipline. The McDonald brothers did not train better cooks. They designed a process in which cooking ability was largely irrelevant to the outcome.

Now here is the part that makes this a story about architecture and not just about efficiency. The reason people came from all over America to look at that kitchen — including a fifty-two-year-old milkshake-machine salesman named Ray Kroc, who came because a single restaurant in the desert had ordered eight of his machines — was not that the food was extraordinary. It was not. It was that the output was *the same every time*, at a speed nobody could match, without heroes.

And notice what the tennis court actually was. It was an experiment. The brothers did not implement the new kitchen and hope. They built the cheapest possible simulation of it — chalk, on a public court, with imaginary hamburgers — and ran it until the process was validated, before they spent a dollar on stainless steel.

**Cotitura:** Before you hire, before you buy equipment, before you build the org chart — draw it in chalk and walk through it. Every collision you find on the tennis court is a collision you would otherwise have paid for in salaries for the next five years.

## 23. The Man Who Studied Hamburgers to Cure Blindness

**Unde intră:** "The machine is successful when normal output does not require exceptional rescue." · Arhitectura mașinăriei · Standardization
**Status:** REALĂ

Dr Govindappa Venkataswamy retired from the Indian government medical service in 1976, at the age of fifty-eight, with rheumatoid arthritis so severe that his fingers were permanently deformed. He had trained himself to hold surgical instruments anyway, and had personally performed something like a hundred thousand eye operations.

On retirement, he opened an eleven-bed clinic in his brother's house in Madurai, in Tamil Nadu.

The problem he was facing was arithmetic, not medicine. India had millions of people going blind from cataracts — a condition that is completely curable with a twenty-minute operation. The operation was not the constraint. The number of surgeons was the constraint, and no plausible number of surgeons was ever going to be enough. If every ophthalmologist in India worked at the normal rate for the rest of their lives, most of those people would die blind.

So Dr V, as everyone called him, went looking for a model of how to produce something in enormous quantity at consistent quality, and he found it in a place no doctor would think to look.

He looked at McDonald's.

The reasoning he gave was almost childlike in its directness: *if McDonald's can sell billions of burgers, and Coca-Cola can sell billions of bottles, why can't I sell millions of sight-restoring operations?*

What he took from it was not the food. It was the insight that made the McDonald brothers' tennis court work — that if you decompose a process precisely enough, you can move most of the skill out of the scarce person and into the system, and the scarce person then only does the part that genuinely requires them.

In an Aravind operating theatre, a surgeon does not walk between patients. There are two operating tables side by side. The surgeon performs the operation on one table while a full team preps, positions, anaesthetizes, and drapes the patient on the other. When the surgeon finishes, they rotate their chair. Everything a surgeon does that is not surgery — the paperwork, the counselling, the prep, the follow-up, the sterilization, the recovery — has been given to trained mid-level ophthalmic personnel, largely young women recruited from rural villages and trained in-house for two years.

The intraocular lenses were a cost problem, so Aravind built its own manufacturing arm, Aurolab, and drove the price of a lens from around two hundred dollars to a few dollars.

The results: Aravind's surgeons average around two thousand cataract operations a year against an Indian national average of around four hundred. The system sees over ten thousand outpatients and performs well over a thousand surgeries a day. Roughly half to seventy percent of patients pay nothing at all — the paying patients cross-subsidize them — and the clinical outcomes for the free patients are the same as for the paying ones. Aravind's complication rates are at or better than published rates in the United Kingdom.

The last sentence is the one that kills the standard objection. Everyone assumes that volume trades against quality, that assembly lines produce worse medicine, that the caring is in the inefficiency.

It is not true, and Aravind proved it at a scale that cannot be argued with. What produced the quality was the same thing that produced the volume: a process so precisely defined that the outcome did not depend on which surgeon you got, or how tired they were, or whether it was the first case of the day or the fortieth.

**Cotitura:** The choice is not between a machine and good care. Very often the machine *is* the good care — because it is what stops your outcome depending on which human being happened to be on shift.

## 24. The Hospital That Does One Thing

**Unde intră:** "Limitele arhitecturii alese" · "Level 2 / Level 3 — repeatability and transferability"
**Status:** REALĂ

Just north of Toronto there is a hospital that will not treat you unless you have a hernia.

The Shouldice Hernia Centre has been operating since 1945 and it does one operation. Not mostly one operation. One. If you arrive with a hernia and also have a serious heart condition, they will decline you. If you are significantly overweight, they will tell you to lose the weight and come back — and some patients do, because the waiting list is long and people fly in from other continents.

Everything about the place is organized around that single procedure, and the organization is strange enough that visitors keep writing about it.

The surgery is done under local anaesthetic, and the patient walks off the operating table. Not is wheeled. Walks. Then walks up a flight of stairs. The building is deliberately designed to make patients move: rooms have no telephones or televisions worth watching, the dining room is a walk away, the grounds are pleasant and you are expected to be out in them. There is a putting green. Patients recover in groups of four to a room, and yesterday's patients become today's patients' informal coaching staff, which is a piece of process design masquerading as hospitality.

Surgeons at Shouldice perform something like twenty times more hernia repairs than a surgeon in a general hospital. A new surgeon is not permitted to operate alone until they have assisted through a long apprenticeship, because the whole point is that the specific technique — a layered repair of the patient's own tissue, developed by Edward Earle Shouldice — is executed the same way every time.

The hospital performs over seven thousand repairs a year.

And here is the number that matters. A peer-reviewed study in the *Canadian Journal of Surgery* compared recurrence rates for inguinal hernias repaired at Shouldice against those repaired at general hospitals across Ontario. Shouldice's age-standardized recurrence risk was 1.15 percent. The hospital's own figures for primary repairs are lower still. General surgical practice has historically reported recurrence in the range of ten percent and above.

Same operation. Same country. Roughly a tenfold difference in the thing the operation is for.

Now, the honest reading of this requires acknowledging the trade-off, because that is where the lesson actually lives.

Shouldice's results are extraordinary *because* of what it refuses to do. It does not take complex patients. It does not take emergencies. It does not do other surgeries. Its architecture has hard limits, and those limits are not a weakness that it is working to overcome — they are the load-bearing walls. The moment Shouldice starts accepting the patients a general hospital accepts, its numbers become a general hospital's numbers.

Every architecture has this property. The one you choose determines what you are excellent at and what you are structurally incapable of. Some architectures need talent that is nearly impossible to hire. Some need enormous capital before they produce anything. Some are profitable only at volumes you may never reach. Some require you to charge a price the market will not bear.

The mistake is not picking an architecture with limits. Every architecture has limits. The mistake is scaling one for six years before discovering where its wall is.

**Cotitura:** Shouldice's competitive advantage is a list of patients it turns away. What is on yours — and have you ever written it down?

## 25. Ten Minutes

**Unde intră:** "Arhitectura mașinăriei este de fapt modelul de business." · constraint-uri care definesc arhitectura
**Status:** REALĂ

In 1972, Southwest Airlines was two years old, flying four Boeing 737s between Dallas, Houston and San Antonio, and losing money badly. It had posted a loss of one and a half million dollars. To stay alive, it had to sell one of its four aircraft.

The obvious consequence of selling twenty-five percent of your fleet is that you fly twenty-five percent fewer flights. The schedule was already published. Cutting it would have told the market that the airline was dying, which it was.

The airline's response — the decision that made Southwest into Southwest — was to keep the entire four-plane schedule and fly it with three planes.

The arithmetic only works one way. The industry standard for turning an aircraft around at a gate — passengers off, cleaning, catering, refuelling, baggage, passengers on — was around forty-five minutes. Southwest would have to do it in ten.

Ten minutes is not forty-five minutes made faster. Ten minutes is a different business.

To get to ten minutes, essentially every element of the airline had to be redesigned, and each redesign looks, from the outside, like a quirky brand decision.

No assigned seating — because assigned seating slows boarding, and Southwest famously handed out reusable plastic boarding cards for decades. No meals — because catering trucks are a serialized dependency you cannot compress. No interline baggage agreements with other carriers, no hub-and-spoke connections — because a connecting bag is a bag whose arrival you do not control. Point-to-point routes into secondary airports — because a congested major hub makes a ten-minute turn physically impossible regardless of how good your crew is. Pilots and flight attendants who help clean the cabin — because a job description is not more important than the turn.

And above all: one aircraft type. Only Boeing 737s, for fifty years.

That single constraint cascades through everything. One type means every pilot can fly every aircraft in the fleet. Every mechanic is trained on one airframe. One spare parts inventory. One set of manuals. One simulator programme. A schedule disruption can be solved by swapping any plane for any other plane. A competitor with six aircraft types has six of everything, and cannot make those swaps.

Herb Kelleher's answer, when told they had to sell a plane, was reportedly four words: *we'll turn planes faster.*

Southwest went on to be profitable for forty-seven consecutive years, through oil shocks, deregulation, 9/11, and the 2008 crash, in an industry that has collectively lost money over its entire history.

Now the part that matters for you.

None of the individual decisions on that list is clever. Open seating is not an innovation. Flying only 737s is not an innovation. What was clever was recognizing that they all had to be the *same* decision — that an architecture is not a collection of good choices but a set of choices that only work together.

And the corollary is brutal. Southwest could not have added first class. It could not have flown to Europe. It could not have offered connections. Every attractive opportunity that violated the ten-minute turn had to be refused, for decades, by people who could see the revenue sitting on the table.

**Cotitura:** An architecture is not what you decided to do. It is the list of profitable things you have permanently agreed not to do — and if that list is empty, you do not have an architecture.

## 26. The Red Beads

**Unde intră:** "A machine is not built because people are untrustworthy. It is built because people are human." · "How to manage a machine?" · feedback bazat pe responsabilități măsurabile
**Status:** REALĂ

W. Edwards Deming was in his eighties when he started doing this, and he did it in front of rooms full of American executives who had paid a great deal of money to be there.

He would bring out a plastic box containing four thousand small beads. Eight hundred of them were red. Three thousand two hundred were white. He would also bring a paddle with fifty holes in it.

Then he would hire from the audience. He needed six Willing Workers, two Inspectors, a Chief Inspector, and a Recorder. He would explain, with total seriousness, that this was a demanding job with strict standards, that there were people waiting for these positions, and that the company's customer accepts only white beads.

The job was simple. Stir the beads. Dip the paddle in. Lift it out. You now have fifty beads. The red ones are defects.

The stated standard was no more than three red beads per paddle.

Then Deming would run the factory, and he would run it exactly the way a real manager runs a real factory.

Worker one dips: sixteen red beads. Deming, gravely: sixteen. That is unacceptable. Worker two: nine red. Deming: excellent, nine — everyone, watch how she does it. Worker three: fourteen. Deming: what is going on? We are all using the same paddle.

Day two. The good worker gets worse. The bad worker gets better. Deming puts up a chart. He institutes merit pay. He puts the best performer on the wall. He warns the worst performer. He gives a speech about commitment. He announces that management has set a stretch goal of two red beads.

By day four he is firing people. He lays off the three worst performers and tells the remaining three to work double shifts, because in his experience the best workers can carry the load.

The numbers do not change. They never change. They cannot change, because there are eight hundred red beads in the box and fifty holes in the paddle, and no amount of effort, incentive, fear, praise, or commitment on the part of the Willing Workers alters the ratio of red beads in a plastic container.

The workers cannot control a single thing that matters. They did not choose the beads. They did not design the paddle. They did not set the standard. They are, in Deming's phrase, willing — and their willingness is irrelevant.

Deming's summary of what this demonstrates was blunt: the overwhelming majority of the variation in an organization's outcomes comes from the system, not from the people in it. His own figure was ninety-four percent.

Now watch what the exercise really exposes. It is not that management is stupid. Every single one of Deming's interventions is a perfectly reasonable management action. Praising the top performer is reasonable. Coaching the bottom one is reasonable. Setting a stretch target is reasonable. Instituting merit pay is reasonable. All of it is what a good manager does.

All of it is also *noise*, dressed as leadership, applied to a system nobody has looked at.

And the human cost is not zero. The worker who pulled sixteen red beads was publicly told they were failing. They will believe it. They will try harder. They will feel worse. And next month they will pull eleven and be praised for an improvement they did not cause.

**Cotitura:** Before you have a conversation about someone's performance, you owe them one question, asked of yourself: how many red beads are in the box I handed them?

**Surse:** [The Red Bead Experiment — The W. Edwards Deming Institute](https://deming.org/explore/red-bead-experiment/) · [Lessons From the Red Bead Experiment with Dr Deming](https://deming.org/lessons-from-the-red-bead-experiment-with-dr-deming/)

## 27. The Funnel

**Unde intră:** "The dangerous sequence is familiar: a process begins to work; the founder becomes less necessary inside it; the founder notices an imperfection; an unvalidated change enters operations..."
**Status:** REALĂ

Deming had a second demonstration, and it is less famous than the red beads, and it is the more useful of the two for anyone who owns a company.

You need a funnel on a stand, a marble, and a target drawn on a cloth on the table. You drop the marble through the funnel and mark where it comes to rest. Then you do it again, fifty times.

Rule One: aim the funnel at the target, and never move it. Just drop the marble fifty times.

You get a scatter of marks around the target. It is not tight. Some are quite far off. This is the natural variation of the system — the funnel, the marble, the cloth, the air, the table.

Now Rule Two, which is what every intelligent, engaged manager on earth does instinctively. After each drop, measure how far the marble ended up from the target, and move the funnel by that amount in the opposite direction. If it landed three centimetres to the left, shift the funnel three centimetres to the right. You are correcting. You are responsive. You are paying attention.

The scatter gets worse. Measurably, reliably worse — the variance roughly doubles.

Rule Three: after each drop, move the funnel from the *target* by the error, rather than from its current position. The scatter explodes outward. The marble ends up wandering further and further from the target in an ever-widening spiral.

Rule Four is the one that should chill you, because it is how most organizations actually learn. Drop the marble, then move the funnel to sit directly over where the marble just landed. Aim at the last result. This is *let's do what worked last time*, *let's follow the successful campaign*, *the last hire came from this channel so let's hire from this channel*.

Under Rule Four, the marble walks away from the target forever, in a random direction, never to return.

Only Rule One — the rule that looks like passivity, like negligence, like a manager who does not care — keeps the output centred on the target.

Deming's point was not that you should never intervene. It was that you must distinguish between two completely different situations that look identical from inside: variation that is inherent to the system, and a genuine signal that something has changed. Adjusting for the first is called tampering, and tampering always makes things worse.

Now transpose it.

A process in your company has been validated. It works. It produces a result inside a known range. This month, the result comes in at the low end of the range. Nothing has broken. This is a marble landing three centimetres left.

The founder sees the number, feels the familiar tightening, and intervenes. Changes the script. Adds a step. Reassigns the owner. Calls a meeting. Announces a new focus.

Next month the number moves — because numbers always move — and the founder has now learned that intervention works. And the process, which was stable, now contains an unvalidated change made in response to noise. Its variation increases. Which produces more alarming months. Which produces more intervention.

Rule Two. And it will feel, the entire time, like leadership.

**Cotitura:** The most expensive thing a founder can do to a working process is pay close attention to it without knowing the difference between a signal and a wobble.

**Surse:** W. Edwards Deming, *Out of the Crisis* (1982) — the Funnel Experiment · [The W. Edwards Deming Institute](https://deming.org/)

## 28. A Billion Dollars of Conveyor Belt

**Unde intră:** "O altă mare greșeală este transformarea fără dovadă a proceselor de level 0 în level 2." · "At an early stage, the business model is not truth. It is a hypothesis."
**Status:** REALĂ

Webvan was going to be the future of groceries, and the people who thought so were not fools.

The founder, Louis Borders, had already built Borders Books into a national chain. The investors were Sequoia, Benchmark, and SoftBank — the best in the business. They hired George Shaheen, the chief executive of Andersen Consulting, a firm with tens of thousands of employees, to run it. The 1999 IPO valued the company at around eight billion dollars. In total it raised something on the order of one and a quarter billion dollars.

The thesis was coherent, and I want to be fair to it, because coherence is exactly the trap.

Groceries are the largest retail category in the economy. Margins are thin because of stores — real estate, shelf-stackers, shrinkage. If you remove the store and replace it with a highly automated distribution centre serving a whole metropolitan area, your cost per order falls below what a supermarket can achieve, you deliver to the customer's door in a thirty-minute window, and you take a slice of the biggest market in retail.

Every sentence in that paragraph is plausible. Nobody could find the flaw by arguing about it. The flaw could only be found by running it.

Webvan did not run it. Webvan built it.

The Oakland distribution centre cost around thirty million dollars and contained roughly five miles of conveyor belt, capable of moving ten thousand totes. It was a genuinely impressive piece of engineering. Then, before it had ever proven it could serve one city profitably, Webvan signed a contract with Bechtel worth around a billion dollars to build twenty-six more of them across the United States. It also bought its closest competitor, HomeGrocer, in a stock deal valued at over a billion.

Twenty-six cities. Before profitability in one.

What the running would have taught them — what it did eventually teach them, at a cost of over eight hundred million dollars — was a set of things nobody could have argued their way to. That the thirty-minute delivery window is enormously more expensive than a two-hour window and customers do not value it proportionally. That the automated warehouses ran at under thirty percent capacity because order density in a given zip code was far lower than modelled. That grocery customers substitute, complain about bruised produce, and are not loyal. That the last mile — a truck, a driver, traffic, parking — is where the money dies, and no amount of conveyor belt upstream fixes it.

Webvan filed for bankruptcy in July 2001, eighteen months after its IPO, and laid off two thousand people.

The comparison that finishes the story is Ocado in Britain, which pursued a broadly similar automated-warehouse thesis but ground through years of unglamorous iteration on a small footprint before scaling, and which is now a going concern that licenses its technology worldwide. And Amazon, which entered groceries slowly, painfully, with a lot of failures, and is still working it out.

The difference was never the idea. It was whether the idea got to be a hypothesis for a while.

**Cotitura:** Building the operation is not the fast route to validating the model. It is the most expensive way to run an experiment, and it destroys your ability to change the answer.

## 29. We Didn't Test at Apple

**Unde intră:** "Sometimes a company truly needs expertise from outside. But external talent cannot compensate for total architectural confusion." · Level 0 → Level 2 fără dovadă
**Status:** REALĂ

In November 2011, J.C. Penney hired the most admired retail executive in the world.

Ron Johnson had built the Apple Store. Not managed it — built it. The layout, the Genius Bar, the removal of the checkout counter, the idea that a computer shop could be the highest-revenue-per-square-foot retail space on earth. Before that he had been merchandising chief at Target and had made a discount chain design-literate. His hiring was announced and J.C. Penney's stock jumped seventeen percent in a day.

He arrived at a company that was, in truth, in bad shape: a hundred-and-ten-year-old middle-American department store whose customers were older and shrinking, whose stores were tired, and whose entire commercial model ran on coupons and permanent sales.

Johnson's diagnosis was correct. The coupon model was corrosive. J.C. Penney was marking merchandise up in order to mark it down, running something like six hundred promotional events a year, and had trained its customers to believe that the printed price was a lie. It is a genuinely broken system and he was right about it.

What he did next is the story.

He replaced it — everywhere, at once. Out went sales, coupons, and clearance, and in came "Fair and Square" everyday low pricing. Out went the store layout, and in came a plan to rebuild every store as a collection of branded boutiques. New logo. New advertising. New leadership team, largely recruited from Apple and Target. He was reported to have cut the marketing budget for the customers J.C. Penney actually had, in pursuit of the customers he wanted.

When people inside the company suggested testing the pricing change in a subset of stores first — the standard, boring, hundred-year-old practice of the retail industry — Johnson's answer became famous.

*We didn't test at Apple.*

The sentence is worth sitting with, because it is not stupid. It is true. Apple did not A/B test the Genius Bar. Apple ran on the taste of a small group of people with an extraordinary track record inside a machine built around them, selling products that had no substitute, to customers who were already devoted.

None of those conditions existed at J.C. Penney. The taste was not calibrated to that customer. There was no track record with that customer. The products had a thousand substitutes. And the customer was not devoted — she was a fifty-five-year-old woman in Ohio who liked coupons, and who, it turned out, did not experience "everyday low prices" as honesty. She experienced it as the end of the game she enjoyed playing.

Sales fell twenty-five percent in a year. The company recorded a net loss of nine hundred and eighty-five million dollars. Holiday quarter sales fell twenty-eight percent. Something like four billion dollars of revenue evaporated.

Johnson was fired in April 2013, seventeen months in. His predecessor was brought back and immediately restored the coupons, with a full-page apology to customers that read, in effect: *we heard you, come back.*

The lesson is not that Ron Johnson was a bad executive. He was and is an excellent one. The lesson is that he arrived at a company with no validated architecture, imported a private machine that had worked somewhere structurally different, and installed it at Level 2 without ever running it at Level 1.

**Cotitura:** A senior hire cannot import a working machine. They can only import the memory of one — and a memory installed without evidence is the most expensive kind of hypothesis.

## 30. Forty-Five Minutes

**Unde intră:** "If invoices are collected only when the founder calls, collection is not yet in the machine." · un pas manual într-un proces critic
**Status:** REALĂ

On 1 August 2012, Knight Capital Group was one of the largest market makers in American equities. It handled a substantial fraction of all retail trading volume on the New York Stock Exchange. It had been in business for seventeen years.

At 9:30 in the morning, the market opened. At 10:15, it was over.

In forty-five minutes, Knight Capital lost approximately four hundred and forty million dollars — more than its entire market capitalization. It had executed trades in roughly three hundred and ninety-seven million shares across more than a hundred and forty securities, and accumulated seven billion dollars of positions it did not want and could not afford.

The company was effectively destroyed. It was rescued days later by an emergency financing that wiped out the existing shareholders, and was absorbed the following year.

The cause was not a sophisticated attack, a rogue trader, or a market crash. It was a deployment.

Knight was preparing for a new NYSE programme launching on 1 August. A technician spent the preceding days installing new software onto the eight servers that ran Knight's order routing system, SMARS. He copied the new code onto seven of them.

He missed the eighth.

That, on its own, should have been a survivable mistake. What made it fatal was a second thing, buried in the code for nine years. The new software reused a flag that had once controlled an old function called Power Peg. Power Peg had been retired in 2003 — but its code was never deleted from the servers. On the seven updated machines, the flag meant one thing. On the eighth, still running the old code, the same flag switched on a nine-year-old function that had no position limits and no stop.

At 9:30 the flag went live, and the eighth server began firing orders into the market, buying high and selling low, thousands of times a second, with nothing in it that knew when to stop.

There was a warning. In the pre-market, Knight's systems generated error messages — reportedly ninety-seven of them, emailed to a group of staff. They were not designed to be alerts. Nobody treated them as alerts.

And there was one more thing, which is the reason this story is here. When the losses began, it took Knight's team a very long time to work out what was happening, because the system's behaviour did not match any model anyone had. At one point, someone made a change that made it worse. There was no documented rollback procedure. Nobody could simply press a button and put the previous state back.

The SEC's subsequent order is a catalogue of absences. No written procedures requiring a second technician to verify deployment. No automated verification that all servers matched. No process for removing dead code. No escalation path for pre-market error messages. No independent review of the safeguards that had been disabled.

Seventeen years of business, and the whole thing rested on one person, working from memory, at night, correctly copying a file onto eight machines.

**Cotitura:** Ask, for every critical step in your company: if the person doing it were distracted for ten seconds tonight, what would catch it? If the honest answer is "nothing, but they're very good," that step is not in the machine.

## 31. Maranello

**Unde intră:** "A useful process description answers: What triggers the process? What input is required? What sequence is followed? Who owns each step?" · 90% copie / 10% inovație
**Status:** REALĂ

Two doctors at Great Ormond Street Hospital for Children in London sat down after a long day of paediatric cardiac surgery, exhausted, and turned on the television.

Their problem was one of the most dangerous moments in their hospital. When a child comes out of open-heart surgery, they are moved to intensive care. That handover involves transferring an unconscious, unstable child — with a ventilator, drips, drains, monitoring lines, and a complete clinical history — from one team to another, in a corridor and a room, with equipment being unplugged and replugged in a specific order.

It was, in their own description, chaotic. Noisy. Uncoordinated. Nobody was obviously in charge. Everybody talked at once. Equipment got disconnected in the wrong sequence. Information got dropped. Alan Goldman and Martin Elliott knew that this twenty-minute window was where a disproportionate share of their errors happened, and they had no idea how to fix it, because everyone involved was already excellent and already trying.

On the television was a Formula One race, and what they were watching was a pit stop.

A car comes in at high speed and stops on a mark. Around twenty people converge on it. Every one of them has one job and touches nothing else. Nobody speaks. The whole thing takes a few seconds. Then the car leaves.

Goldman and Elliott looked at that and saw their own problem: a machine arriving in an unstable state, a large team, a very short window, a rigid sequence, catastrophic consequences for error.

So they wrote to Ferrari.

Ferrari said yes. The doctors filmed their handovers and sent the tapes to Maranello. Ferrari's technical director and race crew watched footage of children being wheeled out of operating theatres, and then the hospital team went to Italy and stood in the pit lane.

What Ferrari gave them was not medicine. It was process design, and the elements are almost insultingly simple.

One person is in charge, and it is explicit who. In the pit lane it is the "lollipop man"; at Great Ormond Street it became the anaesthetist, whose sole job during the handover is to run it, not to do a task.

Everyone has one defined role and does not do other people's jobs.

The sequence is fixed and rehearsed, and there is a phase where nobody speaks except the person leading — because in the pit lane, chatter is how you lose a wheel.

There is a checklist, read aloud, and a defined moment when the transfer of responsibility formally happens.

And critically: the handover is broken into a quiet technical phase — equipment, physically transferring lines and machines — followed by a separate information phase, because doing both at once was where the losses were coming from.

Great Ormond Street rebuilt its handover on that model. Published results showed errors falling sharply — technical and information errors both dropping from around thirty percent of handovers to about ten.

**Cotitura:** Ninety percent of what you need has already been solved perfectly by an industry that is not yours. The innovation is in noticing that their problem is structurally your problem.

## 32. The Legs Came Off

**Unde intră:** "Noi ar trebui să facem 90-95% dintre lucruri la același nivel cu industria, și doar 5-10% să fim mai buni, mai ieftini, mai rapizi." · "Aici trebuie să vin cu exemple."
**Status:** REALĂ

In 1956, a young draughtsman at a small Swedish mail-order furniture company was photographing a table for a catalogue and could not get it into his car.

His name was Gillis Lundgren. The table was called the LÖVET. In frustration, he said something like: *let's take the legs off and put them underneath.*

That sentence is the entire competitive advantage of the largest furniture retailer in the history of the world.

Now, hold IKEA up against the rest of the furniture industry in 1956 and count what is actually different.

IKEA sold furniture — same as everyone. It sourced from third-party manufacturers — same as everyone. It printed a catalogue — mail-order furniture catalogues had existed for decades. It targeted young households with limited money — same as many. Its designs were in the Scandinavian modern idiom that was already established. It rented retail space, hired staff, took payment at a till, ran advertising, and negotiated with suppliers, in exactly the way a furniture retailer does.

Ninety percent of IKEA is an ordinary furniture business executed competently.

The ten percent is: the furniture comes apart, and you assemble it.

But watch what that ten percent does to the economics, because this is why the ratio works.

A flat package is dramatically cheaper to ship, because you are shipping wood and not air. Freight cost per unit collapses. A flat package is cheaper to store, so a warehouse holds several times the inventory in the same volume. Damage in transit falls, because there is nothing sticking out to break. And the final assembly labour — a real cost, in a real factory, in Sweden, with Swedish wages — is transferred to the customer for free.

Then it cascades into the store. If the customer is going to carry the box, the store can be a warehouse with a showroom bolted on, sited on cheap land outside the city rather than expensive land in it. If the customer collects from the racking themselves, you need far fewer staff per square metre. If there is no delivery in the base offer, you have no delivery fleet.

One structural change, and the cost of a bookcase falls to a point where competitors cannot follow without redesigning their entire business.

This pattern repeats everywhere once you start looking.

Dyson is a vacuum cleaner. Motor, hose, head, handle, filter, retail distribution — all conventional. The ten percent is that it uses cyclonic separation instead of a bag, which meant it did not lose suction. That is it.

Netflix in 1997 was a mail-order rental business, using an existing postal service, renting existing discs made by existing studios. The ten percent was no due dates and a subscription instead of per-rental pricing.

Southwest is an airline that flies planes between airports. The ten percent is the ten-minute turn and one aircraft type.

In every case, the founders' energy went almost entirely into one structural difference, and everything else was copied from what already worked — because the ninety percent is not where the advantage lives, and every hour you spend reinventing it is an hour not spent on the part that actually differentiates you.

**Cotitura:** Founders exhaust themselves reinventing the ninety percent, because reinventing everything feels like ambition. It is the reliable way to end up with a business that is slightly worse than the industry at everything and better at nothing.
